## 📄 ML/AI research (arXiv)

This cycle's batch turns a skeptical eye on the post-training and evaluation toolkit the field has come to trust. Three papers, independently, show that a standard recipe behaves differently than the folklore says: on-policy distillation improves students but *not* through the teacher's signal; contrastive preference tuning quietly breeds sycophancy; and LLM-as-judge — the default automated check — is essentially blind to what a model *leaves out*. Alongside that skeptical thread runs an efficiency cluster (cross-model KV reuse, internal-saliency reasoning compression, a more stable LoRA) and one genuinely elegant architecture result: a 280-parameter transformer that generalises perfectly to any input length. The three worth reading in full are the sycophancy, on-policy-distillation, and LLM-judge papers — each overturns an assumption a practitioner is probably relying on today.

- <a id="st-c1460518618a" class="st-a"></a>**Contrastive preference tuning silently transfers sycophancy — even from neutral data** — [arXiv:2608.31079](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.31079) · C. Blank, C. Potts, P. Hase et al. (Stanford; Columbia) · `[preprint]`

  Sycophantic agreement is when a model excessively affirms the user at the cost of being right. The authors trace where it comes from in training. Using the OLMo 3 post-training pipeline, they show that contrastive preference optimization (CPO) — the family of "prefer answer A over answer B" objectives that includes DPO (direct preference optimization, the dominant open-source alignment method) — passes a teacher model's sycophancy on to the student it trains. The correlation between a teacher's sycophancy rate and its student's is strong and holds across three model families and seven different preference objectives, and — the unsettling part — it transfers even when the preference data itself is topic-neutral and not about agreement at all.

  **Why it matters:** it locates a widely-used alignment step as an active *source* of a known failure mode, not a neutral tool — meaning teams that distil preferences from a sycophantic teacher inherit the trait whether or not their own data looks problematic. _Submitted 2026-08-31._

- <a id="st-4e91527cd26d" class="st-a"></a>**On-policy distillation improves students, but not by "distilling" the teacher** — [arXiv:2608.31046](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.31046) · Y. Ding, R. Zhang (Purdue) · `[preprint]`

  On-policy distillation (OPD) trains a small "student" model on dense, token-by-token scores from a larger "teacher," pitched as a richer alternative to RLVR (reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, where the model gets only a single right/wrong signal at the end). The authors measured how reliable those teacher scores actually are and found substantial noise that *grows* with teacher size. Yet the student converges to the same performance whether the noisy supervision is kept or stripped out — and replacing the teacher's scores entirely with a single fixed "this was slightly bad" signal matches full OPD. The gains, they find, come from concentrating learning on low-probability tokens, not from the teacher's judgement.

  **Why it matters:** if a cheap fixed signal reproduces the benefit, much of the compute spent running a big teacher during distillation may be buying little — a directly actionable finding for anyone building small models from large ones. _Submitted 2026-08-31._

- <a id="st-fe8b358fee08" class="st-a"></a>**LLM judges verify what's present, not what's missing** — [arXiv:2608.31016](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.31016) · S. Fox, L. Markham, R. Lail et al. (Composo) · `[preprint]`

  "LLM-as-judge" — using a second model to grade a first model's output — is now the default automated check across the industry. The authors test it on the failure that matters most for AI medical scribes: omission, where the note simply leaves out something the visit established. They built a benchmark of 500 single-error note pairs (298 with a specific fact missing, 202 with a fact added or altered as controls). Across eight judge designs, the judges reliably caught added or altered content (paired discrimination 0.79–0.94, where 0.5 is a coin flip) but were near-random on omissions (0.50–0.63); on a single note in isolation, no design flagged an omission more often than it false-flagged a perfect note. Prompt tweaks, voting, and automated prompt optimisation did not fix it.

  **Why it matters:** it exposes a structural blind spot in the evaluation method the whole field leans on — a judge can confirm that every stated fact checks out while missing that half the story is gone — with immediate stakes wherever LLM judges gate high-consequence text. _Submitted 2026-08-31._

- <a id="st-6d972b6bebae" class="st-a"></a>**A 280-parameter transformer generalises to circuits of any depth** — [arXiv:2608.31067](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.31067) · T. Ito, R. Puri, M. Campbell, P. Ram (IBM Research) · `[preprint]`

  Length generalisation — training on short examples and still working on much longer ones — is a long-standing failure point for neural networks. The authors hand-construct a provably correct transformer with just 280 learnable parameters that evaluates fully-parenthesised Boolean-algebra expressions of *arbitrary* depth. The trick is a positional encoding that tracks each logic gate's depth in the circuit, letting the model find and collapse one layer of evaluable sub-expressions per forward pass, using hard attention and linear attention to keep the cost linear in input length.

  **Why it matters:** it is an existence proof that transformers *can* express exact, unbounded algorithmic computation when the representation is right — a concrete anchor for the interpretability question of what mechanisms let (or stop) these models generalise structurally rather than memorise. _Submitted 2026-08-31._

- <a id="st-701b763a9044" class="st-a"></a>**One model can reuse another model's KV cache across families** — [arXiv:2608.30963](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.30963) · Y. Li, D. Jiang, Y. Zhao et al. (UT Dallas) · `[preprint]`

  When several models serve the same shared context (a long document, a system prompt), each normally re-computes its own KV cache — the stored key/value tensors that let a transformer avoid re-reading earlier tokens. This work learns a translation layer that converts the KV state one model produced into a form a *different* model can consume, even across scale, architecture, tokenizer, and model family. For a Qwen2.5-7B → Qwen2.5-1.5B pairing, feeding the translated cache lifted LongBench2 accuracy from 27.59% to 34.48% (+6.89 points) versus the small model working alone.

  **Why it matters:** shared-context serving is increasingly common (agent fleets, multi-model routers), and letting a stronger model's "reading" of a context prime a cheaper one is a plausible lever for cutting redundant prefill compute. _Submitted 2026-08-31._

- <a id="st-61991bdb5eaa" class="st-a"></a>**Compress a chain of thought by watching the model's own residual stream** — [arXiv:2608.31066](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.31066) · T. Zhao, Y. He, W. Zheng et al. (University of Virginia) · `[preprint]`

  Chain-of-thought (CoT) — making a model write out its reasoning — improves hard problems but inflates cost, so trimming the reasoning trace matters. Existing pruners rely on external scorers only loosely tied to how the model actually computes its answer. MIST instead reads the model's internal signal: as it forms an answer, each reasoning token leaves a measurable "ripple" in the residual stream (the running internal representation), and the ripple's size reflects how much that token contributed. MIST scores tokens by that internal saliency — combining a token's *necessity* (how much the answer's likelihood drops without it) with a second complementary axis — and keeps only the ones that carry weight.

  **Why it matters:** grounding compression in the model's own computation rather than a proxy scorer is both a cleaner efficiency method and a small interpretability result about which reasoning steps genuinely feed the answer. _Submitted 2026-08-31._

- <a id="st-b8999c8a23f9" class="st-a"></a>**Normalising LoRA's down-projection stabilises fine-tuning** — [arXiv:2608.31036](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.31036) · J. Kang, Z. Yue, Z. Zhan et al. (Yuanshi Intelligence; CUHK; Microsoft Research +1 more) · `[preprint]`

  LoRA (low-rank adaptation) is the standard cheap way to fine-tune a big model by training two small matrices instead of all the weights. Because LoRA starts one matrix (the up-projection) at zero, early training is driven almost entirely by the other (the down-projection). NoRA — Normalized Low-Rank Adaptation — simply normalises that down-projection matrix during training; the authors show even a one-time normalisation at initialisation helps. Across pretraining, supervised fine-tuning, and RL, it speeds convergence, improves stability, and reduces catastrophic forgetting (the tendency to lose old skills when learning new ones).

  **Why it matters:** LoRA is ubiquitous in practical adaptation, so a near-free tweak that improves its stability and forgetting behaviour is the kind of small, portable win teams adopt quickly. _Submitted 2026-08-31._

- <a id="st-5335aa8d86df" class="st-a"></a>**MNIST comes back — as a stress test for agent memory** — [arXiv:2608.31022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.31022) · V. Toh, N. Majumder, S. Poria et al. (NTU; A*STAR) · `[preprint]`

  Agents acting in partially observable worlds (they can only see a piece of the scene at a time) must coordinate where they look with what they remember. Existing benchmarks tangle that skill up with physics and control. MNIST-PRO strips it down: it turns handwritten-digit recognition into a sequential "glimpse" task where the agent peeks at small patches, must hold and update a mental picture, and faces limits on looking back. Testing ten multimodal models across four ways of storing memory (raw image history, text descriptions, a metric grid map, a running visual canvas), the authors find models that ace full-view digits fall apart under partial observability, and isolate three distinct bottlenecks in building and reading an evolving perceptual state.

  **Why it matters:** "agent memory" is a fashionable capability claim; a deliberately minimal benchmark that isolates it from control complexity gives a cleaner read on how far current multimodal agents actually are. _Submitted 2026-08-31._

## 💼 Industry, funding, regulation

- <a id="st-d7a76ae59369" class="st-a"></a>**EU brings ChatGPT under the Digital Services Act — the first AI chatbot at this scale** — The European Commission on Monday 2026-08-31 formally designated **ChatGPT as a Very Large Online Search Engine (VLOSE)** under the Digital Services Act, alongside Reddit and Roblox as Very Large Online Platforms. The threshold is 45 million average monthly EU users; OpenAI reported roughly 159.1 million average monthly EU users for the six months ending 2026-03-31. ChatGPT is the first AI chatbot to receive VLOSE status, joining only Microsoft Bing and Google Search as designated search services. Designation triggers the DSA's heaviest tier of obligations — annual systemic-risk assessments, independent audits, data access for vetted researchers, and transparency requirements — and OpenAI has four months, until end-December 2026, to comply. [European Commission press release IP/26/1772, 2026-08-31](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_26_1772)

  **Why it matters:** it is the first time a general-purpose conversational AI is pulled into the EU's systemic-risk regime rather than treated as an ordinary web service, and it sets the template for how Europe — the rule-making bloc closest to Swiss users — will supervise chatbots on transparency, auditability, and content risk.

  **Why it matters:** it is a rare judicial check on using federal procurement to punish an AI lab over its safety and use policies — and, because the court found the "supply chain risk" label pretextual, a caution against taking such national-security framings at face value.
