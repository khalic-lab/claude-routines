## 📄 ML/AI research (arXiv)

- <a id="st-b80b06523933" class="st-a"></a>**An agent's memory survives a model upgrade only if it's stored as a rigid schema, not free-form notes** — [arXiv:2609.05339](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05339) · Ankit Goyal, Jaideep Ray (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]`

  When you swap the language model behind an agent but keep its accumulated memory, the agent can still "forget": the new model reads old notes differently, and mismatched embedding versions (the numeric fingerprints used to look memories up) can break retrieval. The authors compare four ways to hold the same history — kept verbatim for long-context reading, chunked for retrieval-augmented generation (RAG — fetching relevant snippets to feed the model), compressed by a model into natural-language notes, or normalised into a fixed-schema knowledge graph (facts stored as rigid subject–relation–object entries) — across 48 synthetic histories with exact scoring on two sub-10-billion-parameter open models.

  **Why it matters:** model upgrades are routine, memory migrations are not, and this is a rare controlled measurement of what actually transfers. The fixed-schema knowledge graph barely moved when the writing model was swapped (accuracy change of +0.0004 ± 0.0020); model-written notes were tightly coupled to the model that wrote them. The practical takeaway for anyone building durable agents: structure your memory rigidly if you expect to change models under it. _Submitted 4 September 2026._

- <a id="st-083d3fb4db3b" class="st-a"></a>**A KV-cache compressor built for the moments a reasoning model doubles back** — [arXiv:2609.04971](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04971) · J. Kim, M. Kim et al. (Hanyang University; Sungkyunkwan University) · `[preprint]`

  Large reasoning models think in long chains of tokens, and the key–value cache (KV cache — the stored attention state that lets a model avoid recomputing earlier tokens) grows linearly with that length until it overflows GPU memory. Existing compressors guess which cached tokens matter from the most recent queries. The authors show this fails on long reasoning: some decoding steps are "thought-revisiting tokens" that jump back to a plan laid out much earlier, and recent queries don't predict them. Their fix, BeaconKV, notices that the queries doing this revisiting cluster into a few similar groups, and uses those clusters as beacons to decide what to keep.

  **Why it matters:** long chain-of-thought is where today's reasoning gains come from, and it is exactly where naive cache-pruning throws away the early plan the model later needs. Targeting compression at the revisiting pattern, rather than at recency, is a concrete way to fit longer reasoning traces into fixed memory. _Submitted 4 September 2026._

- <a id="st-2c8eb6842de2" class="st-a"></a>**Frontier models often recall a molecule's published value rather than predict it** — [arXiv:2609.05381](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05381) · M. Busch, M. Tacke et al. (Hamburg University of Technology; Helmholtz-Zentrum Hereon) · `[preprint]`

  LLMs are increasingly scored on molecular-property benchmarks, but an accuracy number can't tell a model that reasons about chemistry from one that has simply memorised the answer from its training data. The authors audit 22 frontier models on 12 regression benchmarks for verbatim retrieval — the model reproducing a published number outright. It is widespread but benchmark-specific: on five of the twelve datasets, more than half the models showed it; on the rest it appeared only in isolated cases. Turning reasoning up made it worse, flagging retrieval 89% more often than the lowest setting.

  **Why it matters:** it is direct evidence of benchmark contamination in a scientific domain where LLMs are being pitched as discovery tools — a model that looks accurate may be quoting the literature, not modelling the molecule. That reasoning *increases* retrieval is a pointed warning for anyone reading "reasoning improves science" claims. _Submitted 4 September 2026._

- <a id="st-05aff9ff48ad" class="st-a"></a>**An audit of the ecosystem that strips safety guardrails off open models** — [arXiv:2609.05241](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05241) · 10a Labs (10a Labs) · `[preprint]`

  A growing set of actors removes built-in refusals from open-weight models ("uncensored" models) and re-uploads them. The authors profile that ecosystem: between January 2024 and March 2026 they found 3,471 original uncensored models on Hugging Face, each repackaged an average of 2.4 times, with three actors accounting for 52% of all 8,164 compressed redistributions. Once a model is quantized (shrunk to lower-precision numbers for easier deployment) and mirrored across accounts, formats and registries like Ollama, it persists even if the original is taken down. Of 1,643 GitHub apps built on these models, they classified 25% as explicitly malicious.

  **Why it matters:** it quantifies why "just remove the model" doesn't work as a safety lever — redistribution is the persistence layer, and takedown at the source leaves the mirrors untouched. Concrete numbers on how safety-stripped weights spread are useful input to the open-weights governance debate rather than another round of assertion. _Submitted 4 September 2026._

- <a id="st-826dc07b16ff" class="st-a"></a>**A model builds its own teacher out of its training trajectory** — [arXiv:2609.05295](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05295) · Y. Li, S. Yavuz, S. Joty (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]`

  On-policy distillation (OPD — training a model on dense, per-token scores from a stronger teacher) gives richer feedback than RLVR (reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards, where the model gets only one right/wrong signal at the end), but it needs a good teacher, and an external one often mismatches the student's distribution. RISE instead synthesises a teacher from the model's own recent history: it extrapolates the displacement between the current checkpoint and an earlier "trailing anchor" — in weight space or output-probability space — to turn a sparse outcome reward into a dense token-level target, with no external model.

  **Why it matters:** it is a way to get the fine-grained supervision signal of distillation without paying for a bigger teacher, closing an RLVR-versus-distillation gap that has shaped a lot of recent post-training. Whether the self-extrapolation holds up outside the tested settings is the open question, but the loop is cleanly stated. _Submitted 4 September 2026._

- <a id="st-99b9c1738485" class="st-a"></a>**Layer dropout, largely abandoned in modern LLM pre-training, lowers loss when tuned right** — [arXiv:2609.05275](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05275) · M. Elhoushi, A. Pretko et al. (Cerebras Systems; MBZUAI) · `[preprint]`

  Layer dropout (a.k.a. stochastic depth — randomly skipping whole layers during training) once helped transformers train faster and tolerate later pruning, but it quietly disappeared from large-model recipes on reports that it hurt accuracy. The authors run the missing systematic study and argue the opposite when it is tuned: with the right per-layer dropout schedule, timing and optimizer settings, models reach lower loss at the same training FLOPs (a compute-cost measure), and gain robustness to zero-shot layer removal afterward.

  **Why it matters:** if it holds at scale, it is close to a free efficiency win — the same compute buying a better model, plus the option to cheaply drop layers at inference. The claim is a reversal of current practice, so independent replication at frontier scale is the thing to watch. _Submitted 4 September 2026._

- <a id="st-8f5b38860d2f" class="st-a"></a>**A look inside how a multi-stream residual pathway is actually used** — [arXiv:2609.05309](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05309) · P. Zhao, X. Li et al. (Huawei) · `[preprint]`

  Some recent architectures widen a transformer's residual stream — the running "backbone" vector each layer reads from and writes to — from one path into several (hyper-connections, and a constrained variant called mHC), but how trained models exploit that extra capacity was unclear. Studying the four-stream pathway of DeepSeek-V4-Flash, the authors find each attention or feed-forward site effectively uses only about two of the streams, the dominant stream shifts with depth, mixing between streams is modest and mostly early, and by the middle layers each stream is largely carried forward on its own with directionally distinct content.

  **Why it matters:** it is empirical interpretability of a design choice that is being adopted partly on faith — showing the streams stay specialised rather than blurring together tells architects the added width is doing real work, and where. _Submitted 4 September 2026._

- <a id="st-27aa2b9d757e" class="st-a"></a>**Running an LLM on at most one spike per neuron** — [arXiv:2609.05151](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05151) · Z. Zhao, P. Omidi et al. (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]`

  Spiking neural networks (SNNs — brain-inspired nets that compute only when a neuron "fires", saving energy) are a route to cheaper LLMs, and time-to-first-spike coding is the frugal extreme: each neuron fires at most once in a window, so firing rates stay very low. The catch is that standard TTFS can't express key LLM building blocks like layer normalization and matrix multiplication. The authors add a reference-based scheme to encode embeddings, normalization, attention and dropout, build a fully TTFS-based network and train it end-to-end, reporting parity with conventional (non-spiking) models on BERT and GPT-2.

  **Why it matters:** matching ordinary networks while firing at most once per neuron is the kind of result that makes energy-efficient, on-device LLM inference more plausible. The demonstration is on older, smaller models, so the open question is whether it survives the jump to modern scales. _Submitted 4 September 2026._

- <a id="st-e4e52194ccac" class="st-a"></a>**Borrowing a neural-net interpretability tool to read the algorithm inside a Turing machine** — [arXiv:2609.04661](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04661) · B. Snikkers, R. Salazar et al. (Resolution; University of Melbourne) · `[preprint]`

  "Susceptibilities" — a technique that probes how a model's loss reacts to small perturbations to expose hidden structure — were developed for neural networks. The authors show the same idea detects algorithmic structure in Turing machines (the abstract model of computation), proving that symmetries and separate paths in the implemented algorithm leave signatures — permutation symmetries and low-rank blocks — in a "susceptibility matrix", and recovering those features empirically on finite-state automata via principal component analysis and clustering.

  **Why it matters:** it is a small step toward interpretability with mathematical guarantees rather than heuristics — if the local-loss-landscape approach can read structure out of formal computational objects, it strengthens the case that similar probes see something real in neural networks. _Submitted 4 September 2026._

For a reader in Switzerland, this is the nearest thing to a home-continent alternative in the frontier tier.
