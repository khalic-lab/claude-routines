### Four mathematicians share the 2026 Fields Medals {#st-2602a5cb910e}
**[Simons Foundation, 23 July 2026](https://www.simonsfoundation.org/2026/07/23/2026-fields-medals-awarded-to-four-of-worlds-top-mathematicians/)** `[new source]` · also [Nature news](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-02169-1)
At the opening ceremony of the International Congress of Mathematicians in Philadelphia on Thursday 23 July, the International Mathematical Union awarded the 2026 Fields Medals — the discipline's most prestigious prize, given every four years to mathematicians under 40 — to four researchers. **Hong Wang** (New York University; Institut des Hautes Études Scientifiques) was cited for work in geometric measure theory, including her proof (with Joshua Zahl) of the three-dimensional Kakeya conjecture — roughly, that a set able to contain a unit line segment pointing in every direction cannot be "thin," a question open for decades that underpins deep problems in analysis. She is only the third woman to win in the medal's 90-year history. **Yu Deng** (University of Chicago) was honoured for partial-differential-equations work including a rigorous derivation of the Boltzmann equation — the master equation of gas kinetics — from colliding hard spheres, progress on one of David Hilbert's 1900 problems. **John Pardon** (Stony Brook University) was recognised for symplectic geometry and topology, and **Jacob Tsimerman** (University of Toronto) for number theory and the geometry underlying it. The IMU also gave the Abacus Medal (for theoretical computer science) to Shayan Oveis Gharan of the University of Washington, the Chern Medal to Graeme Segal, the Gauss Prize to Yurii Nesterov, and the Leelavati public-outreach prize to Hannah Fry.
*Why it matters:* The Fields citations are a four-yearly snapshot of which mathematical problems the field judges most consequential — this year, questions where pure mathematics meets the foundations of physics and computation.

### Lattice QCD "solved" the muon g–2 puzzle — and opened a new one at the colliders {#st-8d11daddd810}
**[Quanta Magazine, 29 July 2026](https://www.quantamagazine.org/physicists-solve-a-muon-mystery-now-old-results-dont-add-up-20260729/)** · primaries: [CMD-3, arXiv:2302.08834](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.08834) (Phys. Rev. D 109, 112002) and [BMW lattice, arXiv:2002.12347](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.12347) (Nature 593, 51, 2021)
For 25 years the muon — a heavy cousin of the electron — has been physics' most tantalising anomaly: its magnetic "wobble" in a field (measured as *g–2*, the tiny excess over the naïve value of 2) came out larger than the Standard Model seemed to predict, hinting at undiscovered particles. This Quanta feature lays out how that story inverted. The hardest part of the prediction is the strong nuclear force's contribution. Two methods exist: the **data-driven** approach infers it from measured electron–positron collision rates, and **lattice QCD** (quantum chromodynamics simulated on a spacetime grid) computes it from first principles. In 2021 the BMW collaboration's lattice result matched Fermilab's precise measurement almost exactly — and independent lattice groups have since agreed — suggesting the muon behaves *exactly* as known physics requires, with no new particles needed. The puzzle now is why the older data-driven prediction disagrees. The prime suspect: the CMD-3 detector at the VEPP-2000 collider in Novosibirsk, whose 2023 measurement of the pion-pair production cross section (a proxy for the strong-force contribution) came out significantly higher than every previous experiment, clashing with results from other colliders. Physicists are still trying to determine whether this is an experimental artefact or a genuine sign that something is off.
*Why it matters:* A "5-sigma discovery" that quietly dissolved into a disagreement between experiments is a cautionary tale about how a headline anomaly's fate can hinge on an unglamorous cross-section measurement — and the muon verdict is not yet final.

### The first stars, not a black hole, may light up an early-universe gas clump {#st-efcbc1882bf8}
**[ApJ 1006, 27 (2026), DOI:10.3847/1538-4357/ae7bea](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ae7bea)** · J. Jeon, T. B. Jeong, S. Zhang, V. Bromm (UT Austin) · via [AAS Nova, 27 July 2026](https://aasnova.org/2026/07/27/first-generation-stars-provide-better-fit-than-a-black-hole-for-early-universe-gas-clump/) `[new source]`
"Hebe" is a blob of gas seen by JWST just 450 million years after the Big Bang, next to the luminous galaxy GN-z11. It glows with a distinctive helium-II emission line, which needs a source of very energetic ultraviolet light. Two candidates can supply that: a cluster of **Population III stars** (the universe's hypothetical first generation, made of pristine hydrogen and helium with no heavier elements, and never yet confirmed) or an accreting supermassive black hole. Jeon and colleagues modelled both against JWST's several measured line fluxes at once. An accreting black hole could match any single flux with plausible parameters, but struggled to fit all the measurements simultaneously; a Pop III cluster of a few hundred thousand solar masses gave the best joint fit. The authors are careful about scope: this is a modelling argument, not a detection, and they note the two explanations are not mutually exclusive — Hebe could host both.
*Why it matters:* Confirming the first stars is one of the marquee goals of the JWST era; Hebe is a concrete candidate where the "first stars" reading now has the quantitative edge, though it is far from settled.

### Two repurposed drugs cut the risk of long COVID in randomized trials {#st-c97e2df130fe}
**[ACTIV-6 metformin trial, Clinical Infectious Diseases, DOI:10.1093/cid/ciag335](https://doi.org/10.1093/cid/ciag335)** · as synthesised in [Nature news, 29 July 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-02341-7)
Observational data had long hinted that the diabetes drug **metformin** and the antiviral **Paxlovid** (nirmatrelvir–ritonavir) might blunt long COVID, but rigorous randomized, placebo-controlled evidence was missing. That gap is now closing. In the **ACTIV-6** platform trial, 2,983 outpatient adults aged ≥30 with acute SARS-CoV-2 were randomized to a 14-day course of metformin or placebo within 7 days of symptom onset; metformin roughly **halved** the rate of clinician-diagnosed long COVID at six months (a secondary outcome). Separately, Nature reports that a randomized trial of Paxlovid in 144 people cut the three-month risk of long COVID by about **40%** versus placebo, published 16 July in The Lancet Infectious Diseases `[single-source]` — I could not independently open that primary, so those figures rest on Nature's account. Both are repurposed, widely available medicines, and the effects are relative risk reductions in modest-to-large samples, not cures.
*Why it matters:* Long COVID has had almost no proven prevention; two cheap, familiar drugs showing benefit in controlled trials is the first real foothold — but the metformin benefit was a secondary endpoint and the Paxlovid trial is small, so both need confirmation before they become standard advice.
