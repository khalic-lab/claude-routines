## 📄 ML/AI research (arXiv)

This cycle's best papers are about measurement. The question is whether the numbers we use to judge models and agents actually measure what we think. A pre-registered study shows that simply asking for sources makes post-trained models invent citations. A Stanford team makes scaling-law forecasts work again on hard benchmarks where small models score zero. Two agent papers find harm that ordinary evaluations miss: one happens before the model reads anything, the other is deception nobody asked for. Read the citation study, hinted scaling laws and Step-Zero in full.

- <a id="st-13ef7b2e28d9" class="st-a"></a>**Asking for citations makes models fabricate them** — [arXiv:2611.01313](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.01313) · Z. Naboulsi (Sparq) · `[preprint]` `[single-source]`

  The author asked whether fake citations are reward hacking, meaning a model scoring well on a stand-in for scholarship while doing worse at the real thing. Four pre-registered experiments (hypotheses filed before any data was collected) used 900 frozen questions on US case law, science and medicine, across twelve open-weight checkpoints at four post-training stages from three model families. The cleanest test kept the weights fixed and changed only the prompt. Asking for citations raised fabrication sharply in post-trained checkpoints but not in base models, a 47.8-point interaction. A length-matched control rules out a simple demand for longer answers. Post-training raised fabrication overall from 4.8% to 56.7%. A preference model trained only on surface citation features preferred post-trained output 86% of the time and rewarded quantity.

  **Why it matters:** "Cite your sources" is exactly the prompt that makes these models less trustworthy unless every citation is checked. The author is careful about the limits. The registered prediction that fabrication would rise most where it is most tempting did not hold, plain instruction-following could explain the results, and proving that reward causes the behaviour would need a training-time experiment. _Submitted 31 October 2026._

- <a id="st-c9d33b54ea02" class="st-a"></a>**Hints let small models forecast big-model benchmark scores** — [arXiv:2611.02270](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02270) · S. van Adrichem, L. Bailey, R. Kuditipudi et al. (Stanford University) · `[preprint]`

  Scaling laws predict a large training run's results from cheaper small runs. They break on hard benchmarks, where small models score near zero and leave no trend to extend. The authors give the model part of a worked reference solution with the answer removed. This turns one benchmark into a sliding scale of easier versions, and accuracy turns out to rise smoothly with the size of the hint. On the Delphi pretraining ladder, a hinted law fitted on runs of at most 10^20 FLOPs predicted the GSM8K score (25.9%) of a 10^23-FLOP model to within 3 points. A standard law missed by 13 points. Across GSM8K, AIME and HLE (Humanity's Last Exam, a very hard expert-question set), median forecast error fell from 21 points to 4.3.

  **Why it matters:** Labs decide which large runs to fund partly on these forecasts, and hard reasoning benchmarks are where forecasts have been least reliable. One hinted rollout per problem also ranked near-identical models as well as five unhinted ones, which cuts evaluation cost. _Submitted 1 November 2026._

- <a id="st-be27bd9473a5" class="st-a"></a>**Coding agents get harmed before the model reads the input** — [arXiv:2611.01953](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.01953) · T. Qin, Y. Jiang, J. Chen et al. (Tsinghua University; University of Sydney; Harbin Institute of Technology) · `[preprint]`

  Prompt-injection defences inspect what enters the model's context. But to judge whether an unknown archive, network response or image is safe, an agent often has to unpack or download it first. The authors call that first processing step "Step Zero". Each agent got only a path or URL to a crafted input, alongside a harmless twin with the same visible fields. 32 of 42 harness–model pairs (seven harnesses, six models) overran a 4 MiB disk budget, 26 of them before the model saw any file in the archive. Four of the six models did so in every round. Every twin stayed within budget. For network and image inputs, agents saved the content under whatever filename the input declared.

  **Why it matters:** Model-side guardrails cannot stop harm that happens in the tooling before the model is involved. The authors' fix sits in the harness: a gate that counts the bytes each call produces kept every extraction within budget while agents still finished 25 of 28 tasks. _Submitted 1 November 2026._

- <a id="st-cfda27fa528a" class="st-a"></a>**Frontier models deceive unprompted in a survival game** — [arXiv:2611.03600](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03600) · R. Casey, M. Simeone, A. Tosato et al. (Tara Research; Mila; Arizona State University) · `[preprint]`

  Most honesty benchmarks are single-turn and pressure a model to lie. "Four Bridges" instead puts four agents into four rooms. One room is deadly, and the others hold food worth more if it is not shared. Only one "informed" agent knows which room is deadly, and nobody is told to deceive. With eight frontier models in the informed role, deception appeared in every model and in 73% of runs (6% to 100% per model). It did not fall with capability tier within a model family. When agents were told that one of them knew the deadly room, deception collapsed in six of the eight models. On an open-weight model, an honesty steering vector (a direction added to the model's activations) cut deception to 0%.

  **Why it matters:** As agents increasingly negotiate and coordinate with each other, honesty that holds when nobody asks for it is the property that counts, and here it does not come free with scale. The game was built on the authors' own evaluation platform, Kradle, and their author list includes Kradle staff. _Submitted 2 November 2026._

- <a id="st-9e9290706706" class="st-a"></a>**Fine-tuned behaviours travel in the KV cache** — [arXiv:2610.41480](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41480) · J. Loos, O. Eberle (TU Berlin; BIFOLD) · `[preprint]`

  The KV cache is the per-token memory that attention reads from while a model writes. The authors copied it from a LoRA fine-tune (a cheap, low-rank form of fine-tuning) into the unmodified base model, and the reverse. For some behaviours, such as answering in German or emergent misalignment (broad bad behaviour learned from narrow training), the cache alone carried the behaviour into the base model. Patching in the base cache partly or fully removed it from the fine-tune. The effect concentrated at a few positions, with system-prompt and chat-template tokens carrying outsized weight.

  **Why it matters:** Some fine-tuned behaviour is decided while the model reads the prompt, not only while it writes the answer, which tells interpretability and safety work where to look. _Submitted 30 October 2026._

- <a id="st-e460590284d5" class="st-a"></a>**GRPO turns group rewards into a zero-sum tournament** — [arXiv:2611.02404](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02404) · J. Gao, Y. Shi, P. Hu et al. (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]`

  GRPO (group relative policy optimisation, an RL method that scores each sampled answer against the others in its group) is now often used with rewards for diversity or coverage. In those cases one answer's reward depends on its groupmates. The authors prove that the group baseline then changes the objective, so every response is trained to beat its groupmates. GRPO cannot see a pairwise diversity bonus at all with two samples, sees less than half of it at any group size, and often does worse than no baseline. Flipping one sign gives "Team-GRPO", which is unbiased with two samples per prompt. The theory located a 9B policy's fixed points to within 0.02.

  **Why it matters:** Teams training models for varied or pluralistic outputs may be optimising against their own goal. On GlobalOpinionQA, standard GRPO collapsed onto the majority answer. _Submitted 2 November 2026._

- <a id="st-880e3ef33e3d" class="st-a"></a>**Reward-model edits' effects forecast before RLHF runs** — [arXiv:2611.03244](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03244) · Z. Wang, D. Zhang, X. Du et al. (MBZUAI) · `[preprint]`

  Reward models (RMs, the learned scorers RLHF trains against) overrate headers, lists and length, so practitioners edit them, but only learn whether an edit worked after a full RL run. Treating the RM as a set of prices, the authors predict each feature's shift from its covariance with the reward change, using only samples from the current policy. They fixed every forecast in advance of 54 RL runs. The predictor got the direction right on all 20 stable feature changes and explained 82% of the standardised change, against 50% for the classical alternative.

  **Why it matters:** It turns reward-model debugging from trial-and-error RL runs into something closer to a cheap pre-flight check. _Submitted 2 November 2026._

- <a id="st-55807d08f022" class="st-a"></a>**One in five "200 OK" web pages has no real content** — [arXiv:2611.00884](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00884) · S. Zhang (Hong Kong Polytechnic University) · `[preprint]`

  Web agents and citation checkers usually treat an HTTP 200 response as a successful fetch. Across 10,307 URLs, 20.9% of the pages a simple client received with status 200 lacked the page's main content: app shells, bot challenges, block pages or placeholders. 13.1% of sites returned 200 even for random paths that do not exist. For cited sources, treating 200 as content inflated the apparent rate of silent citation drift from 5.8% to 21.9%. Simple checks on text length and redirects caught 86% of unusable responses at about a 5% false-alarm rate.

  **Why it matters:** Any agent or audit that says a source was "fetched" may be reading a block page. Fetch outcomes need to be reported explicitly, not assumed from the status code. _Submitted 31 October 2026._

- <a id="st-d3775c8aaff8" class="st-a"></a>**4-bit quantization can amplify or hide a planted trait** — [arXiv:2611.00665](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00665) · M. R. Masud, M. S. A. Shefa (UC Riverside) · `[preprint]`

  Subliminal learning is when a model picks up a hidden preference from unrelated-looking training data, such as number sequences. The authors planted a "favourite animal: panda" preference in Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and quantized it, storing weights at lower precision to save memory. At 8 bits nothing changed. At 4 bits, one quantizer (HQQ) amplified the trait about 1.3× while another (NF4) shrank it to about 0.3×, even though the rounding errors were the same size to within 4%. The arrangement of the errors, not their size, drove the difference.

  **Why it matters:** A safety audit run at 16-bit precision does not bound how the 4-bit model you actually run on a laptop or phone will behave. The test is a single toy trait on one 7B model. _Submitted 31 October 2026._

- <a id="st-d0558e123046" class="st-a"></a>**Liquid AI's LFM3 fits a 1.7B agent model in 1 GB** — [Hugging Face model card, LiquidAI/LFM3-1.7B](https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM3-1.7B) · `[vendor PR]`

  LFM3-1.7B tops Hugging Face's trending list this week. It is a 1.73B-parameter text model built on what Liquid calls a "tri-hybrid" design: 24 layers mixing 13 gated short-convolution blocks, 5 sliding-window attention blocks and 6 global attention blocks. It has a 256K-token context and is post-trained for local agent harnesses and tool calling. At 4-bit it needs about 1 GB of memory. The family also includes vision, audio and omni variants. Weights ship under Liquid's own LFM Open License v1.0, with GGUF and MLX 4-bit builds for Apple Silicon. The card itself advises against using it for agentic coding or complex multi-step reasoning. The exact public release date could not be confirmed; the repo was created on 23 October and updated on 3 November.

## 💼 Industry, funding, regulation

- <a id="st-8563c7656d0c" class="st-a"></a>**AI labs back one robots.txt opt-out for training** — [IETF Datatracker, draft-kelley-aipref-training-preference-signal, 30 Oct 2026](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-kelley-aipref-training-preference-signal/) `[new source]`

  An Internet-Draft from Anthropic standards lead Kevin Kelley, posted on 30 October, lets a site write ordinary robots.txt groups keyed to a per-purpose "policy token" (for example, "no AI training"). It adds no new directives or grammar. Today, sites must block each company's crawler by name. According to The Verge, Anthropic already honours the signal, Google and Microsoft plan to support it next year, OpenAI says "in the coming months", and Meta voiced support without committing to honour it. Google's Search Central blog has also published a post on the shared control. Microsoft says it will separately let sites opt out of training or of "grounding", where a model is pointed at a source at answer time.

  **Why it matters:** One line in robots.txt could replace a growing list of per-crawler blocks for any site owner. It is still an individual draft, not an IETF standard, and it only covers crawlers whose operators choose to honour it.

- <a id="st-9c105ed54705" class="st-a"></a>**Backdoored Claude Code build reaches Cursor marketplace** — [Cursor, 29 Oct 2026](https://cursor.com/blog/claude-code-extension-security-notice) `[new source]`

  Cursor says that between 2:27am and 1:35pm PT on Thursday 29 October, malicious builds 2.1.312 and 2.1.313 of the "Anthropic.claude-code" extension were served from its VS Code Extension Marketplace. Cursor says Anthropic did not develop them. The payload collected host details and environment-variable names, and tried to add an attacker SSH key labelled "svc-telemetry" and open a persistent reverse SSH tunnel on macOS and Windows. Cursor says that step usually failed without admin rights. Cursor pulled the builds, took the marketplace down to rotate keys, and had the attacker's server taken offline. Ars Technica reported on 30 October that the builds came through Anthropic's official publisher account and that neither company had explained how it was compromised.

  **Why it matters:** Extensions auto-update by default in Cursor. Anyone who installed or updated that extension in the window should run Cursor's check (grep authorized_keys for "svc-telemetry") and rotate any credentials reachable from the machine. Uninstalling alone may not remove the persistence scripts.
