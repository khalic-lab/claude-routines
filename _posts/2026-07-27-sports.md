Slovenia's Tadej Pogačar (UAE Team Emirates-XRG) sealed his **fifth Tour de France** on the Champs-Élysées, drawing level with the sport's four all-time greats — Jacques Anquetil, Eddy Merckx, Bernard Hinault and Miguel Indurain — as the only riders to win cycling's biggest race five times. As flagged here last week, the contest had effectively closed once his one real threat, two-time winner Jonas Vingegaard, abandoned with a broken collarbone on stage 15; his other rival, Belgium's Remco Evenepoel, finished second overall, 6 minutes 26 seconds back. The final stage itself — normally a processional sprint — produced the drama, because organisers again routed it over the cobbled Montmartre climb. Mathieu van der Poel of the Netherlands attacked with Pogačar 11km out and just held off the chasing sprinters to win, collapsing over the line. The stage was cut from 133km to 89km after emergency services were pulled away to fight wildfires in south-west France. One shadow over the race: the International Testing Agency woke Vingegaard at 2am and Pogačar at 5am for out-of-competition anti-doping tests, citing "exceptional circumstances" for the rare night visits; results have not been published.
*Why it matters:* Five wins puts Pogačar in the conversation as the greatest cyclist ever — but the reception is telling. He was booed by some fans during the race, less out of dislike than frustration that his dominance has drained the Tour of suspense. That tension — a generational champion whose brilliance makes the event less competitive — is now the sport's central question.

## 🧠 Why it matters

- **The Pogačar problem is cycling's success problem.** A rider equalling a 40-year-old record should be a celebration; instead the Tour spent three weeks with no genuine general-classification contest, its one plausible challenger (Vingegaard) gone by stage 15. Pogačar himself framed it as "stranger than fiction." The uncomfortable subtext — booing on the roadside, organisers redrawing the Paris finish just to manufacture a stage-winning contest — is that a sport can be dominated so completely that its showpiece loses the very uncertainty that makes it worth watching.
- **F1's reset went to Mercedes, not the old order.** The 2026 rule overhaul was supposed to be a lottery, and it broke against the recent script: Mercedes' second-year driver Antonelli leads by two wins' worth of points, McLaren only won its first race of the year in late July, and the reordered field now includes Hamilton at Ferrari and new entrants filling out the grid. Half a season in, the story is less "who wins in Hungary" than "how permanent is this new hierarchy" — the summer break is the first moment everyone can take stock of it.
