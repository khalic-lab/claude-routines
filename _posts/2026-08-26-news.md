- <a id="st-f1ccf3907dca" class="st-a"></a>**Switzerland's Federal Supreme Court ruled on Wednesday 26 August that climate activists who blockaded a Zurich street can be criminally punished, holding that freedom of expression is no justification for the blockade.** In a series of judgments published Wednesday, the Bundesgericht (the country's highest court) confirmed the suspended fines imposed for coercion (Nötigung) on several Extinction Rebellion activists who, on 4 October 2021, joined roughly 200 protesters occupying central Zurich's Uraniastrasse and sat in the road for about an hour and a half — ignoring repeated police orders to clear it — until police forcibly removed them. The court rejected the activists' argument that their convictions breached their rights to free expression and assembly: it stressed that free expression is a very high value and that authorities must show some tolerance even toward unauthorised gatherings — and noted Zurich police had indeed initially let the protest proceed — but found the activists had "overstepped," because the decisive factors are the scale and duration of a disruption and how severely uninvolved people are affected in their daily lives. Why it matters: this sets a national precedent on the limits of protest in Switzerland — demonstrations may be disruptive and inconvenient, but blocking traffic for extended periods gives activists no legal "free pass," a line that will shape how future climate and other protests are policed and prosecuted. ([SRF, 26 Aug 2026](https://www.srf.ch/news/schweiz/urteil-zu-verkehrsblockade-bundesgericht-geldstrafe-fuer-klima-aktion-in-zuerich-zulaessig))

For very small players the paperwork dwarfs the volumes involved: Patrick Hufschmid, an independent luthier (stringed-instrument maker) based in Aigle (Vaud) who mails guitar plectrums to EU customers in stiff cardboard envelopes, told the paper he now faces close to €260 a year in compliance fees plus separate registrations, declarations and compliance bodies for each country — just to ship under two kilos of cardboard annually to France and Germany. Why it matters: Switzerland is outside the EU but its exporters must still meet the bloc's rules to sell there, so an EU regulation aimed at large-scale waste lands as a disproportionate administrative burden on Swiss artisans and micro-enterprises — a concrete example of how EU single-market rules reach across the border into Swiss working life. ([Le Temps, 26 Aug 2026](https://www.letemps.ch/articles/un-systeme-aberrant-et-disproportionne-la-nouvelle-reglementation-europeenne-sur-les-emballages-etouffe-les-petits-exportateurs))

Why it matters: SBB is a federally owned backbone of Swiss daily life, and the profit jump — set against the much larger sum the company says it actually needs — is a marker of both a recovering post-pandemic demand and the long-term funding gap facing the network reader-taxpayers ultimately underwrite.
