## 🔭 Physics, chemistry, math & quantum

### A quadrupolar "hidden order" drives superconductivity in lanthanum hydride {#st-3dda856dc1c6}
**[arXiv:2608.10428](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.10428)** · A. Raghav, M. Casula et al. (Sorbonne Université; RIKEN; NIMS) · `[preprint]`
Lanthanum hydride (LaH₁₀) is the poster child of hydride superconductors — materials that carry current with zero resistance at a record-high 250 K (about −23 °C), but only when squeezed to ~170 GPa, roughly 1.7 million times atmospheric pressure. Everyone knew that as you compress it, its crystal changes shape (an R‑3m → Fm‑3m structural transition) right where superconductivity switches on, but *why* was unclear. This French–Japanese team ran the numbers — using a machine-learned interatomic potential (a neural network trained on quantum-mechanical energies, standing in for far slower first-principles simulation) — and found the transition is governed by a **quadrupolar order parameter**: the hydrogen atoms collectively adopt a subtle, four-lobed distortion pattern (think of the symmetry of a cloverleaf rather than a simple stretch). That "hidden order" softens a specific set of lattice vibrations (T₂g phonons) that happen to couple strongly to electrons — and strong electron–phonon coupling is exactly what glues electrons into the pairs that superconduct.
*Why it matters:* it hands materials designers a concrete knob — look for compounds with these soft quadrupolar distortions — in the hunt for a superconductor that works at everyday pressures, the field's holy grail.

### A faster way to measure how "mixed" an unknown quantum state is {#st-1234b8247a6e}
**[arXiv:2608.11151](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11151)** · M. Gao, Q. Wang (Chinese Academy of Sciences; Shanghai Jiao Tong University) · `[preprint]`
The von Neumann entropy of a quantum state is its information-theoretic "temperature gauge" — it measures how mixed or uncertain the state is (zero for a pristine, fully-known state; large for a noisy or highly entangled one), and estimating it underlies benchmarking quantum computers and certifying entanglement. The catch: you can't read a quantum state off like a number; you have to prepare many identical copies and measure them, and every known method needed a number of copies growing as the *square* of the state's dimension d (written Ω(d²)) — a wall that plug-in estimators provably cannot beat. This paper gives the first estimator that breaks that quadratic barrier, needing o(d²) copies (asymptotically fewer than d²) for fixed accuracy, via a new "pinching" inequality plus tailored estimators for large and small eigenvalues.
*Why it matters:* copies of a quantum state are expensive to make, so shaving the sample cost of a routine diagnostic makes verifying real quantum hardware measurably cheaper — the first improvement on a bound that had stood as a hard limit.

## 🧬 Biology, medicine & neuroscience

### 38 years of bat-banding data show hibernation sites are a learned tradition {#st-a5d179c8efa8}
**[bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.08.06.743314v1)** · S. P. Ripperger, G. G. Carter et al. (University of Würzburg) · `[preprint]`
Every autumn, temperate-zone bats fly long distances to gather at specific caves and mines to hibernate (the sites are called *hibernacula*). Biologists have long suspected each generation *learns* these locations from older bats rather than finding them by instinct — but hard evidence was missing. Researchers compiled 30,882 observations spanning 1985–2023 of 13,852 banded greater mouse-eared bats (*Myotis myotis*) and found four independent lines of evidence for social transmission: which hibernaculum an individual ends up using tracks the bats it associated with at summer roosts, not simply the nearest site or its birthplace. It is one of the longest-baseline datasets ever brought to bear on the question of animal "culture."
*Why it matters:* if bats inherit migration knowledge socially rather than genetically, losing the older, knowledgeable individuals in a colony — to disease like white-nose syndrome or to disturbance of a single cave — can erase a route the population can't quickly relearn, a real consideration for European conservation.

### Why a handful of people escape a genetically-destined Alzheimer's {#st-ed8a26409491}
**[bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.08.03.742644v1)** · Y. El-Amri, N. D. Villalba-Moreno et al. (University Medical Center Hamburg-Eppendorf) · `[preprint]`
In an extended Colombian family, thousands of people carry a single mutation (PSEN1 E280A) that all but guarantees early-onset Alzheimer's in their 40s — making them a natural experiment for the disease. A few members are mysteriously spared for decades, and the standout case, a man carrying a rare variant nicknamed RELN‑COLBOS, stayed dementia-free ~20 years past the expected onset. This team dissected the *entorhinal cortex* — the memory-circuit region where Alzheimer's pathology typically begins — in protected and unprotected carriers using single-nucleus and spatial transcriptomics (techniques that read which genes are switched on, cell by cell and in place within the tissue) plus whole-genome sequencing. They report that resilience looks **oligogenic** — the combined effect of several rare variants rather than one — and find unusual cell populations in the protected brain: RELN‑positive inhibitory interneurons enriched in the cortex's top layer and abundant ADAMTSL1‑positive excitatory neurons, hinting at a preserved, rewired local circuit.
*Why it matters:* people who resist a disease they are genetically fated to get are the best possible pointer to a treatment; mapping the cells and genes behind their protection is how you turn a lucky biology into a drug target. As a single-family preprint, it is a mechanistic lead, not proof.

## 🌌 Astronomy & cosmology

### A wandering black hole caught eating its own gravitational wake {#st-a922138445aa}
**[arXiv:2608.10719](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.10719)** · X. Li, Y. Shi, F. Bian et al. (Westlake University; European Southern Observatory; Xiamen University) · `[preprint]`
Supermassive black holes are thought to grow from "intermediate-mass" seeds of a few thousand to a few hundred thousand suns — and theory says many such seeds never make it to a galaxy's centre, instead **wandering** through their host after galaxy mergers fail to drag them inward. A nagging problem: a black hole parked in a galaxy's centre has gas funnelled onto it, but a wanderer drifting through thin interstellar gas shouldn't have much to eat. This team reports evidence for a wandering black hole of ~35,000 solar masses that is feeding anyway — by capturing gas from the dense **wake** its own gravity piles up behind it as it plows through the interstellar medium (the same gravitational-focusing effect that lets a moving mass gather a trailing overdensity). Multi-epoch spectroscopy resolves three gas components — a thin inflow ahead of the hole, a dense redshifted wake behind it, and thick absorbers close in that make its light flicker — matching the predicted geometry.
*Why it matters:* it is the first direct observational evidence for a fuel source that had only been predicted on paper, and it means these elusive middleweight black holes can grow while adrift — a channel that helps explain how the giants at galaxy centres got so big.

### The largest 2D map of the sky yet: nearly four billion objects {#st-11e7bb45faf6}
**[NOIRLab release, 10 Aug 2026](https://noirlab.edu/public/news/noirlab2620/)** · NSF NOIRLab
The DESI Legacy Imaging Surveys team released the newest and largest version of its map of the sky — 13 years in the making, covering three-quarters of the entire sky and cataloguing nearly **four billion** distinct objects: stars, galaxies, supernovae, gravitational lenses and more. The imaging comes from two U.S. National Science Foundation telescopes at Kitt Peak (Mayall) and Cerro Tololo in Chile (Blanco), the latter carrying the U.S. Department of Energy's Dark Energy Camera (DECam). The full catalogue is public, and it is the same imaging that selects targets for the DESI spectroscopic survey mapping cosmic expansion.
*Why it matters:* a free, sky-spanning catalogue of this depth is raw material for years of discovery — it is the finding chart the whole community, and increasingly automated surveys, mines for everything from rare lensed quasars to the next transient.

### JWST reads a young "super-Jupiter's" chemistry — and weighs its rocky share {#st-61f49919064a}
**[arXiv:2608.10068](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.10068)** · (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]`
AF Lep b is one of the nearest young giant planets we can study directly — 3–4 Jupiter masses, orbiting ~9 AU (about Saturn's distance) from its star. Using JWST spectroscopy from 2.85–5.3 microns, the team detected carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, methane, carbon monoxide (including its heavier ¹³CO isotope) and water in its atmosphere. Letting carbon, oxygen and **sulfur** abundances vary independently in the atmospheric fit — sulfur is a newer, powerful tracer because it is locked into dust and rock — they find the planet enriched ~3–5× above its star. That sulfur signature implies the planet swallowed roughly **56 Earth masses** of solid material (ice and rock) as it formed, and its carbon-to-oxygen ratio points to formation near where it now sits rather than in the frigid outer disk.
*Why it matters:* sulfur is emerging as a "formation fingerprint," and here it lets astronomers reconstruct how a giant planet was built — a directly-imaged analog to the metal-enrichment story of Jupiter itself.
