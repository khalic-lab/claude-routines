## 🇨🇭 Switzerland & Vaud

- <a id="st-b28e05feed83" class="st-a"></a>**The Council of States (Ständerat, the cantonal chamber of parliament) opened its debate on the Switzerland–EU treaty package on Monday 28 September — the first chamber to deliberate the deal Bern signed in March.** The package — often called Bilaterals III — would stabilise the existing bilateral treaties (which govern market access, free movement of people, and cooperation on research, security and transport) and add three new accords on electricity, food safety and health. Its most contested elements are unchanged from earlier committee stages: "dynamic legal adoption" (Switzerland would automatically take on most future EU rule changes, with an arbitration panel and, on single-market law, the European Court of Justice as backstop), a safeguard clause letting the Federal Council cap immigration if thresholds on unemployment or welfare use are breached, and wage protection. The SVP (Swiss People's Party) and some FDP and Centre members oppose it, warning of lost sovereignty and higher immigration; the Federal Council, SP, FDP, Greens and GLP back it, arguing it preserves the bilateral path that underpins Swiss prosperity. No referendum date is set, and parliament is still fighting over whether approval will require a cantonal majority (Ständemehr) as well as a popular one — a higher bar that would let a minority of cantons block it. Why it matters: this is the start of formal parliamentary consideration of the framework that will define Switzerland's economic and legal relationship with its largest trading partner for a generation, and the double-majority question will shape how hard it is to pass at the ballot box. - <a id="st-48519c7cd00c" class="st-a"></a>**Official final results confirmed on Monday 28 September that Swiss voters heavily rejected both federal initiatives put to them on Sunday 27 September.** The Federal Chancellery's data platform put the "Safeguard Swiss neutrality" initiative at 29.85% Yes (70.2% No) and the "For safe food" initiative at 27.48% Yes (72.5% No); both also failed the cantonal majority, with no canton in favour, on a turnout of 47.1%. The neutrality text, backed among major parties only by the SVP, would have written a "perpetual, armed" neutrality into the constitution and largely barred sanctions adopted outside the UN Security Council. At the government's press conference, Foreign Minister Ignazio Cassis said the No was "not a vote against neutrality" and that Switzerland "was neutral yesterday, is neutral today and will remain so." On the cantonal ballots, Vaud approved its "12%" initiative to cut cantonal income and wealth tax by a flat 12% from 2027 (53.9% Yes), against the government's opposition; Geneva became the first canton to introduce free contraception (58.5% Yes); and Graubünden rejected voting at 16 (67.4% No). Why it matters: the double rejection leaves Switzerland's flexible, unwritten neutrality practice intact, while Vaud's Yes hands households a recurring tax cut and squeezes the canton's budget. ## 🌍 World politics & geopolitics

- <a id="st-73be77e7e7ba" class="st-a"></a>**Serbian President Aleksandar Vučić announced on Sunday 27 September that he would resign in order to run for prime minister in a snap parliamentary election set for October.** Speaking to supporters at the presidential palace in Belgrade, Vučić said "I am resigning as president" and that he would hand his duties to the parliament speaker the next morning; the constitution bars him from a third presidential term, and he would otherwise have served until May 2027. He has dominated Serbian politics for over a decade, and his move follows nearly a year of anti-corruption protests triggered by the November 2024 collapse of a railway-station canopy in Novi Sad that killed 16 people. Why it matters: the snap vote will test whether Vučić — accused by critics of authoritarian drift while balancing EU-membership ambitions against close ties to Russia — can convert the presidency's power into a renewed parliamentary mandate amid the biggest challenge to his rule in years. - <a id="st-349698c01766" class="st-a"></a>**Sweden's centre-left leader Magdalena Andersson abandoned her attempt to form a government on Monday 28 September, leaving the country in political deadlock more than two weeks after an election her bloc narrowly won.** Andersson's Social Democrats, Greens, Centre Party and Left Party together took 176 of parliament's 349 seats in the 13 September vote — three more than the outgoing right-wing alliance, on a popular-vote margin of about 50,000 — but talks foundered on divisions between the centrist Centre Party and the socialist Left Party. The speaker is now expected to ask another leader to try, most likely outgoing centre-right Prime Minister Ulf Kristersson, whose bloc (which relies on the far-right Sweden Democrats) also lacks a majority and would need Centre Party support the party has ruled out on those terms. Why it matters: the standoff means the losing bloc may end up back in power, and protracted formation is not unusual in Sweden — it took 134 days after the 2018 vote.
