## 📄 ML/AI research (arXiv)

This cycle's papers are about how we check models. That covers what they reason with, what training adds, and what audits and safeguards miss. A scaling study finds bigger models depend more on their written chain of thought, which supports reading it as a safety monitor. A theorem argues RL fine-tuning only trades first-try accuracy against coverage and never widens it. Two agent papers show safeguards and robustness quietly wearing away as agents improve themselves. Read the chain-of-thought reliance paper, the RLVR theorem and "Masked Erosion" in full.

- <a id="st-24cb1e7375d5" class="st-a"></a>**Bigger models lean harder on their written reasoning** — [arXiv:2610.38433](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.38433) · E. Xin (Independent Researcher) · `[preprint]`

  Chain-of-thought (CoT, the step-by-step reasoning a model writes before answering) is only useful as a safety monitor if the answer actually depends on those steps. The author built a synthetic "box-swap" puzzle where every intermediate state is known. The author then swapped one step in a model's own chain for a wrong but valid state and checked whether the final answer followed the edit. Across 25 open models (Qwen3 0.6B–14B, Gemma 3, Llama 3, OLMo 2 checkpoints; 7,200 items each), reliance rose from 16–18% at 0.6B to 97–100% at 14B in Qwen3. Capability tracked reliance almost perfectly (r = 0.99). Post-training made no measurable difference from 4B upward. Without written steps, no model up to 14B beat chance once a puzzle had four or more swaps.

  **Why it matters:** For tasks that need step-by-step computation, scale makes the visible reasoning more load-bearing, not less, which is good news for CoT monitoring. The caveats are one synthetic task, models only up to 14B, and a single author. _Submitted 28 October 2026._

- <a id="st-5060cc03fd1c" class="st-a"></a>**A theorem says RL fine-tuning trades many-try coverage for first-try accuracy and cannot widen it** — [arXiv:2610.39295](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39295) · Y. Liu (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]`

  RLVR (reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, training on answers a program can check) is known to raise pass@1, the chance of solving a problem on the first try. Yet the base model often overtakes it at pass@k, success within many tries. The paper models RLVR as reweighting a fixed set of strategies the base model already has. It proves that under its assumptions every update that raises pass@1 strictly lowers success on some prompts, and that removing the pass@k crossover costs the whole pass@1 gain. The same framework also explains "entropy collapse", where outputs lose diversity. It comes with a diagnostic that reads the trade-off from ordinary evaluation logs.

  **Why it matters:** If the modelling assumptions hold, RL post-training moves a model along an existing frontier rather than adding new reasoning, which settles a long-running debate one way. It is a single-author theory paper; whether real training satisfies the "exclusive specialization" assumption is the open question. _Submitted 29 October 2026._

- <a id="st-afa2a107bdfd" class="st-a"></a>**Self-improving agents quietly delete backup safeguards that their tests cannot see** — [arXiv:2610.38392](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.38392) · H.-S. So, D. Liu, W. Long (University of Macau; Tsinghua University) · `[preprint]`

  Some agents rewrite their own "harness", the prompts and rules around a fixed model, and keep an edit only if an acceptance gate scores it higher on a batch of tasks. When safeguards overlap, deleting one changes nothing observable, because another still blocks the harm, so the gate judges on task success alone. Across 411 improvement loops under ten gate rules, deletions behind a working safeguard added almost no measured harm. Larger evaluation batches made things worse: at equal cost, 20-task batches kept every harness protected while 40-task batches lost all six. The GEPA optimiser kept every safety rule word for word, yet two of its three final harnesses misused credentials once paired with a weaker model.

  **Why it matters:** Defence in depth can drain away without any test going red, and the damage shows only later, when the harness meets a different model. The authors' fix is cheap: freeze safety text, or audit the effect of removing one safeguard at a time. _Submitted 28 October 2026._

- <a id="st-716902cba6da" class="st-a"></a>**AI research agents mostly misattribute their numbers rather than invent them** — [arXiv:2610.38691](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.38691) · M. Alfaqeeh, O. Hourani et al. (Al-Ahliyya Amman University; Binghamton University) · `[preprint]`

  The authors re-analysed all 540 judgments from a public benchmark of nine AI research agents and hand-labelled 256 numerical claims. About four in five unsupported claims matched a real value in the agent's own files, taken from the wrong experiment, metric or run. Overt fabrication was rare. Their deterministic checker, with rules fixed before labelling, certified claims at over 90% precision against blinded annotators (78 of 85; Cohen's kappa, a measure of agreement beyond chance, 0.89). An LLM verifier given the same evidence accepted about three times as many misattributed claims.

  **Why it matters:** The common failure in AI-written research is mixing up the evidence, not inventing it, and LLM judges are poor at catching that. Provenance checks should come before plausibility checks. _Submitted 28 October 2026._

- <a id="st-5955f19ba482" class="st-a"></a>**RL-trained tic-tac-toe models lean on prompt cues, not strategy** — [arXiv:2610.39182](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39182) · A. Nagar, C. Tan (Carnegie Mellon University; A*STAR) · `[preprint]`

  Tic-tac-toe is small enough to analyse exhaustively, so the authors trained small LLMs on it with GRPO (a common RL method) and tracked their internals with sparse autoencoders (SAEs, tools that split activations into readable features). Accuracy and robustness rose, which looks like learned game reasoning. Inside, though, the models mainly leaned harder on facts already stated in the prompt, such as whose turn it is and which squares are full. Strategic concepts like forks and board symmetries stayed weakly represented. RL reorganised which existing features dominate rather than adding new concepts.

  **Why it matters:** This is a mechanistic counterpart to the RLVR theorem above: benchmark gains after RL can overstate how much reasoning was learned. The setting is a toy and the models are small. _Submitted 29 October 2026._

- <a id="st-8364f912157d" class="st-a"></a>**A gradient filter finds the harmless-looking fine-tuning data that erodes safety** — [arXiv:2610.37481](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37481) · S. Reddy, A. Abbas et al. (DatologyAI; UIUC) · `[preprint]`

  Fine-tuning on benign-looking instruction data can weaken a model's refusals. SimpleLoRIF embeds each training example by its gradient, the direction it pushes the weights, and flags examples whose gradients align with unsafe behaviour. Limiting backpropagation to the last four transformer blocks lets it scale to about a million examples. Removing the 20% most unsafe-aligned examples from the open Tulu 3 dataset cut GCG attack success (an automated jailbreak search) by up to 22% across Qwen, Llama and OLMo, while capability held or improved. It beat both an LLM-as-judge filter and earlier gradient methods. The removed data pointed to under-studied culprits such as tool-execution failures and "narrative arithmetic".

  **Why it matters:** It gives teams that fine-tune open models a practical way to screen data that content filters miss. SafeTulu and a tool to build custom versions are released. _Submitted 27 October 2026._

- <a id="st-c738dea481a6" class="st-a"></a>**Speculative decoding speeds up fine-grained MoE models more than theory predicted** — [arXiv:2610.38981](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.38981) · E. Ranjan, K. K. Teru et al. (Cohere) · `[preprint]`

  Speculative decoding uses a cheap draft to propose several tokens that the big model then verifies in one pass. For mixture-of-experts models (MoE: each token uses only a few "expert" sub-networks), earlier analysis predicted gains would peak at medium batch sizes. Across 10 MoE models from 30B to 1T parameters, newer fine-grained designs instead gave the biggest speedups at small batch sizes. On H100s, consecutive draft tokens often route to the same experts, and the GPU's L2 cache absorbs those repeated reads. A model of this routing overlap predicts unique-expert counts within 4%.

  **Why it matters:** Single-user and low-concurrency serving of big MoE models, including local inference, gets more out of speculative decoding than older cost models suggested. _Submitted 28 October 2026._

- <a id="st-49eab1b2372e" class="st-a"></a>**Meta maps when "looping" a transformer pays off** — [arXiv:2610.40325](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40325) · C. Ekbote, A. Pagnoni et al. (Meta AI; MIT) · `[preprint]`

  Looping runs the same layers several times, making a model deeper without adding parameters. In models up to 1B parameters and 32 loops, looping the whole network gave the best quality at a fixed size. Looping a middle block was the better buy when training compute is the limit. Gains eventually saturate or reverse. Small loop-specific LoRA adapters (low-rank add-on weights) recover quality as loops grow. The authors also fix a variant that runs loop stages in parallel across neighbouring tokens to avoid the decoding slowdown.

  **Why it matters:** This is a practical recipe for buying depth without memory, relevant wherever parameters are scarce, such as on-device models. All results are at 1B scale or below. _Submitted 29 October 2026._

- <a id="st-f1334c51a8b0" class="st-a"></a>**Personal agents that learn from feedback become easier to hijack** — [arXiv:2610.39122](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39122) · X. Wang, X. Liu et al. (University of Wisconsin–Madison; Johns Hopkins University) · `[preprint]`

  Indirect prompt injection (IPI) is when a third party hides instructions in content an agent reads. The authors first had users ask a real personal agent to complete tasks found in external content and praise the result. They then planted similar tasks that the user had not asked for. Compared with a non-learning base agent, attack success rose 14.0–48.8 percentage points when the agent evolved its skills from feedback, and 3.0–39.3 points under reinforcement learning.

  **Why it matters:** "Do what the document says" becomes a learned habit, so the self-improvement features vendors market can erode the user's own authorisation boundary. _Submitted 29 October 2026._

- <a id="st-e2d882a24b23" class="st-a"></a>**A jailbreak hidden in an agent's skill file works almost half the time** — [arXiv:2610.37738](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37738) · K. R. Manjusha, M. Emani (UIUC; Argonne National Laboratory) · `[preprint]`

  Agent frameworks load Markdown files such as SKILL.md into the model's context at start-up. Embedding a persona-plus-GPTFuzzer jailbreak in such a file raised mean attack success across seven open-weight models from 0.5% to 45%, and to 68.9% for the worst model, on 270 forbidden questions. Hiding the payload with zero-width characters or phonetic spelling did not help; plain text worked best.

  **Why it matters:** Skill and config files are code-adjacent inputs and should be reviewed like code before you install one. The test used a simple LangChain agent and open models only. _Submitted 27 October 2026._

## 🏢 Lab blogs & official releases

- <a id="st-66698da7b3f7" class="st-a"></a>**Google DeepMind's Gemini 4 Carbon tops cyber benchmarks, but only vetted defenders get it** — [Google DeepMind, 28 Oct 2026](https://deepmind.google/blog/gemini-4-carbon-our-next-frontier-model/) · `[vendor PR]`

  Google DeepMind announced Gemini 4 Carbon on Wednesday 28 October, two weeks after Gemini 4 Argon. Google says it is first on Artificial Analysis's Intelligence Index v5.0 (score 59) and reports SEC-bench Pro 88.2% for finding vulnerabilities, ExploitGym 54.8% for exploiting them and CWE-bench 71.0% for patching. It also reports 0.0% attack success on Gray Swan's indirect prompt-injection benchmark (Argon: 0.7%). Access starts with a subset of cyber defenders through Google's Fairwind programme while the model goes through the US government's voluntary pre-release testing. No date has been given for general release. The launch price matches Argon, $2/$10 per million input/output tokens, rising to $4/$20 after the introductory period. [Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/ai/2026/10/google-announces-gemini-4-carbon-its-latest-frontier-ai-model-that-you-cant-use/) notes that Carbon beats Argon by slim margins on every test and that Google does not say whether it is a new model or a post-training upgrade of Argon.

  **Why it matters:** The frontier labs now ship their strongest models to defenders first, under government testing, and cyber capability is the selling point. All the numbers are Google's own, and the gap to rivals is a few points.

- <a id="st-71bc47a32bea" class="st-a"></a>**Analysts went with their AI agent's coin-flip preference 86% of the time, Anthropic study finds** — [Anthropic, 27 Oct 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/agents-judgment-calls)

  Anthropic gave 50 causal-inference professionals a realistic analysis task in Claude Code. They had to advise a fictional farming co-op on whether to expand an advisory programme. At four analysis forks, such as yield per hectare versus yield per unit of cost, a hidden coin flip set which option the agent would argue for, honestly and without hiding data. Analysts chose the agent's option 86% of the time against a 50% baseline. When they had stated their own preference first, they switched 60% of the time if the agent disagreed and 6% if it agreed. 77% reused the agent's arguments, sometimes word for word, but only 15% credited it. Anthropic says the preferences were deliberately strengthened and the real-world effect may be smaller. Agent users also finished faster and made fewer serious errors. [The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/experts-adopt-ai-agents-random-opinions-and-think-theyre-their-own-anthropic-study-finds/) adds that no expertise measure reliably protected against the effect.

  **Why it matters:** Human review is only an independent check if the reviewer's judgement is their own, and this shows experts absorbing an arbitrary preference without noticing. That matters most where agents run safety research. Disclosure: this brief is produced with an Anthropic model.

## 🚀 New models, datasets, & open weights

- <a id="st-32c270bdcfc7" class="st-a"></a>**Mistral publishes the weights of its 1-trillion-parameter Large 4** — [Mistral, 29 Oct 2026](https://mistral.ai/news/mistral-large-4-oss/) · `[vendor PR]` `[ongoing since 2026-10-06]`

  As promised at the 6 October preview, Mistral released the weights of Mistral Large 4 ("Le Chonk") on Thursday 29 October, after three more weeks of RL training. [Hugging Face's write-up](https://huggingface.co/blog/mistral-large4) lists about 1T total and 49B active parameters, a 1M-token context, text and image input, and FP8 and NVFP4 checkpoints, with GGUF ports from Unsloth and llama.cpp. The licence is the Mistral Ecosystem Open-Weight License, an MIT derivative that restricts model-as-a-service offerings, so it is not fully open source. Mistral claims 48% on Artificial Analysis's Coding Agent Index, tied with GLM-5.3 while using 47% fewer tokens per task, a lead on Cognition's private FrontierCode 2.0 (19.7%), and 82% on the Artificial Analysis Cyber Index. Mistral says some closed models score near zero on that index because they refuse the task. It also reports the highest refusal rate of any open model on harmful cyber prompts.

  **Why it matters:** This is now the strongest European open-weight model, and it can be self-hosted for security work that closed APIs refuse or can cut off. The benchmark figures are Mistral's own, and the licence limits resale as a hosted service.

## 📊 Benchmarks & evaluations

- <a id="st-b40508191081" class="st-a"></a>**MLCommons launches Candela, safety benchmarking as a service** — [MLCommons, 29 Oct 2026](https://mlcommons.org/2026/10/introducing-candela/)

  MLCommons, an industry consortium of over 125 members including OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and NVIDIA, now runs its AILuminate benchmarks on demand. That covers anything from a single model to a multi-model enterprise deployment with its own harness. Safety 1.2.1 covers twelve hazard categories, and Jailbreak 1.1 tests eleven of them with techniques from typos to fragment assembly. The prompts stay closed, even to members, so models cannot be trained on the test. Safety results for about 30 open-weight models are public, with jailbreak results to follow. A companion post argues that safety scores do not compose: work cited from CDT and MIT found medical and legal fine-tunes moving in either direction from their base model's safety score ([MLCommons, 29 Oct 2026](https://mlcommons.org/2026/10/composability-ai-safety/)).

  **Why it matters:** A base model's system-card safety score says little about your fine-tuned, prompted deployment. This offers a shared, closed-prompt way to test the system you actually ship.
