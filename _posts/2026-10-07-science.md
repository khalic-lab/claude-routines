### Chemistry Nobel to Kagan and Soai for how molecules come to "choose" one hand {#st-81867e437b1d}
**[NobelPrize.org — Press release, Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2026](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/2026/press-release/)** · Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, Wednesday 7 October 2026 `[new source]` · triangulated with [Nature news, 7 Oct](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03093-0)
Many molecules, amino acids among them, come in two mirror-image forms, like left and right hands. Chemists call this chirality. Life uses only one hand, and is "homochiral" (built from a single mirror form), yet ordinary reactions in a flask produce a 50:50 mix. How one hand could come to dominate on its own was a century-old puzzle. The Royal Swedish Academy awarded the 2026 prize to Henri B. Kagan (Université Paris-Sud, France; born 1930) and Kenso Soai (Tokyo University of Science) "for the discovery of non-linear effects and autocatalysis in asymmetric organic synthesis." In 1986 Kagan showed that a catalyst's handedness need not pass linearly to the product: a slightly lopsided catalyst can give a disproportionately lopsided product, the "non-linear effect." Soai built on this. In 1995 he published an autocatalytic reaction, one whose product catalyses its own formation, so that a small excess of one hand amplifies itself. By 2003, the Academy says, he had a reaction that delivered essentially only one mirror image. "Other than life itself, no one had previously achieved this feat." Both men are emeriti. Per Nature's report, Soai was out shopping when the call came.
*Why it matters:* Drug molecules usually work only in one mirror form and can be inert or harmful in the other, so amplifying a single hand matters industrially. It also gives the first lab demonstration of how life's one-handedness could arise spontaneously from a tiny initial bias. Half the prize goes to a French chemist working in Europe.

### A blinded 22-qubit study points at three nanoscale fabrication details {#st-40aac5c6f848}
**[Fermilab — SQMS Center release](https://news.fnal.gov/2026/10/sqms-center-uncovers-material-origins-of-variations-in-qubit-performance-through-landmark-study/)** · SQMS Center collaboration (Fermilab; Ames National Laboratory; NIST) · published in *Applied Physics Reviews* 13, 021416 · Thursday 1 October 2026 `[single-source]`
Superconducting qubits made on the same chip, to the same design, can still differ sharply in how long they hold a quantum state. That spread is one of the field's stubborn engineering problems. The Fermilab-led Superconducting Quantum Materials and Systems (SQMS) Center examined 22 transmon qubits (a widely used superconducting qubit design that is relatively insensitive to stray charge) built by Fermilab, Rigetti and NIST. Six institutions used seven materials-characterization techniques. The study was blinded: the teams characterizing the devices did not know each qubit's coherence time (T1, how fast an excited state decays) until all the structural data was in. Three features tracked performance. The first was the thickness of the few-nanometre surface oxide, where single-nanometre differences mattered. The second was the angle of the etched sidewalls: simulations predict a steep ~10–15° sidewall improves performance by 20–30% over a tapered ~30° one. The third was the depth of the trench etched into the substrate beside the electrodes, which matters most below ~20 nm. Visible scratches and particulates showed no clear correlation. This summary rests on the lab's own release; the journal article was not reachable from the sandbox.
*Why it matters:* The study turns "qubits vary for unknown reasons" into three concrete process parameters that fabs can control. It is correlational on 22 devices, though, so the next step is to prove causation by deliberately varying them.

### Waves, not just temperature, decide where mangroves can grow on open coasts {#st-3287911c656a}
**[Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11089-z)** · S. Wells, B. Henderson et al. (UNSW Sydney; Tulane University; University of Oldenburg) · published online Wednesday 30 September 2026
Mangroves protect coasts from storms and lock up carbon, so restoration projects want to know where seedlings can actually take hold. Climate and tides are the usual explanations. This team tested a mechanical one instead. Using 20 years of hindcast wave data (reconstructed historical wave conditions, split into three swell components) at 400 coastal sites worldwide, they built a process-based presence/absence model. It looks for "windows of opportunity", calm-enough spells long enough for seedlings to anchor. The model correctly predicted mangrove presence or absence 78.2% of the time. Modest shifts in wave forcing, up or down, flipped sites between suitable and unsuitable.
*Why it matters:* Wave climates are projected to change regionally as the climate warms. This shows such changes alone could move mangroves along coastlines, which affects where restoration money is likely to succeed. A German group (Oldenburg) is among the co-authors.

## 🧬 Biology, medicine & neuroscience

### Medicine Nobel for optogenetics, the light switch for neurons {#st-ec394220b5de}
**[NobelPrize.org — Press release, Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine 2026](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/2026/press-release/)** · Nobel Assembly at Karolinska Institutet, Monday 5 October 2026 `[new source]` · triangulated with [Nature news, 5 Oct](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03091-2)
Neuroscience long had a causality problem. It could record which brain regions lit up during a behaviour, but could rarely switch a specific set of neurons on or off to test what they do. The 2026 prize goes to Peter Hegemann (Humboldt University of Berlin), Georg Nagel (University of Würzburg) and Karl Deisseroth (HHMI and Stanford) "for their discoveries concerning light-gated ion channels and optogenetics." Starting from how the single-celled alga *Chlamydomonas* swims toward light, Hegemann and Nagel found channelrhodopsin in the early 2000s. It is a membrane protein that opens a channel when hit by blue light, letting charged ions flow in and create an electrical signal, and it made any cell they put it in light-sensitive. The Assembly notes both did the prize-winning work at Max Planck institutes (Martinsried and Frankfurt). Deisseroth put the gene into rat neurons and fired them with blue light in 2005. Two years later he did the same in the brains of living mice. That gave researchers a genetically targeted, millisecond-precise on/off switch for chosen cell types, now called optogenetics.
*Why it matters:* Optogenetics turned brain mapping from correlation into experiment and is behind much of today's circuit-level work on memory, fear and addiction. The Assembly also notes clinical attempts to restore sight with it, which are still experimental. Two of the three laureates did the founding work in Germany.

### Sweets during antibiotics predicted worse gut-microbiome damage in transplant patients {#st-122f00b494fe}
**[Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11077-3)** · A. Dai, A. Ballweg et al. (Memorial Sloan Kettering; NYU Langone; City of Hope) · published online Wednesday 30 September 2026
Antibiotics reliably flatten the gut microbiome, and in bone-marrow transplant patients that damage is linked to worse outcomes. Diet is an obvious lever, but its effect has been hard to isolate. The team logged 9,419 meals eaten by 173 patients hospitalized for haematopoietic cell transplantation (bone-marrow/stem-cell transplant). For 158 of them they had repeated stool samples over time and applied Bayesian inference, a statistical method that weighs how well each factor explains the observed changes. Eating sweets and sugars while on antibiotics predicted worse dysbiosis (an unbalanced microbial community): lower α-diversity (fewer species per sample) and a bigger bloom of *Enterococcus*, a gut bacterium that turns harmful when it overgrows. In mice, added sucrose made the antibiotic-driven *Enterococcus* bloom larger and longer-lasting. This is an observational association in one hospital population, backed by a mouse mechanism. The authors themselves say independent cohorts are needed before generalizing.
*Why it matters:* If replicated, cutting simple sugars during an antibiotic course would be a cheap way to limit collateral microbiome damage. For now it is a hypothesis worth testing, not dietary advice.

## 🌌 Astronomy & cosmology

### Physics Nobel to Francis Halzen for IceCube and the birth of neutrino astronomy {#st-0f117c5a2e24}
**[NobelPrize.org — Press release, Nobel Prize in Physics 2026](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2026/press-release/)** · Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, Tuesday 6 October 2026 `[new source]` · triangulated with [UW–Madison News, 6 Oct](https://news.wisc.edu/university-of-wisconsin-madison-professor-francis-halzen-named-2026-nobel-laureate-in-physics/) `[new source]`
Neutrinos are nearly massless particles that pass through whole planets almost untouched. That makes them very hard to catch, but it also means they arrive from distant cosmic accelerators without being bent or absorbed, pointing straight back at their source. Charged cosmic rays cannot do that because magnetic fields scramble their paths. In 1988 Francis Halzen (University of Wisconsin–Madison; Belgian-born, KU Leuven PhD) proposed turning the clear, seismically quiet ice under the South Pole into a detector. On the rare occasions a neutrino strikes an atomic nucleus, it leaves a faint flash that buried light sensors can record. The result, IceCube, instruments a full cubic kilometre of ice and was completed in 2011. In 2013 it reported the first high-energy neutrinos from beyond the solar system. In September 2017 it caught a neutrino that, together with other telescopes, was traced to a supermassive black hole in a distant galaxy. UW–Madison adds evidence for neutrino emission from a nearby active galaxy (2022) and from the Milky Way itself (2023). Halzen gets the full prize "for decisive contributions to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and the discovery of high-energy neutrinos of astrophysical origin."
*Why it matters:* The prize recognizes a new kind of telescope. Neutrinos are the one messenger that leads straight to the extreme engines producing cosmic rays, a question open for a century. It is also a rare Nobel for large, decades-long detector-building leadership rather than a single discovery.

### A white dwarf caught feeding on a "second-generation" planet {#st-eb23978bb234}
**[Nature Astronomy](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41550-026-02983-7)** · J. Williams, B. Gänsicke et al. (University of Warwick; Instituto de Astrofísica de Canarias; University of Wisconsin–Madison) · published online Monday 5 October 2026
When a star like the Sun dies it leaves a white dwarf, and many white dwarfs show the chemical fingerprints of rocky debris they swallowed. That debris has so far looked like Solar System material. The atmosphere of HS 0209+0832 does not. It is rich in elements heavier than iron (zinc, copper, niobium) and poor in the silicon and iron that make up ordinary rock. The excess of "s-process" elements, which giant stars forge late in life and shed into space, is the key clue. The authors argue the material comes from a second-generation planet, one that formed out of gas the star itself expelled during its giant phase, long after the original planets. The helium in the white dwarf's atmosphere and the lack of rock-forming elements point to it pulling in the escaping atmosphere of an evaporating gas giant. A faint brightness signal repeating every 4.399 days fits either a planetary day-night cycle or a comet-like tail. The authors say "candidate", and this rests on one system, so treat it as strong evidence rather than a settled discovery.
*Why it matters:* It suggests planets can be born twice around the same star, which would change how close-in planets around dead stars are interpreted. Warwick, Spain's Canary Islands institute (IAC) and Germany's University of Kiel are among the contributors.

### Gemini resolves the 1181 supernova's "firework" filaments into chains of knots {#st-9a7bf5bdef1a}
**[The Astrophysical Journal](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ae9cb2)** · T. Cunningham, I. Caiazzo et al. (Harvard–Smithsonian CfA; University of Warwick; ISTA) · published online Tuesday 6 October 2026 · release: [NOIRLab, 6 Oct](https://noirlab.edu/public/news/noirlab2624/)
Chinese, Japanese and Arabic astronomers recorded a "guest star" in Cassiopeia from 4–6 August 1181 that stayed visible for 185 days. Its likely leftover is Pa 30, a nebula with a hot central star and strange radial filaments, first spotted in 2013 by an amateur astronomer combing infrared survey data. It is thought to be a rare Type Iax event, a failed or partial white-dwarf explosion that leaves a surviving star behind. Deep narrowband Gemini North images show about ten times more filaments than earlier studies. They also show the filaments are cascades of knots roughly 10^16 cm across (around 700 times the Earth–Sun distance), not smooth streaks. The oxygen glow comes from the same filaments as the sulphur. The nebula's centre matches the central star's position, which caps any sideways "kick" from the explosion at about 100 km/s (3σ).
*Why it matters:* Pa 30 is one of only five naked-eye Milky Way supernovae recorded before the telescope, and the only accessible remnant of its odd type. The knots and the small kick are hard constraints that any model of how these failed explosions work must now reproduce. ISTA, the Institute of Science and Technology Austria, co-leads the analysis.
