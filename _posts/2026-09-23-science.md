## 🔭 Physics, chemistry, math & quantum

### Antiprotons make their first road trip out of CERN's Antimatter Factory {#st-3d1e4b415340}
**[Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11019-z)** · M. Leonhardt, J. Morgner et al. (Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf; CERN; JGU Mainz)
Until now, every precision comparison of matter and antimatter has had to be carried out inside CERN's Antimatter Factory in Geneva, where magnetic-field jitter from the accelerator complex limits how sharply physicists can measure an antiproton's properties. The BASE collaboration built BASE-STEP — a cryogenic, self-contained Penning trap (a device that holds charged particles with electric and magnetic fields in ultrahigh vacuum) engineered to be transportable — and used it to carry 92 trapped antiprotons out of the facility by road, the first time antimatter has ever been moved by truck. The antiprotons survived the journey, showing that the particles can in principle be relocated to quieter "offline" laboratories away from the accelerator hall.
*Why it matters:* Measuring antiprotons in a low-noise lab could sharpen tests of whether matter and antimatter obey exactly the same laws (CPT symmetry) by up to 100-fold — any discrepancy would be a clue to why the universe is made of matter at all.

### A continuous-wave laser drives the thorium-229 nuclear clock transition {#st-d79956d70f6c}
**[Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11011-7)** · I. Morawetz, T. Riebner et al. (TU Wien; BEV; PTB)
Most atomic clocks tick on transitions in an atom's electron cloud; the thorium-229 nucleus is unusual in having an energy jump low enough to be driven by laser light, which would make a "nuclear clock" far less sensitive to stray fields and potentially more stable. Earlier demonstrations excited that transition with pulsed 148-nanometre ultraviolet lasers, but only a tiny fraction of the photons ever landed on the narrow resonance. Here the team excited the nucleus inside a calcium-fluoride crystal using a continuous-wave (steady, narrow-bandwidth) solid-state laser at sub-nanowatt power and — crucially — detected the resonance in absorption rather than by waiting for the nucleus to re-emit light, which removes the slow fluorescence step that had bottlenecked readout and allows much faster clock cycles. They also found one thorium site sitting in an unusually quiet electric environment (a static crystal-field gradient below 0.1 V/Å², versus roughly 100 V/Å² seen before).
*Why it matters:* Faster, cleaner interrogation is a concrete engineering step toward a robust solid-state nuclear clock — a device that could test whether the "constants" of nature actually drift over time.

### Watching a string of force snap inside a quantum simulator {#st-984a7fe62832}
**[Nature Physics](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41567-026-03422-0)** · A. De, A. Lerose et al. (Duke University; University of Maryland; KU Leuven)
The strong nuclear force behaves like an elastic string: pull two quarks apart and the energy stored between them keeps rising until the string "breaks," spawning fresh particle pairs — which is why isolated quarks are never seen. Simulating that process from first principles overwhelms classical computers, so a Duke-led group emulated it on a programmable trapped-ion quantum simulator (individual trapped ions standing in for the quantum field), using focused laser beams to set site-by-site effective magnetic fields that encode a simplified (1+1-dimensional) Z₂ lattice gauge theory — a stripped-down toy model of confinement. With spatial and temporal resolution they watched isolated charges spread freely when the string tension was switched off, and stay confined — the string breaking into new pairs — when it was on.
*Why it matters:* It is a clean, controllable table-top window onto confinement, one of the least computable features of the theory that governs protons and neutrons.

## 🧬 Biology, medicine & neuroscience

### An epigenetic silencer durably shuts off hepatitis B in animals {#st-87ab5fe6f0a7}
**[Nature Biomedical Engineering](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41551-026-01802-8)** · Y. Anglero-Rodriguez, Q. Xiong et al. (nChroma Bio; San Raffaele Telethon Institute; University of Milan)
Chronic hepatitis B is hard to cure because the virus leaves behind both free-floating (episomal) and genome-integrated DNA that today's drugs suppress but rarely silence for good. CRMA-1001 is an epigenetic silencer — a designed protein that does not cut DNA but instead deposits methyl chemical tags to switch off transcription — aimed at both forms of hepatitis B virus (HBV) DNA. In mouse models a single dose cut viral biomarkers more than 1,000-fold (a >3-log₁₀ reduction), and three doses left up to 90% of animals with undetectable surface antigen and HBV DNA six months later; in non-human primates the main effect was a transient rise in liver enzymes at the highest dose, with no detectable off-target silencing across the human genome.
*Why it matters:* This is a preclinical proof of concept — not yet a human therapy — for a "functional cure" that turns the virus off rather than merely holding it down, and the animal data are meant to support taking CRMA-1001 into clinical trials.

## 🌌 Astronomy & cosmology

### Simulations grow the "impossibly" heavy black holes JWST keeps finding {#st-d593d0b89098}
**[Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10985-8)** · S. Chon, S. Hirano et al. (Max Planck Institute for Astrophysics; Kanagawa University; University of Tokyo)
JWST has turned up a puzzling population of "little red dots" — compact red galaxies in the first billion years of cosmic time whose central black holes look far too massive for their host galaxies compared with the nearby universe. Using fully cosmological radiation-hydrodynamics simulations (which follow gas, radiation and gravity together), the authors find that heavy black-hole "seeds" of about a million solar masses form naturally in dense protocluster regions — roughly ten times heavier than standard theory expects. Those seeds develop dense, optically thick disks whose electron scattering broadens their hydrogen emission lines to mimic the little-red-dot signature, then accrete above the usual Eddington limit (the point where radiation pressure normally chokes off infall) to reach about 3×10⁷ solar masses by redshift z≈8.
*Why it matters:* It offers a single self-consistent origin story for objects that had strained existing models, suggesting the early universe forged supermassive-black-hole seeds far more readily than thought.

### Einstein Probe catches a newborn Galactic magnetar — and IXPE measures its glow {#st-5d84c57051c3}
**[arXiv:2609.25990](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25990)** · N. Rea, F. Coti Zelati et al. (ICE-CSIC; IEEC) · `[preprint]`
On 28 June 2026 the Einstein Probe X-ray satellite caught a new magnetar — a neutron star with an extreme magnetic field — flaring in our Galaxy, now catalogued EP J223759.5+531421. Follow-up with Einstein Probe, XMM-Newton, NuSTAR, SVOM and IXPE found 6-second X-ray pulsations and a spin-down rate implying a dipole magnetic field around 2.6×10¹⁴ gauss (hundreds of trillions of times Earth's) and a characteristic age near 34,000 years; over the first month its X-ray luminosity faded by roughly half. A companion study from IXPE [arXiv:2609.25958], the first satellite that measures the polarization (the alignment) of X-rays, adds that the emission is about 7% polarized, consistent with two hot regions (near 0.5 and 1 keV) on the star's surface.
*Why it matters:* Magnetars are the strongest magnets known, and catching one this early in an outburst with both precise timing and polarization lets astronomers test how their crusts and magnetospheres actually behave.

### The first planet caught orbiting an M dwarf backwards {#st-5380df472532}
**[arXiv:2609.24870](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24870)** · Y. Carteret, V. Bourrier et al. (University of Geneva; Université de Montréal) · `[preprint]`
The angle between a star's spin and a planet's orbit is a fossil record of how the planet migrated inward. Using the Geneva-led NIRPS spectrograph (a near-infrared instrument on the European Southern Observatory's 3.6-metre telescope in Chile) together with HARPS, the team measured the Rossiter–McLaughlin effect — the subtle spectral distortion a transiting planet imprints as it blocks part of the rotating stellar disk — for the sub-Neptune GJ 3090 b. They find the planet on a retrograde orbit (circling opposite to its star's spin), the first such misaligned orbit measured around an M dwarf, the small, cool, abundant stars that host most of the galaxy's planets.
*Why it matters:* A backwards orbit points to a violent dynamical past rather than gentle disk migration, and it pushes these spin–orbit measurements into the M-dwarf systems where most rocky planets — and most searches for habitable worlds — are found.

## 🧠 Why it matters

- **Precision by isolation.** Two of this week's physics results chase the same trick from opposite ends: to test the deepest symmetries and constants of nature, you have to wrestle a quantum system away from noise. BASE-STEP does it in space — physically hauling antiprotons out of a magnetically noisy accelerator hall — while the thorium-229 team does it in time, switching to steady-state laser excitation and absorption readout to strip the slow, noisy step out of a future nuclear clock. Both are less about a single measurement than about building the quieter apparatus that the next round of fundamental tests will need.
