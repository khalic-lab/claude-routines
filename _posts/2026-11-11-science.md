### A Bose–Einstein condensate in orbit reaches below 10 picokelvin {#st-3d2be43dab15}
**[Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aea5324)** · W. Xiong, T. Li, S. Jin et al. (Peking University; Shanghai Institute of Optics and Fine Mechanics) · published Thursday 5 November 2026 · `[single-source]`
A Bose–Einstein condensate (BEC) is a cloud of atoms cooled so far that they share one quantum state and behave like a single wave. In space you can let the cloud float and expand for seconds without it falling, and slower expansion means a lower "equivalent temperature". The previous record, 38 picokelvin (trillionths of a degree above absolute zero) with 2 s of free expansion, used magnetic traps on an atom chip. This team made the condensate aboard the China Space Station in an optical dipole trap, which holds atoms with focused laser light rather than magnetic fields. That design has better optical access and can also trap non-magnetic atoms, but no one had used it to make a quantum gas in microgravity before. Their cloud of 2.0 × 10⁴ atoms reached 9.7 pK (+3.2/−2.7) and expanded freely for 7.0 s. The result rests on the paper's own measurement. Science's news section calls it the coldest temperature yet produced, but no independent group has checked it.
*Why it matters:* longer, colder free fall is what atom interferometers need for tests of gravity and the equivalence principle in space. It also shows that laser-based traps, which suit more atomic species, now work in orbit.

### The cuprate pseudogap is where electrons start to pair {#st-a84d50434d49}
**[Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adt3444)** · J. Niu, M. Ortego Larrazabal, T. Gozlinski et al. (Leiden University; LMU Munich; Utrecht University) · published Thursday 5 November 2026 · `[single-source]`
In copper-oxide ("cuprate") high-temperature superconductors, a partial energy gap called the pseudogap opens well above the temperature at which the material superconducts. For three decades one camp has said it shows electrons already pairing up without yet moving in step. The other camp says it is a competing order, such as charge-density waves (periodic ripples in electron density). The Leiden-led team used local shot-noise spectroscopy on Bi₂Sr₂CaCu₂O₈₊δ. Shot noise is the graininess of an electric current, and it doubles when charge moves in pairs (2e) rather than as single electrons. They find that the pseudogap energy coincides with the onset of pairing, that it varies from place to place, and that it reaches up to 70 meV. The authors say this rules out an origin purely in local order. In their reading, cuprates are limited by phase coherence (getting the pairs to lock in step), not by forming pairs at all.
*Why it matters:* if pairs already exist at the pseudogap scale, the way to raise the superconducting temperature is to make those pairs coherent, not to make new ones. That narrows a debate that has shaped materials research for 30 years. The work comes from Dutch and German labs.

### Iron melts at a lower temperature at core pressures than thought, which points to a cooler core {#st-2aad5b27a90d}
**[Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aeh5267)** · Z. M. Geballe, J. Yang, V. V. Dobrosavljevic et al. (Carnegie Science) · published online Monday 9 November 2026 · `[single-source]`
The temperature of Earth's core is set by where iron melts at enormous pressure, and estimates have disagreed by more than 1,000 K. Carnegie's team squeezed iron in diamond anvil cells and heated it with microsecond current pulses (pulsed Joule heating), spotting melting as a brief temperature plateau. The plateau marks the latent heat being absorbed. More than 300 reproducible runs give a surprisingly shallow melting curve, rising about 6 K per gigapascal and reaching 4,160 ± 80 K at 200 GPa. The team also measured liquid iron's electrical resistivity, 76 μΩ·cm. Extrapolating to core pressures and alloy composition, they infer a relatively cold core–mantle boundary of about 3,430 K and a low heat flow from the outer core of about 9.8 TW. The extrapolation is the uncertain step, and these numbers are the authors' model inference, not direct measurements of the core.
*Why it matters:* a cooler core with lower resistivity means ordinary thermal convection could have powered Earth's magnetic field before the solid inner core formed. That bears on the long-running "new core paradox" about how the early magnetic field was sustained. It also implies that the lowest mantle is fully solid today.

### Ice-age ocean circulation took more than a century to recover in sudden warmings {#st-35c23457dd0b}
**[Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aed4270)** · X. Dong, X. Zhang, Y. Sun et al. (Xi'an Jiaotong University; British Antarctic Survey; Alfred Wegener Institute) · published Thursday 5 November 2026 · `[single-source]`
During the last ice age, Greenland repeatedly warmed by many degrees in "Dansgaard–Oeschger" (DO) events. These are usually read as abrupt restarts of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC), the current system that carries heat north toward Europe. Greenland ice cores suggest each warming took only decades, or about ten years. This team used precisely dated cave formations (speleothems) from the Asian monsoon region, whose oxygen isotopes track the same events. Those records show the warmings unfolding over more than a century. Isotope modelling reconciles the two archives. Greenland mostly records a fast change in atmospheric circulation, triggered when sea ice first retreated in the northeastern North Atlantic. The ocean overturning and the hemisphere's heat balance adjusted much more slowly.
*Why it matters:* "abrupt" ice-core transitions are often used as benchmarks for how fast the AMOC can switch. This paper says those benchmarks measure the atmosphere's response, not the ocean's. Europe's climate depends on the AMOC, so the distinction matters, although the paper is about natural glacial events, not today's warming.

### A 3.95-billion-year-old rock from China hints at very early subduction {#st-20bff0c3e8ed}
**[Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11280-2)** · H. Xie, C. Dong, P. Li et al. (Chinese Academy of Geological Sciences; Jilin University; Nanjing University) · published Wednesday 11 November 2026
Rocks older than 3.9 billion years are so rare that the only accepted examples come from the Acasta Gneiss in Canada. This team reports a 3.95-billion-year-old trondhjemitic gneiss, a light-coloured granite-like rock, from eastern Hebei in the North China Craton. Zircon crystals in it have elevated oxygen-18 (δ¹⁸O about +6.8‰) and positive hafnium-isotope values. Together these suggest the magma came from basalt that had been altered by water near the surface, itself derived from a mantle already depleted during the Hadean, Earth's first eon. Thermodynamic modelling puts its formation at more than 1.6 GPa and 740–820 °C, by low-degree melting of basaltic crust. That requires surface rock to have been buried quickly to mantle depths along a cool path. The authors propose a "transient subduction event", one plate sinking beneath another, rather than continuous plate tectonics. [Nature's news coverage](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03499-w) treats it as a second window on the period alongside Acasta.
*Why it matters:* when plate tectonics began is one of geology's biggest open questions, and a second >3.9-billion-year-old rock body with a high-pressure signature gives that debate a new data point. One sample site is a narrow base, and "transient subduction" is the authors' interpretation.

### Bern-led trial: stopping statins after 70 meets its safety test, but events rose {#st-cfffbd5e13ab}
**[NEJM](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2606634)** · N. Rodondi, M. L. Haller, J. B. Bardoczi et al. (University of Bern; Inselspital Bern) · published Saturday 7 November 2026 at the AHA Scientific Sessions; results via **[HCPLive](https://www.hcplive.com/view/stream-statin-discontinuation-noninferior-in-older-adults-without-cvd)** `[new source]`; trial design via **[BIHAM, University of Bern](https://www.biham.unibe.ch/research/research_projects/stream/index_eng.html)** `[new source]`
Statins clearly prevent heart attacks in middle-aged people, but whether people over 70 who never had cardiovascular disease should keep taking them has never been tested in a randomised trial. STREAM, sponsored by Inselspital and run mostly through Swiss hospitals and family doctors, randomised 1,880 people aged 70 or older to stop or continue their statin. Sites were in Switzerland, the Netherlands and France, and every participant had at least two chronic conditions. Mean age was 77.5. The main test was non-inferiority: was stopping not meaningfully worse over two years? It passed. Event-free time over two years was 96.5 weeks after stopping versus 98.5 weeks with continuing. The difference of −2.0 weeks fell inside the prespecified −5.2-week margin. But over a median 103 weeks, the composite of non-fatal heart attack, non-fatal ischaemic stroke or death occurred in 12.7% of those who stopped versus 9.2% of those who continued (hazard ratio 1.38, 95% CI 1.05–1.81). The investigators estimate one extra major event or death per 40 people who stop over two years. In June 2026 the safety board recommended that people in the stop arm be told to discuss restarting with their doctor. So the trial met its pre-set test while also showing measurable harm, and commentators disagree about which reading should guide practice. The NEJM full text was not reachable here, so the figures come from HCPLive's report of the paper.
*Why it matters:* this is the first real evidence on a decision that millions of older people face, and it comes from Swiss primary care. The authors' own conclusion is "cautious" discontinuation only after shared decision-making, not a general licence to stop.

### Scythian hair holds the oldest direct evidence of getting high on cannabis {#st-00a6e09ec4f9}
**[Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aeg3800)** · G. Caspari, T. Sadykov, D. Fomicheva et al. (University of Bern; Max Planck Institute of Geoanthropology; Russian Academy of Sciences) · published Thursday 5 November 2026; **[University of Bern release](https://www.unibe.ch/aktuell/medien/media_relations/medienmitteilungen/2026/medienmitteilungen_2026/cannabis_wurde_offenbar_schon_vor_2_800_jahren_als_rauschmittel_genutzt/index_ger.html)** (German)
A team led by Bern archaeologist Gino Caspari excavated a monumental Scythian tomb in Tuva, southern Siberia. The Scythians were a horse-riding culture of the Eurasian steppe. The tomb held about 1,000 cannabis seeds, and radiocarbon dating of six of them places the burial in the early 8th century BC. Seeds alone do not prove anyone got high, since hemp was also grown for oil and fibre. The decisive evidence came from preserved human hair. High-resolution mass spectrometry found clear signals matching cannabinol (CBN), a breakdown product of THC, the plant's main psychoactive compound, plus weak signals consistent with THC itself. The Bern release is careful about the limits. The hair cannot say how much was consumed, or whether it was smoked or eaten. The seeds come from wild or feral plants, so they do not show cultivation.
*Why it matters:* it pushes direct, in-the-body evidence of psychoactive cannabis use back to at least 2,800 years ago. It also combines plant remains with chemistry from a human body, a method other sites can copy.

### A gene therapy with a drug-controlled "dimmer" nearly stopped seizures in three patients {#st-90a3e9c6d727}
**[NEJM](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMc2606815)** · Y. Dai, Q. Liu, Y. Wang et al. (Xuanwu Hospital, Capital Medical University) · published Wednesday 4 November 2026; details via **[Nature news](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03566-2)**
The three adults had temporal-lobe epilepsy caused by scarring of one hippocampus (hippocampal sclerosis). They had been diagnosed 10–25 years earlier and had become resistant to at least two drugs. Surgeons infused an adeno-associated virus (AAV9, a harmless virus used as a gene courier) into the patch of brain where each person's seizures start. The virus carried a gene that makes excitatory neurons produce a designer receptor that responds to clozapine. This approach is called chemogenetics. Two weeks after surgery, patients started low-dose clozapine every eight hours, which switches the receptor on and quiets the targeted neurons. Unlike conventional gene therapy, the dose can be adjusted, or stopped. About two months later, monthly seizures had fallen from 4.5 to 0.5 in two patients and from 4 to 0 in the third, with no serious side effects. This is a three-patient report, published as a correspondence letter rather than a full trial. Clozapine at high doses carries cardiovascular and other risks, and long-term use is unstudied. An outside neurologist quoted by Nature raised both points.
*Why it matters:* it is a first human proof that a brain circuit can be gene-modified and then tuned with a pill. If it holds up in larger trials, it could become an option for patients who would otherwise need part of their brain surgically removed.

### Pelacarsen cut Lp(a) by 78% and heart events not at all: the full Lp(a)HORIZON data {#st-abaa0e81157d}
**[NEJM](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2610159)** · L. Cho, S. J. Nicholls, S. Frndak et al. (Cleveland Clinic; Monash University) · published Saturday 7 November 2026; results via **[HCPLive](https://www.hcplive.com/view/lp-a-horizon-shows-pelacarsen-lowers-lp-a-78-but-not-cardiovascular-events)**
Lipoprotein(a), or Lp(a), is a genetically set cholesterol-carrying particle. Genetic studies strongly link high levels to heart disease, and there is no approved drug for it. Pelacarsen, an antisense oligonucleotide (a short synthetic strand that silences the gene's messenger RNA), is developed by Novartis under licence from Ionis. The trial randomised 8,350 patients with prior heart attack, stroke or peripheral artery disease and Lp(a) of at least 70 mg/dL to monthly injections or placebo. The topline miss was announced in September. The full data show how flat the result was. Lp(a) fell 78.4% at 12 months, yet over a median 4.7 years the primary composite (cardiovascular death, heart attack, stroke or urgent revascularisation) occurred in 12.3% versus 12.1% (HR 1.02, 95% CI 0.90–1.15). The result was the same in patients with the highest Lp(a). Flu-like and injection-site reactions were far more common on the drug, 20.6% versus 7.5% stopped treatment because of side effects, and 19 patients on pelacarsen had adjudicated anaphylaxis versus none on placebo.
*Why it matters:* it is a sharp reminder that lowering a risk marker does not guarantee fewer events. It is a major setback for Basel-based Novartis's cardiovascular pipeline, and the question now passes to other Lp(a) drugs still in outcome trials.

### The earliest close pair of quasars, 900 million years after the Big Bang {#st-34a027e72a2d}
**[ApJ Letters](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/aeb11f)** · S. Onorato, E. P. Farina, R. Decarli et al. (NSF NOIRLab; INAF; Heidelberg University) · published Tuesday 10 November 2026; **[NOIRLab release](https://noirlab.edu/public/news/noirlab2627/)**
A quasar is a supermassive black hole feeding so fast that it outshines its whole galaxy. When galaxies merge, their quasars should sometimes appear in close pairs, but such pairs are short-lived, faint and hard to separate at great distances. Using Gemini North and JWST, the team found J1602+4228AB, nicknamed Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua by a Hawaiian-language naming programme. It is a pair of quasars about 18,100 light-years apart, roughly a fifth of the Milky Way's diameter, seen when the Universe was about 900 million years old. That makes it the most distant and most compact quasar pair known from "cosmic dawn", and only the third pair found from that era. The black holes weigh about 1.5 billion and 400 million solar masses. Lead author Silvia Onorato says there is no definitive theoretical explanation for how even one such giant existed so early, "much less two". It is a single system, so it shows such objects exist but does not say how common they are.
*Why it matters:* it adds to the JWST-era evidence that black holes grew faster in the young Universe than standard models allow, and it catches two of them on course for a merger. European groups at INAF and Heidelberg are core members of the survey.

### Rocky exoplanets look like they lost volatiles the way Earth did {#st-bc525d768983}
**[arXiv:2611.12581](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12581)** · Y. Reinarz, L. Acuña-Aguirre, H. S. Wang (Max Planck Institute for Astronomy; Heidelberg University; University of Copenhagen) · posted Monday 9 November 2026, accepted for publication · `[preprint]`
Earlier studies found that rocky exoplanets carry more iron relative to silicon than their host stars. That was usually explained by planets somehow collecting extra iron. Earth shows a different pattern called devolatilization. Compared with the Sun, it is depleted in volatile elements such as oxygen and sulfur but keeps refractory ones such as iron and magnesium, which survive heat. The team revisited 14 rocky planets of 0.5–10 Earth masses whose stars have measured iron, silicon, magnesium, oxygen and sulfur abundances. They inferred each planet's interior from its mass and radius using two independent models. Eight of the 14 follow an Earth-like devolatilization trend, and only one matches its star one-to-one. The two models agree. The authors name the limits: a sample of 14, few planets with good stellar chemistry, and little range in orbital distance. They expect ESA's PLATO mission to extend the sample.
*Why it matters:* it suggests that the processes that shaped Earth's composition may be common, which makes "Earth-like" a more natural outcome of planet formation than a fluke.

## 🧠 Why it matters

- **The marker is not the outcome.** Two of this week's cardiology results make the same point from different directions. Pelacarsen removed 78% of a genetically validated risk particle and prevented no heart attacks. In STREAM, stopping a drug passed a pre-set non-inferiority test while event rates still rose by a hazard ratio of 1.38. Both say to judge treatments by what happens to patients, not by what happens to a number on a lab report.
- **"Abrupt" depends on the recorder.** The Dansgaard–Oeschger study and the pseudogap paper both resolve old debates by changing the measuring instrument. Cave records separate the atmosphere's fast response from the ocean's slow one. Shot noise counts the charge of the current carriers directly instead of inferring pairing from an energy gap. In both cases the long-standing "fact" was partly a feature of the instrument.
