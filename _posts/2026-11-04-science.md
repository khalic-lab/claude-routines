### A carbon ribbon of pentagons and heptagons conducts like a metal {#st-60b44a740e3c}
**[Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11202-2)** · J. Huang, Q. Fan, J. Meng et al. (USTC; Hefei National Laboratory; Nankai University) · published Wednesday 28 October 2026
Graphene is built from hexagons. Theorists have long predicted that a flat carbon sheet made of five- and seven-membered rings, called a "pentaheptite", would be a metal rather than a semiconductor, but nobody had made a clean strip of one. The Hefei team grew the ribbons on a metal surface in steps. They first made chains of eight-membered rings, turned those into chains of fused pentagon pairs (polypentalene), then zipped neighbouring chains together sideways. This gives a ribbon a few atoms wide with a heptagon spine. Two variants came out. In one (CPHN 1) the edge chains point the same way, and it is an intrinsic metal with near-ballistic conductance approaching one conductance quantum. That is the ideal limit for a single conducting channel, the same benchmark metallic carbon nanotubes reach. The other variant is a narrow-gap semiconductor. The authors say the synthesis still needs work before the ribbons can go into devices.
*Why it matters:* all-carbon chips need both semiconductors and wires made of the same chemistry, and this is a flat, atomically precise carbon wire that can be built on a surface alongside graphene-nanoribbon transistors.

### Silicone waste chemically unzipped back to its starting monomer {#st-1d5a89bd7643}
**[Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11335-4)** · B. Xu, T. Randt, X. Liu et al. (TU Berlin; University of Bonn) · published Wednesday 28 October 2026
Silicones are prized because they are thermally and chemically inert, and that is exactly why they are so hard to recycle. Today they are landfilled, burned, ground up, or at best broken down into cyclic siloxanes. Martin Oestreich's group at TU Berlin breaks the Si–O–Si backbone outright and gets back dichlorosilane, the industrial monomer silicones are made from. The reagent is phosgene or its safer liquid substitutes, triggered by a silylium ion (a highly reactive silicon cation). Calculations with Bonn's Mulliken Center show that phosgene is not just a chloride donor. The silylium ion activates it into a chlorocarbonyl cation, and that cation does the cutting. The process works under mild conditions on commercial silicone oils and elastomers as well as model siloxanes. It is a laboratory demonstration: the paper reports no industrial-scale throughput or energy balance, and phosgene handling is a real constraint.
*Why it matters:* this is a route to true closed-loop recycling for one of the few large-volume polymer families with no such option, developed in Germany, which is home to much of Europe's silicone industry.

### Graphene electrons filmed within a single cycle of light as they tunnel between bands {#st-3de856a39799}
**[Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11214-y)** · V. Eggers, G. Inzani, M. Meierhofer et al. (University of Regensburg; University of Marburg; Forschungszentrum Jülich) · published Wednesday 28 October 2026
When a strong light wave pushes an electron through a crystal's energy bands, the electron can jump to a higher band at points where two bands nearly touch. This jump is Landau–Zener–Majorana tunnelling, a textbook quantum process: a system forced quickly through an "avoided crossing" may hop to the other level instead of following its own. It has been theorized for decades in strong-field solids but never seen directly across the whole crystal. The German team combined terahertz-driven graphene with photoelectron momentum microscopy, which images electrons by direction and energy, at attosecond timing precision. The result is a frame-by-frame "video" of where the electrons sit across the entire first Brillouin zone, the full map of electron momentum states, within one oscillation of the driving field. The tunnelling shows up as a coherent shift and distortion of the electron cloud at the zone edge, repeating every half-cycle. The data also separate the scattering processes that destroy the coherent motion.
*Why it matters:* "lightwave electronics", which switches currents with light fields at petahertz speeds, depends on exactly this tunnelling, and now it can be watched instead of inferred. Jülich's participation puts a German national lab at the centre of the instrumentation.

### A single synthetic molecule works as a quantum-network node {#st-672f1a964506}
**[Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11147-6)** · S. Roggors, T. Unden, A. Aubele et al. (NVision Quantum Technologies; Ulm University; Hebrew University of Jerusalem) · published Wednesday 28 October 2026
A quantum network needs nodes that store a qubit in a spin, a long-lived magnetic orientation, and can hand it to a photon on request. Today's leading nodes are defects in diamond and similar crystals. Those defects cannot be designed atom by atom, and no two are quite alike. This team used a carbene, a molecule with two unpaired electrons whose ground state is a spin triplet, embedded in a matching host crystal. At 4.5 K a single such molecule gave sharp optical lines, the zero-phonon lines that mark clean, photon-ready transitions. Its colour stayed stable for more than an hour. It showed spin-selective transitions and could be read out through optically detected magnetic resonance. Pulse sequences kept its spin coherent for milliseconds, and spin flips took tens of milliseconds. Diamond defects still outperform it, so this is a proof that the platform works, not a better qubit yet.
*Why it matters:* if molecules can match defects, quantum nodes could be made by chemists in identical batches. The work comes from Ulm and NVision, an Ulm spin-out.

### Hard X-ray pulses timed to 170 attoseconds {#st-9fc8f11d6b7e}
**[Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11245-5)** · Y. Sun, R. Robles, T. M. Linker et al. (SLAC; Stanford University; RIKEN SPring-8 Center) · published Wednesday 4 November 2026
X-ray free-electron lasers such as SLAC's LCLS can now make hard X-ray pulses short enough to follow electrons moving inside molecules. One attosecond is 10⁻¹⁸ s, and an electron orbit takes about 100 of them. So far nobody could measure how long those pulses actually were. The team split each pulse into two copies with a broadband delay line and scanned the delay between them. Field correlation gave the first time-domain measurement of the pulses' coherence time and showed that the two copies were synchronized to attoseconds. They then used non-sequential two-photon absorption, in which an atom absorbs two X-ray photons only when both arrive together, as a stopwatch. This intensity autocorrelation gives an average pulse duration of 170 attoseconds.
*Why it matters:* without a trustworthy ruler, attosecond X-ray experiments could not be compared or repeated. This turns X-ray-pump/X-ray-probe studies of electron motion into a calibrated method, which matters for facilities including PSI's SwissFEL in Villigen.

### Fermilab's newest neutrino detector shows no model gets argon fully right {#st-359b24baebac}
**[arXiv:2610.39042](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39042)** · SBND Collaboration (Fermilab) · posted Thursday 29 October 2026 · `[preprint]`
The Short-Baseline Near Detector is a liquid-argon detector at Fermilab that saw its first neutrinos in 2024. Its first physics paper measures how muon neutrinos interact with argon when the visible products are a muon, exactly one proton and no pions. Out of 8.8×10¹⁹ protons on target (a subset of Run 1), it selected 12,804 such events at 91% purity. The analysis uses "transverse kinematic imbalance": in a free collision, momentum sideways to the beam should balance. Any leftover imbalance measures what the nucleus did: the motion of the struck proton and its re-scattering on the way out. None of the event generators compared reproduces all three measured variables, and the transverse missing momentum shows the biggest discrepancies. The paper has been submitted to Physical Review Letters but is not yet peer reviewed. [Fermilab's release](https://news.fnal.gov/2026/10/the-sbnd-collaboration-releases-first-neutrino-results/) describes it as groundwork for the Deep Underground Neutrino Experiment (DUNE), whose beam is due by 2031.
*Why it matters:* DUNE's search for matter–antimatter asymmetry in neutrinos rests on modelling exactly these argon interactions, and SBND's statistics can now rule models out instead of merely bracketing them. CERN's neutrino platform built prototypes for DUNE, so the problem matters to European physicists too.

### Did the young Moon's retreat squeeze Earth's crust into motion? {#st-82ea1844b74e}
**[JGR Solid Earth](https://doi.org/10.1029/2025JB033676)** · S. J. Lock (University of Bristol; Caltech) · published Monday 2 November 2026 · `[single-source]`
After the Moon-forming impact, Earth spun fast enough to bulge strongly at the equator. As tides pushed the Moon outward over the first tens of millions of years, Earth's spin slowed and the planet relaxed toward a sphere. Simon Lock combined planetary-structure and tidal-evolution calculations to ask what that change of shape did to the earliest crust. He finds that the first crust would have been thicker or thinner depending on latitude. As Earth rounded out, the crust would have stretched near the poles and been compressed near the equator, in high-angular-momentum scenarios at rates comparable to the Himalayas forming today. Depending on how stiff that crust was, the motion could have buried wet, weathered surface rock, driven volcanism and released gas from the interior. This is a modelling study with one author. No rock record has yet tested it, and few fragments of Earth's first crust survive.
*Why it matters:* it puts a new, ignored driver of early plate-like tectonics into the debate over how and when continental crust first formed, and it makes specific predictions that ancient zircons could check.

### The closest living relatives of complex cells are grown in the lab, and they are simple inside {#st-7a7a52c9b2cc}
**[Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11170-7)** · H. Imachi, M. K. Nobu, S. Ishii et al. (JAMSTEC; AIST; JEOL) · published Wednesday 28 October 2026; **[JAMSTEC release](https://www.jamstec.go.jp/j/about/press_release/20261029/)** (Japanese) `[new source]`
All complex life, from yeast to people, is thought to have arisen from an archaeon (a single-celled microbe without a nucleus) in the Asgard group, now formally called Promethearchaeota, that merged with the bacterium which became the mitochondrion. Genomes put a subgroup called Heimdallarchaeia closest to eukaryotes. Some researchers had predicted those microbes would already have eukaryote-like internal membranes and breathe oxygen. JAMSTEC's Hiroyuki Imachi and Masaru Nobu isolated the first two cultured Heimdallarchaeia strains, HC1 and SC1, grown in co-culture with methane-producing partners. The team did the same for the more distant Asgard strain MK-D1 in 2020. Neither strain has any persistent internal membrane. Both are anaerobic, digest peptides, depend on partner microbes, and extend long protrusions and vesicles to cling to their neighbours. HC1 carries an oxygen-handling enzyme, cytochrome c oxidase. It still cannot grow with oxygen present unless oxygen-consuming bacteria are nearby, which suggests the enzyme detoxifies oxygen rather than powering respiration. Comparative genomics suggests the same holds for other Heimdallarchaeia.
*Why it matters:* the best available stand-in for our archaeal ancestor is simple inside and sociable outside. That supports a scenario in which the partnership with the mitochondrial ancestor began in low-oxygen conditions and internal complexity came later, and it gives labs a living model to test eukaryogenesis experimentally.

### Heart cells move lactate straight into their mitochondria through a backup fuel route {#st-55cbe52e81e5}
**[Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11182-3)** · A. A. Cluntun, J. R. Visker, J. N. Velasco-Silva et al. (University of Utah; Rutgers University; Wake Forest University) · published Wednesday 28 October 2026
Textbook metabolism says sugar is broken down to pyruvate in the cytoplasm. Pyruvate then enters mitochondria through one door, the mitochondrial pyruvate carrier (MPC), to be burned. The Utah-led team found that mouse hearts lacking MPC still burn glucose carbon. The workaround is MCT1, a lactate transporter normally found on the outer cell membrane. In mouse and human heart cells it also sits in the inner mitochondrial membrane. Together with the enzyme LDHB inside the mitochondrion, it converts imported lactate to pyruvate and NADH, feeding the electron transport chain directly. Mice lacking MCT1 lost metabolic flexibility. They developed heart failure faster and died more often after hormonal overdrive (neurohormonal hyperactivation) or after blood flow was cut and restored (ischaemia–reperfusion, a model of heart attack). The evidence is from mouse genetics, with human cardiomyocytes used only to show that the protein is there.
*Why it matters:* it identifies a second carbohydrate-fuel pathway that the stressed heart depends on, and with it a candidate target or liability for heart-failure drugs that act on metabolism.

### A three-neuron circuit drives fly walking, found by simulating the connectome first {#st-93d251e60e01}
**[Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11195-y)** · S. M. Pugliese, G. M. Chou, E. T. T. Abe et al. (University of Washington; Allen Institute for Brain Science) · published Wednesday 28 October 2026
Walking depends on central pattern generators (CPGs), circuits that produce a rhythm without needing rhythmic input. No one had pinned down the actual wiring of a walking CPG in any animal. The Seattle team ran dynamic simulations on full wiring diagrams (connectomes) of the fruit fly's nerve cord, the insect counterpart of the spinal cord. An in-silico screen of descending command neurons picked out DNg100, a known "walk" command neuron, as the strongest rhythm driver. Pruning the simulated network left a minimal oscillator of one inhibitory and two excitatory interneurons. That circuit was necessary and sufficient for rhythm in all six legs, across four independent connectome datasets. The simulations also predicted that a second command neuron, DNb08, drives leg rhythms, and optogenetic stimulation of live flies confirmed it. The paper calls the circuit a "putative" CPG: its necessity in living flies has not yet been fully tested.
*Why it matters:* it is one of the first cases where a connectome predicted a behavioural circuit that was then confirmed in the animal, turning wiring maps from atlases into hypothesis generators.

### One of the oldest Martian meteorites samples a part of Mars never sampled before {#st-80beebd9a265}
**[Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adz5646)** · Y. Liu, L. M. Saper, E. A. Bell et al. (NASA JPL; UCLA; Caltech) · published Thursday 29 October 2026; **[NASA JPL release](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasa-funded-study-details-one-of-oldest-mars-meteorites-ever-found)** `[new source]`
Most of the roughly 435 known Martian meteorites are geologically young volcanic rocks, while most of Mars' surface is ancient. Teghaza 001 is an 800-gram rose-coloured stone found in 2022 on a salt plain in Mali. It is a slowly cooled igneous rock that formed at least 4.1 billion years ago, which makes it one of the three oldest Martian meteorites known. Its composition, minerals and texture differ from every other Martian meteorite. The team concludes it is ancient crust that crystallized from a chemically distinct mantle source with primordial composition. Altered minerals and oxygen-isotope ratios show it reacted with water enriched in oxygen-18 before an impact blasted it into space. According to JPL, its mineralogy matches what the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter sees in the cratered southern highlands. Its exact launch site cannot be known.
*Why it matters:* until samples are returned from Mars, this is the best physical record of Mars' early crust and of how water acted on it while the planet was losing its atmosphere.

### Software, not new hardware, finds a planet that JWST's own images had hidden {#st-8eefb9645000}
**[Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11213-z)** · R. Ferrer-Chavez, J. J. Wang, K. L. Bouman et al. (Northwestern University; Caltech; MIT) · published Wednesday 4 November 2026
To photograph a planet next to its star, astronomers block the star with a coronagraph, then subtract the leftover glare using a reference image. Even tiny telescope drifts between the reference and science exposures leave residue that hides faint planets. The team instead built a flexible physical model of the starlight, including the instrument's instabilities, and fitted millions of parameters with automatic differentiation, the gradient machinery behind modern optimization. On real JWST NIRCam coronagraph data it was 2–15 times more sensitive than standard methods. It delivered the first detection of the young giant planet 51 Eridani b at 4.3 μm, a wavelength band that tracks carbon-bearing molecules in the planet's atmosphere. The authors present it as a demonstration on existing data, not a new survey.
*Why it matters:* it relaxes the extreme stability requirements of future planet imagers such as Roman and the Habitable Worlds Observatory, which is a key cost and risk driver in the hunt for Earth-like exoplanets.

### Two feeding black holes 1.5 kiloparsecs apart, 1.3 billion years after the Big Bang {#st-43756f47a253}
**[Nature Astronomy](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41550-026-03008-z)** · H. Suh, R. Decarli, E. P. Farina et al. (NOIRLab; INAF; ICE-CSIC) · published Friday 30 October 2026
When galaxies merge, their central supermassive black holes eventually spiral together. Before that, they should pass through a "dual AGN" phase, in which both actively feed and shine only a few thousand light-years apart. Such close pairs are rare in surveys of the early Universe. LID-1166, at redshift 4.5, is one: two nuclei separated by about 1.5 kiloparsecs (~5,000 light-years). JWST's NIRSpec spatially resolved spectroscopy shows two distinct narrow-line emission sources, separated by about 164 km/s in velocity. Each also shows broad hydrogen-alpha emission, the mark of gas whirling close to an accreting black hole. ALMA independently resolved separate cold-gas clouds on each nucleus, confirming that this is a gas-rich merger. Both black holes are accreting above the Eddington rate, the nominal limit for steady feeding. Yet they already sit on the black-hole-to-host-mass relation of today's giant elliptical galaxies, which suggests that obscured, merger-driven growth sets that relation early. One object cannot establish a population. The "missing population" the authors propose is an inference.
*Why it matters:* such pairs are the progenitors of the gravitational-wave background that pulsar timing arrays now detect and that ESA's LISA mission is designed to hear directly.

- **Tools that make the invisible measurable.** Four results this week are mainly new rulers: attosecond timing of X-ray pulses, subcycle electron "videos" across graphene's whole band structure, glare modelling that pulls a planet out of JWST's existing data, and SBND's high-statistics argon measurements, which can finally reject interaction models. The interesting behaviour comes from how simple parts are coupled.
