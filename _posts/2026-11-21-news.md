- <a id="st-c705fa90a2c0" class="st-a"></a>**The SVP parliamentary group nominated National Councillors Thomas Aeschi (Zug) and Michael Graber (Upper Valais) on Friday 20 November as its two candidates to succeed Guy Parmelin in the Federal Council election on Wednesday 9 December.** The party's press release says Aeschi was chosen in the sixth round with 37 votes and Graber in the seventh with 42; both will now go before the other parliamentary groups' hearings. Left off were the French-speaking candidates, among them Geneva's Céline Amaudruz, the only woman in the field, and Bernese cantonal minister Pierre Alain Schnegg, along with former party president Marco Chiesa. SVP vice group chair Franz Grüter called it a "real and good choice" and reminded the other candidates that they had signed a pledge not to accept election if parliament picked them instead, and that a member elected that way can be expelled under the party's statutes. - <a id="st-453a1a6bb8ec" class="st-a"></a>**Federal Councillor Albert Rösti joined about 30 countries' ministers at COP31 in Antalya on Friday 20 November in presenting plans to exit coal, oil and gas, and said Switzerland would raise its contribution to two special climate funds by 10% over four years.** Rösti said Switzerland "already has a plan": the Climate and Innovation Act that voters approved in 2023, which sets net-zero greenhouse-gas emissions by 2050. He also said Switzerland wants the exit and its timetable written into the conference's final text. SRF points out that, unlike France or Norway, Switzerland presented no year-by-year phase-out schedule, only a summary of targets it had already set, and that around 140 countries have announced net-zero-by-2050 goals. On money, SRF reports that the Federal Council at the same time plans to cut its payment to the overall Global Environment Facility (the multilateral fund the two special funds sit inside), saving almost ten times what the 10% increase adds. Rösti called the increase "a modest amount" meant as "a signal", and said climate spending was the area least affected by the federal savings package. ## 🌍 World politics & geopolitics

- <a id="st-a30f8e586599" class="st-a"></a>**The 128 member states of the UN Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) agreed in Geneva early on Saturday 21 November to "elaborate" an international instrument on lethal autonomous weapons, often called "killer robots", over the next two years, but not to negotiate a legally binding ban.** Nearly 100 countries had pushed for treaty negotiations on weapons that can kill without a human making the final decision to fire. The United States, Russia and Israel were among those opposed, and since CCW decisions need consensus, any one of them could block. Talks on the wording ran through the night, and the conference's Swiss chair said some states had rejected the word "negotiate". He called the result proof that "multilateral diplomacy can still deliver concrete results". The Stop Killer Robots coalition called the mandate "watered down", and Human Rights Watch called the outcome disappointing. ([Al Jazeera, 21 Nov 2026](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/21/nations-agree-to-draft-rules-for-killer-robots-at-arms-control-summit); [DW, 21 Nov 2026](https://www.dw.com/en/killer-robots-talks-at-un-end-with-no-legally-binding-ban/a-80008584))
  - Why it matters: after more than ten years of talks, this is the first mandate to draft actual rules, but "elaborate" is weaker than "negotiate", and the states building these weapons kept their veto. Campaigners say AI-targeting systems are already in use in current wars.
