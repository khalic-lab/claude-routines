## 🔭 Physics, chemistry, math & quantum

### Superconductivity survives in a single copper–oxygen plane {#st-6d1364bee376}
**[Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10857-1)** · H. Luo, D. Song, Y. Yu et al. (Fudan University; University of British Columbia; Zhejiang University)
Cuprates — copper-oxide ceramics that superconduct (carry current with zero resistance) at unusually high temperatures — are built from stacked CuO₂ (copper–oxygen) planes, and a long-standing question is how few of those planes the effect actually needs. Borrowing the top-down, sticky-tape exfoliation trick that made graphene, a Fudan-led team thinned a cuprate all the way down to a single CuO₂ plane and still measured superconductivity, isolating the truly two-dimensional limit of the problem. Earlier work had shown the essential physics fits within two planes; pushing to one strips the system to what may be its minimal ingredient.
*Why it matters:* If one copper–oxygen layer is enough to superconduct, theories of high-temperature superconductivity can be tested against the barest possible system — a cleaner route toward understanding, and someday designing, better superconductors.

### An EPFL graphene device weighs one-third of an electron {#st-19fb2d603f06}
**[Nature Physics](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41567-026-03412-2)** · M. Di Luca, E. Hajigeorgiou, Z. Zhou et al. (EPFL; Oxford; NIMS) · EPFL release: [How do you measure one third of an electron?](https://actu.epfl.ch/news/how-do-you-measure-one-third-of-an-electron/) `[new source]`
In the fractional quantum Hall effect — what emerges when electrons are confined to two dimensions in an intense magnetic field — the electron fluid gives rise to "quasiparticles" that act as if they carry only a fraction of an electron's charge. Measuring that fraction cleanly is hard, because the usual interferometer designs entangle bulk and edge contributions. The EPFL-led team instead built an *antidot* — a controllable bump in the device's potential landscape that behaves as a tunable artificial impurity — into a graphene sample and read the charge of quasiparticles tunnelling through it, directly extracting values such as e/3 (one-third of the elementary charge).
*Why it matters:* A clean way to weigh fractional charges is a prerequisite for probing these quasiparticles' exotic quantum statistics — the same physics being courted for topological quantum computing. The lead group is Swiss.

### Chemists learn to walk the nitrogen around a ring {#st-655582cea6af}
**[Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11006-4)** · W. Choi, H. Ju, J. Park et al. (Institute for Basic Science; KAIST)
Pyridines — six-membered aromatic rings carrying a single nitrogen, ubiquitous in drug molecules — behave very differently depending on where a substituent sits relative to that nitrogen, and normally each "positional isomer" (same atoms, different arrangement) must be built from scratch. The IBS/KAIST team report a way to reorganise the ring itself: a controlled sequence of nitrogen insertion and deletion transposes the nitrogen to a new position, relocating the attached groups without changing their identity. A companion paper from Cambridge performs related "skeletal editing" on tertiary benzylamines, rewriting their core carbon–nitrogen connectivity late in a synthesis ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11009-1), University of Cambridge).
*Why it matters:* Skeletal editing lets chemists remodel a molecule's core in the final steps instead of redesigning a whole synthesis — a faster lane for exploring drug-like variants around a promising scaffold.

## 🧬 Biology, medicine & neuroscience

### A blood test tracks who benefits from immunotherapy before lung surgery {#st-b63b986ea23d}
**[Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10925-6)** · T. Cascone, M. M. Awad, J. D. Spicer et al. (MD Anderson Cancer Center; Memorial Sloan Kettering; McGill University)
In resectable non-small-cell lung cancer (a lung tumour still removable by surgery), the CheckMate 77T trial had already shown that giving the immunotherapy nivolumab before and after surgery improves event-free survival; this analysis asks *who* benefits. Following circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA — fragments of tumour genome shed into the blood) in the biomarker-evaluable patients (41% of those randomised), the team found ctDNA detectable in 85% before treatment and 92% by the end of pre-surgery dosing, and that clearing it during that window tracked with benefit.
*Why it matters:* A blood test that signals whether immunotherapy is working before the operation could help spare non-responders unnecessary treatment and flag who needs more — though this is a retrospective subgroup analysis, not yet a decision rule.

### An in-vivo CRISPR screen reads immune cells inside real tumours {#st-c398db606c41}
**[Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10906-9)** · Q. Liu, P. A. Chen, E. Urs et al. (UCSF; Gladstone Institutes)
Finding genetic tweaks that help immune cells attack solid tumours is a central goal of cell therapy, but the genes that matter inside a living tumour often stay hidden in lab-dish (in-vitro) screens. The UCSF/Gladstone team built a mouse model that efficiently recovers human T cells from solid tumours, making genome-wide CRISPR screens — systematically switching off each gene to see which alter T-cell behaviour — feasible with only a handful of animals. The tumour-infiltrating cells showed the classic signs of "exhaustion," the dysfunctional state that blunts immunotherapy, giving the screen a realistic setting to map its drivers.
*Why it matters:* A screen that mirrors the hostile tumour environment can surface engineering targets that dish experiments miss — grist for the next generation of CAR-T and tumour-infiltrating-lymphocyte therapies.

## 🌌 Astronomy & cosmology

### Distant galaxies may hide extra mass in dim, low-mass stars {#st-b409a5e8c561}
**[Nature Astronomy](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41550-026-02932-4)** · C. M. Cheng, M. Slob, M. Kriek et al. (Leiden University; Carnegie Institution for Science; Yale)
A galaxy's stellar mass is inferred from its light, but that conversion hinges on the initial mass function (IMF) — the assumed spread of star masses at birth, usually copied from the Milky Way. Faint low-mass stars dominate the mass yet contribute little light, so the low-mass end is normally guessed, not measured. Using ultra-deep JWST spectra of nine massive quiescent galaxies at redshift z ≈ 0.7 (light that left them roughly 6 billion years ago), the Leiden-led team constrained that low-mass end directly and found the IMF "bottom-heavy" — richer in dwarf stars than the Milky Way's, implying more hidden stellar mass than standard assumptions give.
*Why it matters:* JWST has turned up early galaxies that look almost too massive to exist so soon; a bottom-heavy IMF shows galaxies really can carry extra unseen stellar mass, which bears on how the masses — and the assembly speed — of the earliest galaxies are read. The measurement is at intermediate redshift, so it informs rather than settles the high-redshift debate.

### The first globular-cluster stream spotted around another galaxy {#st-1526219da6d2}
**[Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10878-w)** · J. K. Holm, S. Pearson, J. Nibauer et al. (University of Copenhagen; DTU; Princeton)
Stellar streams — thin ribbons of stars pulled from a cluster by a galaxy's tides — trace the host's gravity, and so its dark matter. Until now, globular-cluster streams were known only in the Milky Way. In deep Hubble Space Telescope imaging of the ultra-diffuse galaxy UGC 9050-Dw1 (a galaxy as sprawling as the Milky Way but with very few stars), a Copenhagen-led team found evidence for the first such stream around another galaxy, and used generative modelling of its shape to weigh the progenitor cluster.
*Why it matters:* Extragalactic streams open a new way to map dark matter well beyond the Milky Way — including the puzzling ultra-diffuse galaxies whose dark-matter content is fiercely debated.

### Mars's moon Deimos was shaped by one glancing blow {#st-705a3ea31b01}
**[Nature Astronomy](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41550-026-02956-w)** · S. D. Raducan, H. F. Agrusa, E. Asphaug et al. (University of Bern; Vrije Universiteit Brussel; Royal Observatory of Belgium)
Deimos, the smaller, 12-km Martian moon, is oddly smooth and coated in fine regolith (loose surface dust), unlike its heavily cratered sibling Phobos, and its shape is dominated by one large south-polar depression. Simulations by the Bern-led team show a single oblique impact from a roughly 320-metre projectile can quantitatively reproduce that depression, the global dust blanket, and finer features such as bright streaks and infilled craters — a "subcatastrophic" hit that remodelled the moon without breaking it apart.
*Why it matters:* Tying Deimos's odd surface to a single impact sharpens the science targets for JAXA's planned Martian Moons eXploration (MMX) mission to the two moons. The lead group is Swiss.

### The Milky Way ate a galaxy even earlier than we knew {#st-86e729dc2452}
**[Nature Astronomy](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41550-026-02931-5)** · D. Massari, C. Zerbinati, C. Fanelli et al. (INAF; University of Bologna; University of Groningen) · INAF release: [Antiche vestigia nel cuore della Via Lattea](https://www.media.inaf.it/2026/08/17/vestigia-cuore-via-lattea/) `[new source]`
The Milky Way grew by devouring smaller galaxies, and the largest early merger securely known — Gaia-Sausage-Enceladus (a dwarf galaxy that fell in) — dates to about 10 billion years ago. Using globular clusters as tracers and a new method to date them, the INAF Bologna-led team found the clusters split into three distinct age–metallicity sequences: one native to the proto-Milky Way, one from Gaia-Sausage-Enceladus, and one pointing to an earlier, previously unrecognised major accretion event some 1.8 billion years before it.
*Why it matters:* Each identified merger is a chapter in the Galaxy's origin story; finding an even older one pushes back the clock on when the Milky Way began assembling in earnest.

## 🧠 Why it matters

- **Weighing what we cannot see.** Three of this week's astronomy results are, at heart, about accounting for unseen mass — a bottom-heavy stellar mix hiding extra mass in distant galaxies, a tidal stream weighing dark matter around another galaxy, and dated star clusters reconstructing a merger written into the Milky Way's halo. Different tools (spectra, streams, cluster ages), one theme: the visible light is only a proxy, and the interesting mass is usually the part you have to infer.

"Skeletal editing" is quietly maturing from a curiosity into a general tactic — the kind of method shift that, like the physicists' single-CuO₂-plane and single-antidot experiments, is really about controlling a system down to its last essential element.
