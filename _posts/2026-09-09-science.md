## 🔭 Physics, chemistry, math & quantum

### AI agents claim a Navier–Stokes “blowup” — a Millennium Prize result, and a fight over who did it {#st-8c435283c1a4}
**[OpenAI — “On the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize Problem”](https://openai.com/index/navier-stokes-solution/)** · announced Tuesday 8 September · `[via snippet]` `[disputed]`
On Tuesday 8 September, OpenAI announced that a swarm of roughly 10,000 autonomous AI agents — running on an internal model not released to the public — had constructed a proof that the three-dimensional Navier–Stokes equations can develop a *singularity* (a "blowup," where some infinitesimal patch of fluid is driven to flow infinitely fast in finite time). The Navier–Stokes equations apply Newton's second law to fluids, and whether their smooth solutions can blow up is one of the six remaining Millennium Prize Problems posed by the Clay Mathematics Institute in 2000, each worth $1 million. OpenAI says its result is the version with a *smooth forcing* term (a mathematically well-behaved external push) and finite energy throughout — a legitimate form of the problem — and that the whole argument was formally checked in Lean, a proof-assistant language that mechanically verifies each logical step. By the company's account the agents worked about 88 hours, exchanged ~2.7 million messages, and burned ~130 billion output tokens at a cost of several million dollars (per OpenAI's Sébastien Bubeck). The result has *not* been peer-reviewed, and even a Lean-verified proof leaves open what it means: mathematicians will need time to judge whether it truly settles the prize's specific formulation. Independently, twelve hours earlier, Tristan Buckmaster (NYU) and Levent Alpöge (Anthropic) had announced finite-time blowup with smooth forcing for three related systems — incompressible porous-media flow, the Boussinesq equations, and 3D incompressible Euler — also with a Lean formalization, after nearly a year of work using Anthropic's Claude and OpenAI's Codex. Both efforts lean heavily on a strategy developed by Diego Córdoba (ICMAT, Madrid) and Luis Martínez-Zoroa (CUNEF, Madrid); Charles Fefferman of Princeton, who wrote the Clay problem's official statement, called those two "the heroes of the story." The dispute is twofold: OpenAI cedes priority on the 3D Euler result to Buckmaster and Alpöge but claims Navier–Stokes for itself, while Buckmaster has suggested OpenAI's researchers or agents may have benefited from his group's work through their own models — and, separately, the mathematical significance is still being weighed even though the code checks out. As reported by Quanta, timelines and credit remain unsettled.
*Why it matters:* If it holds, it is by a wide margin the most consequential mathematical proof an AI system has produced — a real result on a century-and-a-half-old question about turbulence, not a benchmark score. But the credit tangle is the cautionary half of the story: the decisive ideas came from two European mathematicians working by hand, and "an AI proved it" flattens a messier, more human picture. (Read soberly: the blowup is an idealized-math statement — real fluids are made of molecules, so nothing here changes how water flows down a drain.) ## 🌌 Astronomy & cosmology

### The most distant galaxy proto-supercluster yet, mapped in 3D {#st-98c87a104889}
**[The Astrophysical Journal — 10.3847/1538-4357/ae8091](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ae8091)** · V. Ramakrishnan et al. (Purdue University; KASI; Rutgers University) · via [NOIRLab, 8 Sep 2026](https://noirlab.edu/public/news/noirlab2622/)
An international team has found the most distant progenitor of a galaxy supercluster ever identified — a *proto-supercluster* (an enormous, still-loosely-bound collection of galaxies not yet settled into a stable, gravitationally-bound cluster) dubbed COSMOS-z3.1-A, seen as it was when the Universe was only about 1–3 billion years old. Led by then–Purdue graduate student Vandana Ramakrishnan, the team first flagged strong galaxy overdensities in the ODIN survey — 100+ nights of narrow-band imaging with the 570-megapixel Dark Energy Camera on the 4-metre Blanco telescope in Chile — which gives only 2D positions on the sky. To place the galaxies in three dimensions they added spectroscopy (splitting light to measure each galaxy's distance) from the DESI instrument at Kitt Peak, Gemini South, and Keck, confirming the structure's members and its link to the surrounding cosmic web. Among 150 candidate protoclusters, COSMOS-z3.1-A and a companion stood out as strikingly massive for that early epoch.
*Why it matters:* Nearby galaxy clusters are mature; to see how the Universe's largest gravitationally-bound structures actually assembled, you have to catch them mid-build in the early Universe — and this is the farthest-back example on record, a benchmark against which theories of structure formation can be tested.

### Hubble and Webb team up on the tiniest bodies beyond Neptune — and find fewer than expected {#st-f6277660bcf4}
**[The Astronomical Journal — 10.3847/1538-3881/ae907f](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-3881/ae907f)** and **[10.3847/1538-3881/ae9084](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-3881/ae9084)** · teams led from the University of Victoria (National Research Council of Canada) and Northern Arizona University · via [NASA/Hubble, 8 Sep 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/hubble/nasas-hubble-webb-find-far-out-solar-system-objects-remember-past/) `[new source]`
For the first time, astronomers pointed the Hubble and James Webb space telescopes at the *same* patch of sky simultaneously to study Trans-Neptunian Objects (TNOs) — small, icy bodies orbiting beyond Neptune that are leftover *planetesimals*, the city-sized building blocks that never merged into a planet out there. In two companion papers published Tuesday 8 September in *The Astronomical Journal*, the teams characterised 27 newly found, exceptionally faint TNOs (most more than 100 million times dimmer than the naked-eye limit), using Hubble's visible light and Webb's infrared to pin down each object's colour — a fingerprint of surface composition — plus its size and orbit. Two surprises: they found *fewer* small TNOs than models predicted, and the colours of these tiny bodies track the same colour–orbit relationships seen in their larger cousins, in both the dynamically "cold" population (on near-circular orbits) and the "hot" population (scattered outward onto tilted, elliptical orbits when the giant planets migrated).
*Why it matters:* A shortfall of small bodies is a clue about *how* planetesimals formed — it fits pictures where they condense quickly and whole, rather than grinding up from countless collisions — and the shared colours suggest these objects still "remember" the primordial chemistry of the disk they were born in.

## 🧬 Biology, medicine & neuroscience

### 2026 Lasker Awards: wakefulness, a hemophilia antibody, and a Parkinson's advocate {#st-69729c971e41}
**[Lasker Foundation — 2026 Awards](https://laskerfoundation.org/)** · announced 8 September 2026 · `[new source]`
The 2026 Lasker Awards — often a leading indicator of future Nobels — went to three lines of work. The **Basic Medical Research Award** honours Emmanuel Mignot (Stanford) and Masashi Yanagisawa (UT Southwestern / University of Tsukuba) for discovering *orexin* (also called hypocretin), the brain peptide that keeps us awake; loss of orexin-producing neurons causes narcolepsy, and the finding has since seeded a new class of orexin-targeting drugs for sleep–wake disorders. The **Clinical Medical Research Award** goes to Takehisa Kitazawa, Kunihiro Hattori and Tomoyuki Igawa (Chugai Pharmaceutical) for a *bispecific antibody* for hemophilia A — an engineered antibody whose two arms grab two different clotting factors and hold them together, mimicking the missing factor VIII so patients bleed far less. The **Public Service Award** recognises actor Michael J. Fox for two decades of Parkinson's-disease research advocacy.
*Why it matters:* Each award marks basic biology that already reached patients — narcolepsy therapeutics, a subcutaneous prophylactic that reshaped hemophilia care, and a foundation that has become one of Parkinson's largest research funders.

### An anti-IL-33 antibody cuts COPD flare-ups by up to a third in two phase 3 trials {#st-d2b23fcfc912}
**[New England Journal of Medicine — 10.1056/NEJMoa2606998](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2606998)** · AstraZeneca (OBERON and TITANIA trials) · presented at the European Respiratory Society Congress, Barcelona, 8 September 2026
AstraZeneca reported full results from two Phase 3 trials of *tozorakimab*, a monoclonal antibody that blocks IL-33 — an "alarmin" signalling protein that helps drive airway inflammation and mucus over-production in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Added on top of standard inhaled therapy, tozorakimab reduced the annual rate of moderate-to-severe exacerbations (the flare-ups that send COPD patients to hospital) versus placebo: among former smokers, by 29% in OBERON and 34% in TITANIA; across the wider population including current smokers, by 30% and 29%. The results were published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* simultaneously with their presentation in Barcelona. AstraZeneca expects a US filing decision in early 2027 and has raised its peak-sales forecast above $5 billion.
*Why it matters:* COPD is among the world's leading causes of death and has stubbornly resisted new biologics — several anti-inflammatory antibodies flopped in it — so a clean, replicated exacerbation benefit across two trials is a meaningful win for a large, underserved patient group, most of it developed and reported on this side of the Atlantic.

## 🧠 Why it matters

- **AI as a mathematical instrument — and the credit problem it creates.** This week's Navier–Stokes claims are the clearest sign yet that large models can do real work at the research frontier of mathematics, not just reproduce known results. But they arrive entangled: the pivotal strategy was human (Córdoba and Martínez-Zoroa), two labs and two companies are disputing priority, and even a machine-checked proof still needs human mathematicians to say what it *means*. The lesson isn't "AI solved a Millennium problem" — it's that verification (here, Lean) and attribution are becoming the hard parts, and a science desk should resist the tidy headline in favour of the messier, more accurate one.
