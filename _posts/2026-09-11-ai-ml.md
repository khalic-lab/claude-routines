## 📄 ML/AI research (arXiv)

This cycle's centre of gravity is the **reward problem** in reinforcement learning: where do reliable training signals come from once you leave the narrow worlds of maths and code, where an answer is cheap to check? Three papers circle it from different sides — one arguing that verifier soundness, not compute, is now the binding constraint (Proof-Carrying Cognition); one manufacturing objective rewards for messy diagnostic tasks by hiding the answer inside a simulator (TRACE); and one showing that a coding agent trained against its own executable tests can close most of the gap to frontier models (T1). A second, quieter thread is **doing more with less at inference time** — a mechanistic account of why post-training quantization barely hurts accuracy, and a compressor that hits any ratio from one model. If you read three in full, make them **Proof-Carrying Cognition** (the clearest statement of where RL's headroom actually is), **T1** (the most complete open recipe for long-horizon agent RL this cycle), and **Why Does Post-Training Quantization Work?** (a rare, satisfying "why", not just "how").

- <a id="st-11f7bd2a9f1a" class="st-a"></a>**Verifier soundness — not compute — is what caps RL on reasoning** — [arXiv:2609.09776](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.09776) · E. Reddy M, S. Karmakar (Testsigma; University of San Diego; Intuit) · `[preprint]`

  Recent gains in model reasoning come from reinforcement learning on reasoning traces, but almost entirely in domains that have a cheap, trustworthy checker — a maths answer key, a unit test. The authors call the missing ingredient elsewhere the *verification gap*, and model it: in a best-of-N setup (generate N answers, keep the one the verifier likes best), the correlation between the verifier and ground truth (ρ) is an exact "exchange rate" between test-time compute and real capability, and an unsound verifier imposes a polynomial penalty as you scale N. In program-synthesis tests with executable ground truth, a weak verifier's soundness collapses under optimization pressure (0.94 → 0.32 at N=4096) — i.e. selection alone "manufactures" apparent quality that is really reward hacking — while a verifier re-anchored to reality keeps improving.

  **Why it matters:** it reframes the scaling debate: past the domains with a clean checker, throwing more inference compute at a shaky reward can actively make a model *look* better while getting worse, and the paper gives a way to measure how much. _Submitted 2026-09-09._

- <a id="st-20f7787cabb2" class="st-a"></a>**A 122B coding agent trained by running a real shell** — [arXiv:2609.11042](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11042) · J. Yang, Y. Shi, H. Mi et al. (NUS; Tencent) · `[preprint]`

  T1 is a Mixture-of-Experts model (MoE — only a fraction of the network's "expert" sub-modules fire per token, so a 122B-parameter model runs at a fraction of the compute) trained with RL while operating an actual shell in a cloud sandbox for 300-plus tool-call turns per task, rewarded by executing each task's own test suite. The recipe's substance is in stabilising that training: a dense process reward that scores a trajectory by how many verifiers pass, plus engineering (they call it TITO/R3) to keep the tokens the model trained on aligned with the ones it actually sampled — cutting the train-vs-inference log-probability gap from 0.021 to 0.013. On Terminal-Bench 2.1 the post-training raises the base model from 43.8% to 64.0% of tasks resolved, with training tasks deliberately disjoint from the benchmark to guard against memorisation.

  **Why it matters:** long-horizon "do real work in a terminal" agents are exactly the coding-assistant frontier, and this is one of the more complete public recipes for training one with RL rather than prompting a closed model. _Submitted 2026-09-10._

- <a id="st-6d93888bc6cd" class="st-a"></a>**Running a skill as a subagent beats pasting its instructions into context** — [arXiv:2609.09233](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.09233) · W. T. Piriyakulkij, R. Lawrence, N. Prasad et al. (Cornell University; Microsoft Research) · `[preprint]`

  "Agent skills" are reusable capability bundles — instructions, scripts, resources — that an agent normally uses by loading the instructions into its own context window and following them. The paper tests the alternative: invoke the skill as a *subagent* with a fresh context window instead. It finds subagent execution wins when a skill exposes a clear input→output contract and its instructions actually encode the procedure to fulfil it, because the main agent's reasoning degrades as its context fills up; the cost is extra tokens spent coordinating between the main agent and the subagent.

  **Why it matters:** it's a direct, empirical read on an architecture choice practitioners face today (this is the subagents-vs-skills question in tools like Claude Code), and names the specific condition — a clean contract — under which delegation pays for its overhead. _Submitted 2026-09-09._

- <a id="st-e6ded962baca" class="st-a"></a>**Why post-training quantization barely dents accuracy** — [arXiv:2609.11716](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11716) · Y. Chen, M. Beyer, J. Zhu, J. Chen (Tsinghua University; Bosch) · `[preprint]`

  Post-training quantization stores a trained model's weights at lower precision (e.g. 4-bit) to shrink it, and each rounded weight injects a small error into the model's internal activations. Naively those errors should compound layer by layer and wreck predictions — and in a randomly initialised network they do. The authors ask why they don't in a *pretrained* one, and find two mechanisms that emerge during pretraining: the error a layer newly adds tends to partially *cancel* the error it inherited from below, so the discrepancy grows slowly; and the output ("LM-head") geometry preferentially preserves the ranking and probabilities of the top, most-confident tokens.

  **Why it matters:** quantization is how large models fit on modest or local hardware, and it has largely been an empirical dark art; a mechanistic account of *why* it survives suggests where it will break (long chains, low-confidence tokens) rather than leaving it to trial and error. _Submitted 2026-09-11._

- <a id="st-fb677ba2f6f5" class="st-a"></a>**Manufacturing objective rewards for tasks with no answer key** — [arXiv:2609.10315](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10315) · R. Sun, Z. Shi, B. He (independent researchers) · `[preprint]`

  RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR — training against an automatic correctness check) works for maths and code but not for diagnosis, where establishing the true cause of an anomaly needs costly expert investigation and may stay ambiguous. TRACE sidesteps that: it samples a hidden intervention, injects it into a controlled simulator, and generates the data that intervention would produce — so the injected cause *is* the ground-truth label and gives an objective reward, while the agent still has to investigate noisy, confounded evidence with Python and SQL. In a digital-advertising diagnostic environment with 12 root causes, supervised fine-tuning lifts a 35B model from 0.159 to 0.637 full-attribution accuracy, and subsequent RL reaches 0.757 — above every prompted baseline tested, including Claude Opus 5 at 0.686.

  **Why it matters:** it's a general trick for extending RLVR into domains where verification is normally expensive — synthesise the world so the answer is known by construction — and shows a mid-size open model overtaking frontier models on the resulting task. _Submitted 2026-09-11._

- <a id="st-d8c31199241b" class="st-a"></a>**One compressor for every compression ratio** — [arXiv:2609.11192](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11192) · K. Zhao, Z. Miao, A. Aizawa, Y. Tsuruoka (University of Tokyo; National Institute of Informatics) · `[preprint]`

  Soft context compression squeezes a long context into a handful of "memory tokens" a frozen model reads in place of the raw text. Existing compressors bake the ratio in at training time, so each deployed ratio needs its own model and the same ratio gets applied to every input regardless of need. FlexComp trains one model to work at any ratio (Matryoshka-style: the memory budget is sampled per example during training), then picks the budget per input via confidence-based routing or a lightweight predictor. A single model matches separately trained fixed-ratio specialists, preserves 98%+ accuracy at up to 266× average compression, and at serving scale cuts the KV cache (KV-cache — the stored keys/values that let a model avoid recomputing earlier tokens) by 50% while raising decoding throughput 47%.

  **Why it matters:** it turns compression ratio from a fixed deployment decision into a per-request knob, which is the practical difference between one model to serve and a dozen. _Submitted 2026-09-11._

- <a id="st-db63f3404b2c" class="st-a"></a>**An agent's hidden states predict whether it will succeed** — [arXiv:2609.09448](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.09448) · P. M. Mammen, E. Joswin, S. Medicherla (UMass Amherst; independent) · `[preprint]`

  As agents move into safety-critical settings, knowing how much to trust an in-progress run matters, but agentic workflows fail in complex ways — bad plans, wrong tool calls, a shifting environment — that surface confidence scores capture poorly. The authors probe the model's internal representations instead: Latent Trajectory Dynamics summarises how the residual stream (the model's running internal activation vector) changes across a trajectory, and an Action Representation Probe reads the state at each action decision. Across Bash, SQL and Python benchmarks and three model families, both beat generation- and sequence-based confidence baselines, giving a zero-overhead reliability monitor that needs no extra prompting or repeated sampling.

  **Why it matters:** a cheap "is this run going off the rails?" signal read straight from activations is exactly what you want to gate or halt an autonomous agent before it does damage. _Submitted 2026-09-09._

- <a id="st-0db67c569421" class="st-a"></a>**Planning with a peek at future states: hard in theory, tractable in practice** — [arXiv:2609.11807](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11807) · C. Pla, H. Richard, M. Abeille, V. Perchet (ENSAE; Criteo AI Lab) · `[preprint]`

  This is a theory result on RL with "transition look-ahead" — the agent may see which states a sequence of ℓ actions *would* visit before committing. Look-ahead can help a lot, but exact optimal planning with it was known to be NP-hard (computationally intractable at scale) only for discount factors pushed arbitrarily close to 1; it was open whether the hardness was real for ordinary settings. The paper resolves both ends: exact planning stays NP-hard for *every* fixed discount factor, yet a randomized polynomial-time scheme gets near-optimal for any fixed look-ahead depth, with regret matching classical tabular RL up to log factors even when transitions are unknown.

  **Why it matters:** "look before you leap" is a natural upgrade for planning agents; this says you shouldn't chase the exact optimum (it's genuinely hard) but can get essentially all the benefit efficiently. _Submitted 2026-09-11._

- <a id="st-128470e2b248" class="st-a"></a>**A dataset of how AI scientists reason, not just what they output** — [arXiv:2609.09203](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.09203) · A. Bansal, K. Balaji (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]`

  Benchmarks for autonomous "AI scientist" agents usually grade only the final artefact — code, a hypothesis, a paper — discarding the reasoning that produced it. OpenDiscoveryTrace releases 558 complete agent trajectories across 124 scientific tasks (drug discovery, materials, genomics, literature analysis), each step recording thoughts, tool calls, observations, errors and self-reported confidence. A striking early finding: three frontier models reach comparable success rates (84–89%), yet the *process* differs sharply — one model produced roughly 30× more errors than another despite similar final scores.

  **Why it matters:** if agents that score the same reason very differently, output-only leaderboards are hiding the failure modes that matter for trusting an agent's work — and a public trace dataset is what lets anyone audit them. _Submitted 2026-09-09._

On the full run, **Fable 5.1** (xhigh) leads with a 3.25 judge average (grade mix A=104 / B=112 / C=10, no D/F), edging **GPT-6 Astra** (xhigh) at 3.23 (A=106 / B=102 / C=18) by 0.024 points — the leaderboard calls it an audit-level tie, with Fable stronger at conservatively-scoped partial work and Astra the better "theorem closer." Both sit well short of saturation: this is a real but modest step on research-math reasoning, not a solved benchmark, and it lands the same week OpenAI moved GPT-6 Astra to general release.
