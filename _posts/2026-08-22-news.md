- <a id="st-d62fca04f3a7" class="st-a"></a>**A US federal judge struck down the State Department policy that had suspended immigrant visas for applicants from 75 countries, calling it "patently unlawful" on Friday 21 August.** US District Judge Jeannette Vargas, sitting in Manhattan (the Southern District of New York), ruled that the freeze — announced and in effect since January 2026 — exceeded Secretary of State Marco Rubio's authority, because federal immigration law explicitly strips the secretary of control over how consular officers process immigrant visas. "The Policy, which categorically prohibits the issuance of immigrant visas based upon the nationality of the applicant, represents a direct abrogation of this statutory scheme," she wrote. The State Department had justified the suspension by labelling applicants from those countries — among them Brazil, Colombia, Uruguay, Bosnia, Albania, Pakistan and Bangladesh, plus many African, Middle Eastern and Caribbean states — as "at a high risk for becoming a public charge" (immigration-law jargon for someone judged likely to depend on government support). The suit was brought by the Catholic Legal Immigration Network and African Communities Together, together with visa applicants and US citizens sponsoring relatives; the department did not immediately comment and can appeal. [Reuters via KELO, 21 Aug 2026](https://kelo.com/2026/08/21/us-judge-strikes-down-policy-suspending-immigrant-visa-processing-for-75-nations/); [Al Jazeera, 22 Aug 2026](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/22/us-judge-strikes-down-trump-immigrant-visa-ban-affecting-75-countries)
  - Why it matters: This is one of the broadest court rollbacks yet of the administration's immigration drive, restoring a legal path for families across dozens of countries. It rests on a structural limit rather than a discretionary one — Congress assigned immigrant-visa processing to statute, not to the secretary's judgement — which is harder to route around than a policy the administration can simply reword.

- <a id="st-bc17f02f597f" class="st-a"></a>**The Council of States' energy committee announced on Friday 21 August that it will not finish vetting the Switzerland–EU electricity agreement in time for the autumn parliamentary session, pushing the upper chamber's debate on that treaty into the winter session.** The electricity accord is one of three new treaties — alongside food safety and health — inside the wider Bilaterals III package, the negotiated update to Switzerland's bilateral relationship with the European Union. The committee (known as Urek-S) said too many complex questions remain open and is planning extra sittings after the autumn session, which detaches the contested electricity deal from the rest of the package. The Federal Council intends to put the package to voters in four separate tranches, and after these first deliberations two or more separate referendums now look increasingly likely. [NZZ, 21 Aug 2026](https://www.nzz.ch/schweiz/zu-viele-schwierige-fragen-offen-staenderaete-verschieben-die-debatte-zum-stromabkommen-auf-den-winter-ld.10020504)
  - Why it matters: For anyone in Switzerland, this is about how — and how fast — the EU relationship moves forward. Splitting the electricity accord out gives its critics more room and lets voters accept the overarching framework while rejecting individual treaties, reshaping both the timeline and the referendum arithmetic of Bilaterals III.

- <a id="st-b6feca571f59" class="st-a"></a>**Economiesuisse — Switzerland's main business federation — has withdrawn its earlier backing for raising taxes to fund the army, a reversal reported on Friday 21 August that leaves Defence Minister Martin Pfister increasingly isolated.** Having surprised observers in May by supporting a temporary rise in value-added tax (VAT) for defence, the federation changed course after the federal government sharply lifted its tax-revenue forecasts — companies, in particular, are paying more than expected. It now argues the roughly 2 billion francs a year the army needs should be found by reprioritising within the ordinary budget "without a tax increase," and without breaching the debt brake (the constitutional rule that caps federal deficits). Pfister's VAT plan had already been rejected across the spectrum in consultation — the Social Democrats, Greens, SVP and FDP all opposed it, leaving only his own Mitte party behind it — and the federation also rebuffed an FDP idea to book part of the arms spending as "extraordinary," a manoeuvre the government deems unlawful. [NZZ, 21 Aug 2026](https://www.nzz.ch/schweiz/hoehere-steuern-fuer-die-armee-martin-pfister-verliert-weitere-verbuendete-ld.10020492)
  - Why it matters: Parliament broadly agrees the army should get a fund allowed to take on temporary debt so it can buy equipment faster, but there is no consensus on how to pay for it. Losing the business lobby's cover makes an already politically toxic tax rise even less likely and pushes the fight toward finding cuts elsewhere in the federal budget.

Liel stressed that the appeal is distinct from the BDS movement, which seeks a boycott of Israel itself: "We are not calling for a boycott of Israel… we are Israeli patriots," he said, casting pressure on the settlements as "a measure for Israel, not against it" and meant to push the country back toward negotiations with the Palestinians. He argued Switzerland so far does "not nearly enough." [SRF, 21 Aug 2026](https://www.srf.ch/news/international/schweiz-und-israel-israelische-persoenlichkeiten-fordern-siedlungsboykott-der-schweiz)
  - Why it matters: The letter lands as Switzerland faces the same question after seven Western governments condemned Israel's E1 settlement plan on 20 August, and it is notable precisely because it comes from Israelis — framing a halt to settlement trade as compliance with international law rather than as hostility to Israel, and putting direct pressure on Parmelin's economics department, which oversees Swiss trade policy.
