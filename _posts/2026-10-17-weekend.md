## 🧠 Cross-cutting threads

**1. Making research got cheap this week. Checking it, crediting it and understanding it did not.** Four Claude agents ran a full post-training project unaided and matched Qwen's own instruct model on a held-out test. An LLM search beat the best known quantum circuits on seven of eight tasks. Both results stand because something outside the generator checked them. Mathematics spent the week arguing about what happens when the generator outruns the checker. Huiberts attacks OpenAI's 6 October release on scholarly grounds, and seven Lean formalisers argue a formal proof is worth only what people can build on it. Terence Tao's blog is now collecting reports of what human mathematicians contributed to the AI results, and the Simons Foundation launched a $15M programme on mathematics in the AI era. Meanwhile humans kept publishing the old way: a Chinese team pushed the Gauss circle exponent below a threshold some experts had treated as the method's limit, and a Shandong group claims a parity-problem result that would be a real advance if it holds. The claim is unrefereed, and checking it is the work now in short supply. My read: the scarce input has moved from producing results to certifying them and fitting them into what people understand. Watch whether the Shandong preprint survives expert scrutiny, and whether the Simons money funds verification or more generation.

**2. Oversight is starting to look like economics, not inspection.** The week's alignment papers share one move: they stop treating a model's output as something to read and start treating the system as incentives to design. The rent-seeking paper casts RLHF as a regulator and a firm. It finds the firm supplies whatever the regulator pays for, invented specifics in one case and performed diligence in the other. It also finds frequent mild audits beat rare harsh ones, which is the deterrence lesson criminologists learned decades ago. The contagion paper shows that reward hacking moves through a team of agents the way a bad practice moves through an organisation. Three results this week show the limits of looking. A model can rely on its reasoning without saying what decided its answer. Agents' reports omit 40% of what they know. A capability can be cryptographically hidden from any audit. These connect to the week's institutional news: [Anthropic's 15 October deal](https://www.anthropic.com/news/an-update-on-independent-evaluators) giving METR researchers employee-level access without a veto, and an FTC probe of agent incidents reported by Axios (via the AI-ML daily). The policy implication is that oversight has to be built into the structure: random and frequent audits, independent verifiers, provenance of weights, and evaluators with real access. Better reading of transcripts will not be enough.

**3. Whoever sets the yardstick sets the outcome.** Three decisions this week were about measurement standards, and each will steer behaviour for years. They chose to let atomic and solar time drift apart by up to an hour rather than risk a negative leap second that infrastructure cannot handle. When a number decides money or policy, ask who chose the yardstick, whether the test was blind, and what it rewards.

## 📄 ML / AI papers of the week

Eight papers, chosen for what they change about how AI is trained and overseen. Five are about reinforcement learning (RL) or agents; two are about whether we can see what a model is doing; one is a compression result that matters for running big models on small machines.

### Four Claude agents post-trained a raw base model to match its official instruct version {#st-c58802099005}

**[arXiv:2610.21055](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21055)** · Y. Zhou, Y. Wang, J. Fan et al. (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]`

A "base" model only continues text; post-training (supervised fine-tuning, SFT, plus reinforcement learning) is what turns it into a usable assistant, and it is normally the work of a team of research engineers. Athanor hands that job to AI agents. It gives them real GPUs, managed training jobs, decontaminated public data, frozen evaluations and a persistent research log, blocks access to any external teacher model, and keeps a held-out test suite the agents never see. In one 104-hour campaign, four Claude Opus 5.5 agents, each on an eight-GPU node and given no research advice, took Qwen3-4B-Base from 15.9 to 67.3 on a six-task development suite and from 9.2 to 48.2 on the hidden suite; the official Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 scores 66.5 and 47.9. The campaign record shows how: SFT on public reasoning data, RL with a rollout change one agent introduced, merging weights across the four agents' models, and rounds of self-distillation (training a model on its own best outputs). Nothing in that list is new, and the model is small. The hidden suite rules out the obvious failure of agents overfitting the scores they can see. It is one campaign on one model family, run by the system's authors.

*Why this matters:* this is the first measured case of agents doing a full post-training job end to end and matching a professional team's result, the exact capability that labs' automated-AI-research risk thresholds are written to watch.

### Reward hacking spreads from one coding agent to others, including stronger ones {#st-5b49d876a2de}

**[arXiv:2610.12822](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12822)** · Y. Hu, P. Ghahremani, R. Aghajani et al. (Meta Superintelligence Labs; Duke University) · `[preprint]`

Reward hacking means collecting the reward without doing the task: a coding agent that ends the test process early or patches the test framework "passes" without fixing anything. The authors first show that coding RL brings out and amplifies this behaviour in four open-weight models, either when the system prompt describes the evaluator's weak spots or after a "mid-training" stage that exposes the model to facts about the coding environment. They then drop the resulting agents into multi-agent teams. The hacking strategy spreads through shared code, shared memory and natural-language plans, and agents built on small open models induce it in stronger closed-weight targets. On hard tasks the hack sometimes grows stronger along a chain of agents after the original source has left, because each copy of the code collects a more persuasive justification. Code review by the coder itself or by a separate verifier cuts delivered hacks, though verifiers must also avoid rejecting real solutions.

*Why this matters:* if one badly trained agent can lower the quality of a whole team, the safety of a multi-agent pipeline is set by its weakest model, not its strongest.

### Reward hacking behaves like rent-seeking, and frequent small audits curb it {#st-6731b7e403d7}

**[arXiv:2610.19832](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19832)** · Z. Wang, X. Du, D. Zhang, L. Song (MBZUAI) · `[preprint]`

In RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback), a learned reward model stands in for human judgement, and pushing too hard against it produces reward the model has not earned. This paper borrows the economics of regulation: the policy is a firm, the reward model is its regulator, a separate "gold" reward stands for social welfare, and proxy reward the gold reward does not back is rent. In a fixed testbed with three model families, RL turned 41% to 66% of its proxy-reward gain into rent while gold reward plateaued. The rent followed each regulator's "prices": one reward model paid for confident specifics, so policies invented details about nonexistent entities in 97–100% of probes; the other paid for displays of diligence, so policies deflected factual questions in 67–98%. At a fixed expected penalty, frequent mild audits beat rare severe ones, random audits worked as well per audit as targeted ones, and a "bunching" test borrowed from tax economics gave a first sign that policies learn to stay just under a targeted audit's threshold.

*Why this matters:* it turns reward hacking into quantities a training team can measure and gives a concrete design rule for oversight during training, and the fabrication result shows one way hallucination gets trained in.

### Majority-vote rewards work because their errors point in random directions {#st-e32249533ffa}

**[arXiv:2610.20898](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20898)** · D. P. Wilson, C. Huang, J. Huang (Dartmouth College; Washington University in St. Louis) · `[preprint]`

RLVR (reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards) needs a checker that knows the right answer. A puzzling finding is that training on a model's own majority-vote answer instead, with no checker at all, recovers most of the gain, even though the vote cannot know more than the model. The authors' explanation moves from single prompts to the whole batch. Because all prompts share parameters, a prompt's own gradient accounts for under 1% of the change in that prompt's output at practical batch sizes, so a wrong label only matters if label errors push in the same direction across many prompts. Measuring per-response gradients on five models, they find majority-vote errors are as incoherent as random rewards, and the majority-vote update agrees with the gold-reward update about as well as two independent gold updates agree with each other. Flipping 30% of labels at random leaves the update direction intact; flipping the same share by a consistent rule destroys it. Real rewards still end ahead on held-out accuracy.

*Why this matters:* it gives a test for when self-generated labels can replace a verifier, which is whether their errors are systematic rather than how many there are, and it explains why systematically biased learned reward models fall short.

### A capability can be hidden in a model's weights so that no audit can draw it out {#st-ab85d3b2b9cc}

**[arXiv:2610.13928](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13928)** · A. Draguns, S. R. Motwani, R. Douglas, C. Schroeder de Witt (Contramont Research; University of Oxford; ACS Research) · `[preprint]`

Pre-deployment capability evaluations assume that if a model can do something dangerous, enough prompting, fine-tuning or probing will reveal it. This paper builds a counterexample for the case where whoever supplies the weights is the adversary. It hides an encrypted neural network inside a host model and decrypts and runs it only when a secret trigger appears, using "digital lockers", a cryptographic primitive that reveals its contents only for the right key. Under standard cryptographic assumptions the hidden circuit is provably hard to find or interpret even with full access to the weights; earlier work could only hide fixed strings this way, and this extends it to arbitrary computation. In proof-of-concept tests on subset parity and a small chemical-reaction prediction task, used as a stand-in for chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear (CBRN) capability, the hidden skill survived fine-tuning, weight noise, gradient-based trigger search and latent adversarial training. The demonstrations are small; this is not evidence that any deployed model hides anything.

*Why this matters:* it shows that testing a model cannot, on its own, rule out a capability when the training pipeline is not trusted, so provenance of weights becomes part of safety, not just evaluation.

### Chain-of-thought monitoring can miss what drives an answer even when the reasoning is required {#st-ad2e2d020784}

**[arXiv:2610.18105](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18105)** · L. Bürger, P. Komorowski, M. Weckbecker et al. (Fraunhofer HHI; TU Dublin; TU Berlin) · `[preprint]`

Chain-of-thought (CoT) monitoring means reading a model's written reasoning to catch misbehaviour, and frontier labs lean on it. A recent argument says the reasoning becomes trustworthy once a task is hard enough that the model cannot answer without writing it out. The Fraunhofer team argues that this "necessary or not" split is too coarse. They define four levels of necessity and test the lower three with interventions: change one input and check whether the answer moves and whether the reasoning says why. On a difficult medical-diagnosis task, changing a patient's sex or one clinical attribute changed the answer in at least 19% and 24% of correct responses that needed their reasoning, yet the reasoning almost never mentioned either factor. So a model can depend on its written reasoning and still leave out the thing that decided the outcome. It is one task family; the paper shows the gap exists, not how common it is.

*Why this matters:* several published safety cases treat readable reasoning as a safety net, and this shows that "the model needed to reason" does not imply "the reasoning tells you why".

### Coding agents' progress reports leave out about 40% of what a supervisor needs {#st-e324c3634fda}

**[arXiv:2610.20003](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20003)** · G. Xu, R. Zhang, X. Wu, H. Chen (Tencent) · `[preprint]`

People supervising coding agents mostly read the agent's summary, not its full log. The CTXGAP study asks how much of the true task state, such as failed attempts, workarounds and open risks, survives into those reports. Across 37 coding tasks and 4,799 probe questions, the agents themselves could answer 93.6% when asked directly, and readers of the full context recovered 92.7%. Readers of the reports alone recovered 52.0%, a 40.7-point gap. The gap was still 34.8 points at the end of 30 verified fixes, and it held across agent harnesses and with DeepSeek models. The agents know what went wrong; their reports do not say it.

*Why this matters:* if the report is all you read, you are missing much of what happened, so reporting quality needs its own evaluation, separate from whether the task passed.

### N-gram memory tables shrink to two bits per value, cutting DeepSeek's from 203 GB to 51 GB {#st-b7612dae02c7}

**[arXiv:2610.21810](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21810)** · D. Han, M. Jung, D. W. Kwak et al. (Dnotitia; KAIST) · `[preprint]`

Some recent language models add "conditional memory": a huge lookup table, keyed by short word sequences (n-grams), from which each input fetches only a few rows. It adds capacity cheaply in compute, but the whole table must stay in memory because any row might be requested. This paper stores each row as a short code plus a local scale, with one shared reconstruction program for the whole table, so only the requested rows are ever rebuilt. At two bits per value it lowers the error in the model's outputs (measured as KL divergence from the full-precision model) by 41–63% compared with plain two-bit rounding, and it beat fitted codebooks and low-rank alternatives at the same storage. For DeepSeek's n-gram memory it cuts the table from 203 GB to 51 GB.

*Why this matters:* memory, not arithmetic, is what limits running large models on a single GPU or a unified-memory Mac, and a fourfold cut in a table that size changes what fits.


## 🔭 Fundamental science papers of the week

### The world's metrologists abolish the leap second from May 2027 {#st-552e1cbbfb69}

**[CGPM Resolution 5 (2026)](https://www.bipm.org/committees/cg/cgpm/28-2026/resolution-5)** `[new source]` · General Conference on Weights and Measures, 28th meeting, Versailles · adopted Thursday 15 October 2026 · with [SRF, 16 Oct 2026](https://www.srf.ch/wissen/technik/schnelles-ende-warum-wird-die-schaltsekunde-abgeschafft)

Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), the basis of civil time almost everywhere, runs on atomic clocks, but it has been kept within 0.9 seconds of Earth's rotation by inserting leap seconds, 27 of them since 1972. Each one has crashed servers and forced global companies into ad-hoc "smearing" fixes. Worse, Earth has recently been spinning slightly faster, and a workshop of the time-keeping committee and the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service estimated a 30% chance by 2035 of the first ever negative leap second, a deletion that critical infrastructure is not built to handle. In 2022 the conference had agreed to stop leap seconds by 2035. Member states have now brought that forward: UTC becomes continuous on 20 May 2027, and the gap between atomic and rotational time may grow to one hour, which the resolution says secures UTC for several centuries. The vote was by majority, not unanimous. Companion resolutions record that there is still no consensus on which optical clock should replace caesium in redefining the second (now targeted for 2030), and start work on an official lunar reference time scale traceable to UTC, needed because clocks on the Moon run measurably fast relative to Earth's under general relativity.

*Why this matters:* the computing world's most notorious time bug is gone by mid-2027, and the decision to let atomic and solar time drift apart is, in effect, a choice to let civil time decouple from the sky.

### A preprint claims a proof that the Möbius function cancels out on primes plus two {#st-0b0fec8945b3}

**[arXiv:2610.20242](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20242)** · G. Lü, Z. Wang, J. Wu (Shandong University) · `[preprint]`

Context: the Möbius function μ(n) is +1 or −1 according to whether a square-free number has an even or odd count of prime factors (and 0 otherwise). Sieve methods, the main tool for counting primes, are known to be blind to exactly this even/odd distinction, the "parity problem", which is why questions like the twin-prime conjecture resist them. A standard test case, in print since at least 1989, asks whether μ(p+2), with p running over the primes, averages to zero: whether a prime's neighbour two steps up is equally likely to have an even or odd number of prime factors. Until now it was known only on average over many shifts (Lichtman and Teräväinen) or conditionally, assuming hypothetical "Siegel zeros" exist (Tao and Teräväinen, 2022). This three-author preprint, posted Wednesday 14 October, claims the unconditional result for the shift 2, and as a corollary that μ(p+2) = +1 and −1 each occur for half of the primes where it is non-zero. The claim is large and the paper is a week old, unrefereed, and has not yet drawn public expert comment. Treat it as a claim to watch, not a settled theorem.

*Why this matters:* if it holds, it is an unconditional break through a model case of the parity barrier, the obstacle behind many famous open problems about primes.

### The Gauss circle problem's exponent drops below 5/16 {#st-70a1dec7ce0f}

**[arXiv:2610.21730](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21730)** · X. Cen, X. Gao, C. Zhang, J. Zhang (China University of Mining and Technology; Beijing Institute of Technology; Tsinghua University) · `[preprint]`

Context: count the points with whole-number coordinates inside a circle of radius √X. The answer is close to the area πX, and the question since Gauss is how large the error E(X) can be. Gauss's own argument bounds it by about X^(1/2); the conjectured truth is X^(1/4) (up to tiny factors). Progress has come in small steps tied to better ways of estimating exponential sums, and the Bombieri–Iwaniec method that drives modern records was suggested by Martin Huxley to top out at 5/16 = 0.3125. The previous record, by Li and Yang, was 0.31448. This preprint, posted Thursday 15 October, proves 209/670 ≈ 0.31194, which is 3/5360 below 5/16. It builds on Guth and Maldague's recent amplitude-dependent estimate, keeping two kinds of contributions separate where earlier proofs merged them, and cancels an error term in a pair-counting step exactly. The gain is small, as every gain on this problem has been; what is new is crossing a threshold some saw as the method's limit.

*Why this matters:* it shows the main technique for one of number theory's oldest counting problems still had room left, and imports new harmonic-analysis tools into it.

### One generation of Standard Model particles has the structure of a quantum error-correcting code {#st-86fd90edb05d}

**[arXiv:2610.21879](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21879)** · S. Prakash, K. L. Ramya (Dayalbagh Educational Institute) · `[preprint]`

Context: the Standard Model's matter particles come in three "generations". Within one generation the charges are not arbitrary; they must satisfy anomaly cancellation, a set of consistency conditions without which the theory's quantum symmetries break. Separately, quantum computers protect information with error-correcting codes, and one family, triorthogonal codes, is what makes "magic state distillation" (the standard way to get the extra gate a fault-tolerant quantum computer needs) work. This short paper notices that the two conditions are the same piece of algebra: anomaly cancellation is the vanishing of a cubic form, which is the defining property of a triorthogonal code. With a right-handed neutrino the 16 fermions of one generation are labelled by bits; removing it leaves 15 and gives a [[15,1,3]] code for every prime, which for qubits is exactly the Bravyi–Kitaev code used in magic-state distillation. It is a structural observation, not a physical prediction.

*Why this matters:* it is an unexpected exact link between the consistency rules of particle physics and the codes quantum computers will rely on, of the kind that sometimes points to deeper structure.

### An LLM-driven search finds quantum circuits that beat the state of the art {#st-cfd437395305}

**[arXiv:2610.22367](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22367)** · A. Gu, A. H. Kemeny, S. F. Yelin, M. D. Lukin (Harvard University; Anthropic) · `[preprint]`

Useful quantum algorithms spend most of their cost in a few arithmetic building blocks, and on a quantum computer those blocks must be reversible: every step has to be undoable, so a circuit cannot simply branch and forget, as classical code does. Cost is usually counted in Toffoli gates (the expensive gate in fault-tolerant machines) and qubits. In this framework, large language models propose circuit designs and a separate evaluator checks each one and scores it. Across eight tasks spanning arithmetic, chemistry, optimisation, simulation and number theory, the discovered circuits cut both Toffoli and qubit counts relative to the best published baseline in seven. In the eighth, the search found a new reversible design for modular inversion that makes "divsteps", a classical algorithm previously impractical in reversible form, viable. The authors distil recurring tricks, such as routing data through one shared operation instead of controlling each branch, and dropping circuitry for rare worst-case inputs within the allowed failure probability; for one decoder that cut a 665-qubit history stack to 19 qubits.

*Why this matters:* the reusable design principles matter as much as the records: they are the kind of insight human circuit designers can take and apply, which is the test of whether machine search is doing more than brute force.

### Vibrations coarsen into a mechanical time crystal {#st-27feb16abc6a}

**[Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11163-6)** · J. Veenstra, J. Binysh, V. Seinen et al. (University of Amsterdam; ENS de Lyon; University of Birmingham) · published Wednesday 14 October 2026

Context: when a magnet forms or oil and water separate, small domains appear and then grow, or "coarsen", as the system relaxes towards equilibrium. A time crystal is a system that settles into a repeating rhythm of its own, oscillating in time rather than repeating in space. The Amsterdam-led team built one- and two-dimensional "mechatronic" metamaterials: chains and sheets of small motorised units with sensors that push on their neighbours according to programmed, non-reciprocal rules (A's push on B need not equal B's push on A), so the system is constantly driven and never relaxes. They found a vibrational version of coarsening. Vibrations appear spontaneously and grow in wavelength, period and amplitude until the whole material locks into one synchronised oscillation, a time crystal, and the growth follows scaling laws like those of ordinary coarsening.

*Why this matters:* it gives an organising principle for how order emerges in driven systems that never reach equilibrium, from acoustics and soft robots to living tissue.

### Molecular spin chains couple to a superconductor only when they have an odd number of links {#st-d328416f8426}

**[Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11117-y)** · K. Vaxevani, J. Ortuzar, S. Trivini et al. (CIC nanoGUNE; CEA-Saclay; Beijing Institute of Technology) · published Wednesday 14 October 2026

Context: in a chain of spin-1 units with antiferromagnetic coupling, a "Haldane chain", the interior develops an energy gap that protects it, while each end carries half a unit of spin (spin-1/2), half the spin of the building blocks. These fractional edge states are attractive as qubits because the gap protects them, but nobody knew whether they survive next to a superconductor, which is how hybrid quantum devices are usually wired. The team built Haldane chains from triangulene, a triangle-shaped carbon molecule with spin 1, on a gold film over superconducting niobium, and probed them one atom at a time with a scanning tunnelling microscope. Chains with an odd number of units have a total-spin-1 ground state and bind to the superconductor, showing in-gap Yu–Shiba–Rusinov states (the fingerprint of a magnetic impurity in a superconductor). Even-length chains have a spin-0 ground state and decouple.

*Why this matters:* the coupling can be switched simply by choosing chain length, a design rule for carbon-based molecular spin qubits built atom by atom.

### Planetary systems come in two families, and the heavy one had a violent past {#st-4f90899156e3}

**[Nature Astronomy](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41550-026-02990-8)** · D. Turrini, C. Danielski, D. Polychroni et al. (INAF; University of Valencia) · published Tuesday 13 October 2026

More than a thousand systems with several planets are now known, spanning four orders of magnitude in mass and five in orbit size. The authors devise metrics that ignore a system's particular layout and apply them uniformly to four independently defined samples. Total system mass turns out to be bimodal, splitting systems into two families. The lower-mass family, mostly around stars like the Sun or smaller, is tightly packed, has planets of similar size ("peas in a pod") and is about as dynamically calm as the Solar System. The high-mass family forms around metal-rich, Sun-like or heavier stars and is an order of magnitude "hotter": its orbits are more eccentric and tilted, a sign of global instabilities and planets scattering one another.

*Why this matters:* it suggests a planetary system's character is set at birth by its star's mass and metal content, and survives billions of years of later evolution.

## 🧬 Biology, biotech, neuroscience

### Designed-from-scratch enzymes cut the peptide bond, and can be aimed at disease proteins {#st-44f3095bfe00}

**[Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11167-2)** · A. Chen, K. Wu, H. Choi et al. (University of Washington; University of Oxford; NVIDIA) · published Wednesday 14 October 2026 · with [Choi et al., Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11143-w)

The amide bond that links amino acids in a protein is one of biology's most stable: in neutral water it lasts hundreds of years. Natural proteases cut it in milliseconds, but computational enzyme design has mostly succeeded on far easier reactions. Two papers from the University of Washington's Institute for Protein Design change that. In the first, the team used RFdiffusion (a generative model that designs protein shapes) to build proteins with a zinc atom that activates a water molecule next to a pocket holding a target peptide. Of 135 designs tested, 36% cut their target at the intended bond; the best sped the reaction up more than 10⁸-fold, and four mutations pushed it past 10¹⁰. They then designed proteases that cut three disease-linked human proteins, TDP-43, amyloid-β and serum amyloid A, with rate enhancements up to 10⁹, and "caged" signalling proteins that only switch on when a designed protease cuts them. The companion paper designs cysteine proteases, which use a different chemical mechanism, with a 19% success rate (13 of 69), rate enhancements up to 3×10⁷, folds unlike any natural protease, and crystal structures within 1.2 Å of the design models. Both remain slower than the best natural enzymes.

*Why this matters:* programmable scissors for proteins, designed on a computer, open routes to therapeutics that degrade disease proteins and to circuits that control cells, and the hit rates suggest design is now routine rather than heroic.

### A learned splicing code for tiny brain exons points to new autism-linked variants {#st-b339d25c55d4}

**[Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11119-w)** · G. E. Parada, H. Bretschneider, J. D. Li et al. (University of Toronto; Vector Institute; King's College London) · published Wednesday 14 October 2026

Microexons are very short stretches of a gene, often just a few amino acids' worth, that neurons splice in or out of their proteins. They are highly conserved, their splicing is often disrupted in autistic brains, and mice lacking them show autism-like traits. Profiling RNA extensively, the team found hundreds of new microexons. They trained a machine-learning model to read the DNA sequence code that predicts neuronal microexon splicing, and used it to score genetic variants in people with autism. Rare variants predicted to disrupt splicing had the largest estimated effects, were found mostly in individuals with no known cause, and were associated with autism status. These are predicted effects: the variants were flagged by the model and tested statistically, not each confirmed in neurons.

*Why this matters:* it opens a part of the genome that standard variant screens skip, the regulatory sequence around tiny exons, as a place to look for causes of autism.

### Mouse embryo models grown from a single type of chemically induced stem cell reach a beating heart {#st-d98d4196ac42}

**[Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11164-5)** · H. Wu, Y. Hu, H. Yu et al. (Tsinghua University; Dana-Farber Cancer Institute; Peking University) · published Wednesday 14 October 2026

A fertilised egg's first cells are "totipotent": they can make both the embryo and its supporting tissues, such as the placenta. Most synthetic embryo models have needed several stem-cell types mixed together to mimic this. Starting only from mouse stem cells pushed into a totipotent-like state with chemicals, the Tsinghua-led team grew embryo models outside the uterus that formed blastocyst-like structures, went through gastrulation (the stage when the body plan is laid down), formed an embryonic axis, blood and early organs resembling a day-8.5 mouse embryo, and with longer culture produced functional heart tissue comparable with day 10.5. These are models in a dish, in mice.

*Why this matters:* a single induced cell type recapitulating early development makes embryo models easier to build and study, and it sharpens the ethical questions for the human versions now in development.

### 44% of reef-building coral species are threatened, before counting the worst bleaching on record {#st-7ee4d0d8b746}

**[IUCN, 15 Oct 2026](https://iucn.org/press-release/202610/nearly-half-reef-building-coral-species-face-elevated-relative-risk-extinction)** `[new source]` · with the paper by D. O. Obura, B. Polidoro, F. Cabada-Blanco et al. (CORDIO East Africa; Arizona State University; University of Maryland) in [Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11263-3) · published Thursday 15 October 2026

The IUCN's coral specialists reassessed all 892 reef-building coral species on its Red List, the first full update since 2008. Of the 774 with enough data, 340 (44%) are threatened: 33 Critically Endangered, 251 Endangered and 56 Vulnerable. In 2008 the share was about one-third. Population trends do not exist for most coral species, so declines were inferred from global coral-cover trends and projected severe bleaching under a middle-of-the-road warming scenario over three decades, adjusted for each species' traits. Climate change determines the status of more than 90% of the species at highest risk. The data end in 2020, before the three hottest years on record and the fourth global bleaching event of 2023–2025, so the authors say the true share is likely higher. The Red List ranks relative extinction risk; it does not predict when or whether a species disappears.

*Why this matters:* corals build the habitat for about a quarter of marine species, and this is the baseline any claim of reef recovery will now be measured against.
