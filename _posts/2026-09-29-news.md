## 🇨🇭 Switzerland & Vaud

- <a id="st-5245032e1b68" class="st-a"></a>**The Swiss Financial Market Supervisory Authority (FINMA) concluded an enforcement proceeding against Julius Bär on Tuesday 29 September, finding "serious violations" of supervisory law and ordering the bank to hold CHF 250 million in additional capital while confiscating about CHF 10 million in profits.** The case combined two strands: private-debt loans — lending secured not by traditional collateral but by borrowers' unlisted shares — that FINMA says the Zurich private bank was neither organisationally nor operationally equipped to run, and client relationships tied to two Russian PEPs (politically exposed persons — senior figures who carry a higher money-laundering risk). From September 2019 the bank granted eight loans to a single European group and its founder, with the total exceeding CHF 1 billion in 2022–2023 and an outstanding exposure of CHF 586 million at end-2022; Swiss press (Le Temps, SRF) identify the borrower as the collapsed Signa real-estate empire of Austrian magnate René Benko. It is FINMA's fifth concluded proceeding against Julius Bär in under ten years. Dividends now require FINMA's prior approval; the regulator has also opened separate proceedings against three unidentified former employees, and the ruling is not yet legally binding (the bank can appeal). Why it matters: a CHF 250 million capital add-on and a fifth enforcement action in a decade point to a repeated, structural failure of risk culture at one of Switzerland's largest wealth managers — the kind of governance lapse that, in the Credit Suisse case, ended in collapse. [FINMA press release, 29.09.2026 (EN) [new source]](https://www.finma.ch/en/news/2026/09/20260929-mm-enforcementverfahren/); [Le Temps, 29.09.2026 (FR)](https://www.letemps.ch/articles/affaire-signa-la-finma-clot-sa-procedure-contre-julius-baer-en-exigeant-250-millions-de-fonds-propres-supplementaires-et-en-confisquant-10-millions-de-francs)

- <a id="st-d616faf541c3" class="st-a"></a>**The National Council (Nationalrat, the large chamber of parliament) voted on Tuesday 29 September, by 157 to 27 with 8 abstentions, to advance a proposal capping the pay of basic health-insurers' executives.** The measure — a parliamentary initiative from Baptiste Hurni (SP, Neuchâtel) — would tie the maximum salary of an insurer's management and board, for their work on mandatory basic insurance, to the top pay grade of the federal administration, adjusted for inflation and scaled to each insurer's insured population and average per-head costs. Insurers would also have to publish each named executive's compensation and workload, listing individual members by name. The proposal now goes to the Council of States. Why it matters: it targets pay at the top of a system financed by compulsory premiums, and the vote lands as households brace for the annual mandatory-insurance premium increase for 2027, due to be announced this week — though a cap on executive pay would do little to move overall premium costs. - <a id="st-69bd31ddfdc1" class="st-a"></a>**South Korea's defence minister on Tuesday 29 September blamed North Korea for a landmine explosion inside the Demilitarized Zone (DMZ, the buffer strip dividing the peninsula), calling it a "clear violation" of the 1953 armistice.** Three South Korean officers were wounded when two mines detonated on 21 September as troops cleared ground ahead of a joint operation with the UN Command; one soldier lost a foot, and a third, unexploded mine was found as the others withdrew.
