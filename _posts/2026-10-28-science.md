### The last Laurentide ice cap started a new glaciation, then warming ended it {#st-1dac89e6c289}
**[Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aed3379)** · Young, Miller et al. (Lamont-Doherty Earth Observatory; CU Boulder; University of Alaska Fairbanks) · published Thursday 22 October 2026 · `[single-source]`
The Laurentide Ice Sheet, the largest of the ice-age ice sheets, once covered most of North America. Its last surviving fragment is Barnes Ice Cap on Baffin Island in Arctic Canada. Dating the ice cap's past margins, the team finds it shrank to its smallest size some time after about 3,600 years ago and then began growing again around 2,000 years ago. In glacial terms, that regrowth is the start of the next glaciation, driven by the slow decline in summer sunlight that follows Earth's orbital cycles. The regrowth peaked around 1960 CE. Since then human-driven summer warming has pushed the cap into net mass loss, and the margins are retreating. The authors call this "the termination of the next glaciation", even though orbital forcing is still pushing towards cold. This rests on one ice cap and one paper; the abstract was read via Crossref metadata because the Science page did not load.
*Why it matters:* This is direct field evidence that greenhouse warming has already overridden the orbital cooling trend that would otherwise be rebuilding an ice sheet. The question stops being a model projection and becomes something observed in the ice.

### Chemists isolate oxoboranes, molecules with a boron–oxygen triple bond, after 60 years of trying {#st-7563d5ad038a}
**[Nature Chemistry](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41557-026-02272-w)** · Lubczyk, Markl et al. (University of Würzburg; Tezpur University) · published Tuesday 27 October 2026; companion paper **[Nature Chemistry](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41557-026-02273-9)** · Zou, Yang et al. (Sichuan University; Liaocheng University; NTU Singapore)
Boron is electron-poor and does not readily form triple bonds, so oxoboranes (R–B≡O: a two-connected boron triple-bonded to a lone oxygen) had only been seen in the gas phase, trapped in frozen inert-gas matrices, inferred from reactions, or made as derivatives with weaker bonds. The Würzburg group made three of them by a "remarkably simple" route, and they are stable as crystals at room temperature. Their B–O bonds measure 1.208–1.217 Å, which is exceptionally short and strong. Working independently, a Sichuan-led team used carbon monoxide to trigger a rearrangement and isolated a free oxoborane. X-ray diffraction shows its terminal B≡O unit, and calculations find two perpendicular π bonds, the signature of a real triple bond, though a strongly polarised one. The Sichuan compound reacts with both electron donors and electron acceptors, and the team used that to make neutral and cationic derivatives. Würzburg's [press release](https://www.uni-wuerzburg.de/en/news-and-events/news/detail/news/oxoboranes/) `[new source]` traces attempts back to at least the early 1960s.
*Why it matters:* Two independent groups reaching the same "unisolable" class of molecule in the same week makes the result unusually solid. Main-group chemists now have a new bond type they can bottle and study.

### Designed proteins turn off-the-shelf photocatalysts into mirror-selective enzymes {#st-9e16fc4cf721}
**[Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11317-6)** · Mishra, Payal et al. (Indiana University; University of Washington; HHMI) · published Wednesday 21 October 2026
Metal polypyridine complexes, which are ruthenium- or iridium-based light absorbers, drive much of modern visible-light chemistry. They cannot, however, choose between the left- and right-handed (enantiomeric) forms of a product, and drug makers usually need only one. The team used generative protein design to build new protein scaffolds that bind these photocatalysts. Directed evolution, rounds of mutation and selection, then turned the protein–catalyst hybrids into highly enantioselective light-driven catalysts. The evolved proteins handle several reaction types, run for many turnovers, can be recycled, and work even with cheap commercial catalyst samples that are a 50/50 mix of both handed forms. Spectroscopy shows the protein lengthens the catalyst's excited-state lifetime and holds the substrate in place, and that positioning is what sets the handedness.
*Why it matters:* Asymmetric synthesis has relied on finding the right chiral catalyst. This shows a designed protein can impose handedness on a simple metal complex that no natural enzyme was built to hold.

### A single positron is trapped for months as a "quantum cyclotron" {#st-cf590d4ebebe}
**[Physical Review Letters](https://journals.aps.org/prl/abstract/10.1103/gbkc-6nlj)** · Myers, Soucy et al. (Northwestern University) · published Wednesday 21 October 2026
Gerald Gabrielse's group captured one positron (the electron's antimatter twin) from radioactive decay and suspended it in a Penning trap, a container made only of electric and magnetic fields, so it never touches matter and annihilates. In a trap cooled to about 300 millikelvin, the positron's circular motion in the magnetic field falls into discrete quantum levels that can be controlled and read out. This is a "quantum cyclotron", which [Northwestern](https://news.northwestern.edu/stories/2026/10/scientists-trap-and-tame-a-single-particle-of-antimatter-for-months) `[new source]` likens to an artificial atom. The positron has stayed trapped for months. The same technique gave the group its record measurement of the electron's magnetic moment. The paper itself is a set-up result: the precise positron measurement has not been made yet.
*Why it matters:* The Standard Model requires the electron and positron to have magnetic moments equal in size. CPT symmetry is the rule that predicts this: flip charge, mirror image and time direction together and the laws stay the same. Comparing the two moments at quantum-cyclotron precision would be the strictest test yet of that rule for leptons.

### A trapped ion corrects its own errors without being measured, and beats break-even {#st-7f9b9b46dd9a}
**[Nature Physics](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41567-026-03486-y)** · Li, Mei et al. (USTC; Hefei National Laboratory) · published Friday 23 October 2026
Standard quantum error correction repeatedly measures error "syndromes", applies fixes in real time and uses many physical qubits. This experiment does none of those. A logical qubit is stored in several internal spin levels of one trapped ion. Engineered couplings move the disorder each error creates (its entropy) into the ion's vibration, and a second "ancilla" ion cools that vibration away. Errors are dissipated, not detected. Repeating the cycle extended the logical qubit's lifetime past break-even, the point where the protected qubit outlives the best unprotected one. It held both in ordinary lab conditions and under added magnetic noise. The abstract gives no improvement factor. "Break-even" is a threshold, not a margin, and this is one protected qubit, not a computation.
*Why it matters:* Measurement and fast feedback are among the most expensive parts of quantum error correction. A route that skips them could make robust quantum memories much cheaper to build.

### A helical molecule spins one way under electric current, and spinning makes it conduct better {#st-4183fcc2094b}
**[Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11312-x)** · Singh, Martin et al. (Weizmann Institute; IIT Madras; University of Augsburg) · published Thursday 22 October 2026
The idea borrows from the Archimedes screw. A helicene, a corkscrew-shaped molecule, was suspended between two electrode tips, and an applied voltage set it cycling with 100% directional preference. Reversing the voltage's polarity reverses the direction. Above a threshold voltage the current through the molecule rises sharply, and stretching or compressing the helix shifts that threshold. Calculations attribute both effects to current-induced torque. The rotation moves from stepwise to continuous, and that lets the molecule pass through highly conducting shapes it cannot reach while at rest. Earlier helical-molecule junctions showed neither effect, which the authors put down to differences in structure and operation.
*Why it matters:* This is a prototype molecular motor driven directly by electric current, with perfect one-way rotation and a readout built into its own conductance. Both are prerequisites for molecular machines that a circuit could drive.

### NMR on a single atom, done with a microscope tip {#st-8057b5d868bf}
**[Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-78801-5)** · Vennema, Mier et al. (TU Delft; CIC nanoGUNE) · published Saturday 24 October 2026
Nuclear spins are well shielded from their surroundings, so they hold quantum information for a long time. The same shielding makes them hard to address. Sander Otte's group used a scanning tunnelling microscope, which images and probes single atoms on a surface, to run nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) on one titanium-47 atom with nuclear spin 5/2. The method is electron–nuclear double resonance: it drives the atom's electron spin and its nuclear spin together. The team resolved the individual nuclear-spin transitions, characterised how the nucleus couples to its electron (the hyperfine and quadrupole interactions), and addressed the nuclear spin even when that coupling was weak. The weak-coupling regime is the one needed for long-lived nuclear-spin storage.
*Why it matters:* Nuclear spins can now be addressed one atom at a time on a surface that can be assembled atom by atom. That makes them candidate building blocks for quantum memories made to order.

### ETH Zürich implant burns off excess blood sugar with electricity, not insulin {#st-0ecec5c6058e}
**[Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.ady8560)** · Maity, Guha Ray et al. (ETH Zürich; University of Lyon) · published Thursday 22 October 2026
Martin Fussenegger's group in Basel built ECHO, a wirelessly powered implant. Its catalyst is graphite felt studded with gold nanoclusters dusted with platinum, and it oxidises blood glucose into gluconate, which the kidneys excrete. The implant has no cells, enzymes or genetic circuits. A commercial continuous glucose monitor triggers it through a smartphone when sugar runs high and switches it off when levels fall. In diabetic mice, activation at 0.75 V and 10 mA restored normal glucose within an hour. It shifted metabolism towards burning fat, cut body weight and triglycerides, and caused no detectable hypoglycaemia or tissue damage over six weeks ([ETH Zürich](https://ethz.ch/en/news-and-events/eth-news/news/2026/10/tackling-diabetes-with-an-electronic-implant.html)). All of this is mouse data. No human has been tested.
*Why it matters:* This is a Swiss-built, drug-free approach to type 2 diabetes. It removes surplus glucose outright instead of pushing it into fat stores, as insulin does. The open questions are whether it scales to human glucose loads and what long-term implant safety looks like.

### "Recordanelles" let a cell archive its own RNA for days {#st-fe2d53a034ea}
**[Nature Biotechnology](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41587-026-03327-6)** · Reinkemeier, Manero-Carranza et al. (ETH Zürich; Botnar Research Center for Child Health; University of Basel) · published Wednesday 21 October 2026
Measuring RNA, the readout of which genes are switched on, destroys the cell and captures only the present moment. Randall Platt's Basel group built a synthetic organelle, the "recordanelle", from one engineered protein: a mitochondrial membrane anchor, a module that forms condensates (droplet-like compartments) and a poly(A)-binding protein that grabs messenger RNAs. It can capture chosen engineered RNAs or a sample of up to 90% of the cell's endogenous mRNAs and keep them protected for several days. Short-lived responses, such as interferon signalling or a briefly induced promoter, stay readable in single cells after they have switched off. That means researchers can look back at a cell only once they know it matters, for example a cancer cell that survived treatment ([ETH Zürich](https://ethz.ch/en/news-and-events/eth-news/news/2026/10/new-method-reveals-what-a-cell-did-days-ago.html)). The work is in cultured human cells so far.
*Why it matters:* Single-cell biology gains a record of what a cell did days earlier, not just a snapshot of the present. That matters most for rare cells, such as drug-resistant clones, which are only identified after the fact.

### In ALS, T cells in the spinal fluid track slower disease and those in the blood track faster {#st-aa036938542d}
**[Nature Medicine](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04728-5)** · Chen, Mansikkaviita et al. (Karolinska Institutet; University of Helsinki; Helsinki University Hospital) · published Tuesday 27 October 2026
Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), a fatal motor-neuron disease, has increasingly been tied to the immune system. The team used single-cell RNA and T-cell-receptor sequencing, longitudinal immune profiling and imaging flow cytometry on patients' cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and blood. In the CSF they found long-lived T-cell clones and immune-cell pairs locked in synapse-like contacts, consistent with antigen being presented inside the nervous system. More CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the CSF went with slower progression. More circulating CD4+ effector and regulatory T cells in the blood went with faster progression. In sporadic ALS, cytomegalovirus (CMV)-specific T cells in the CSF and anti-CMV antibodies correlated with faster progression and shorter survival. All of these findings are associations, not proof of cause.
*Why it matters:* The immune system appears to have two opposite roles in ALS, protective inside the nervous system and harmful in the blood. Immune therapies may need to target one without suppressing the other. The CMV link is a lead worth testing, not yet a target.

### NeoCOAST-2: clearing tumour DNA from the blood marks lung-cancer patients who stay relapse-free {#st-1e2f3267cfbe}
**[Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11328-3)** · McGrath, Kar et al. (AstraZeneca; University of Padova; Istituto Oncologico Veneto) · published Sunday 25 October 2026
This sponsor-run phase 2 platform trial treated stage IIA–IIIB non-small-cell lung cancer before surgery. Each arm combined chemotherapy and durvalumab (an anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy) with oleclumab, monalizumab or the antibody–drug conjugate datopotamab deruxtecan (Dato-DXd). Two-year event-free survival was 70.5%, 75.7% and 77.9% respectively. Circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA, tumour fragments shed into blood) had cleared by the third treatment cycle in 50.9%, 54.5% and 72.5% of patients. Patients whose ctDNA cleared had far fewer events than those whose did not (pooled hazard ratio 0.22). Those with both ctDNA clearance and a pathological complete response (no viable tumour at surgery) did best of all (hazard ratio 0.10 versus neither). Arm sizes were 54–76 patients and not powered for comparison, so the Dato-DXd lead is "numerical", as the authors say.
*Why it matters:* A blood test taken partway through treatment could show early which patients need more aggressive follow-up, though prospective trials have to confirm that.

### Rubin Observatory's first dwarf-planet candidate lies beyond Pluto {#st-046b2adb921e}
**[NOIRLab / Rubin Observatory release](https://noirlab.edu/public/news/noirlab2626/)** · published Tuesday 27 October 2026
Before its 10-year Legacy Survey of Space and Time has formally begun, the Vera C. Rubin Observatory has found 2026 ED138. It is a trans-Neptunian object about 46 au from the Sun; Pluto averages 40 au, and 1 au is the Earth–Sun distance. The International Astronomical Union's Minor Planet Center announced it on Tuesday 27 October. Matthew Holman (Harvard–Smithsonian CfA) spotted it on 13 October by running Rubin data from March–July 2026 through a moving-object search code. Archival images going back to 2016 pin down more than a decade of its orbit. Its brightness and an assumed reflectivity give a size of 600–1,500 km, large enough that it is probably round. The team says infrared observations are needed to confirm the size and the dwarf-planet status, so the 900 km spread in the estimate is real uncertainty.
*Why it matters:* This is the first major Solar System find from the survey that will dominate the coming decade of sky mapping. If the size holds up, it would sit among the largest bodies known beyond Neptune.

### A dead star with a double debris disc, shaped by two surviving bodies {#st-9e70292c866b}
**[Nature Astronomy](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41550-026-03004-3)** · Gentile Fusillo, Lagos-Vilches et al. (University of Trieste; INAF; University of Warwick) · published Monday 26 October 2026
All 21 known white dwarfs with gaseous debris discs show the double-peaked emission lines of a single rotating ring. WD J1328−1450 shows four peaks. Spectra from ESO's Very Large Telescope resolve two separate gas rings, one from 0.14 to 0.34 solar radii and one from 0.54 to 1.93 solar radii, with a stable gap between them. The authors propose that each ring is fed by its own surviving planetary body, one a few kilometres across and the other possibly as big as Pluto or the Moon ([ESO](https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso2613/)). No existing model of debris-disc formation predicts this structure. Switzerland is an ESO member state.
*Why it matters:* It previews our own Solar System's end state, rocky leftovers grinding down around the Sun's eventual white-dwarf core, and shows that this end state can keep more structure than models assumed.

### Interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS carries methane 13 times richer in deuterium than comet 67P's {#st-88ccb8653b18}
**[Nature Astronomy](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41550-026-03002-5)** · Roth, Cordiner et al. (NASA Goddard; American University; Catholic University of America) · published Friday 23 October 2026
JWST's near-infrared spectra of 3I/ATLAS, the third known interstellar object, show methane with a deuterium-to-hydrogen ratio of 3.18 ± 0.30%. That is higher than any methane measured in the Solar System and 13 ± 2 times the value Rosetta found at comet 67P, the only other comet with deuterated methane on record. The detection is a very rare sighting of a deuterated organic molecule in an interstellar object. Heavy deuteration builds up in ices that form very cold under strong cosmic-ray irradiation. The ratio fits the hypothesis that 3I/ATLAS's ices formed in a cold, low-metallicity (poor in heavy elements) interstellar or protostellar cloud, but it does not pin that down. The author list includes the Physikalisch-Meteorologisches Observatorium Davos (PMOD/WRC).
*Why it matters:* It is a direct chemical fingerprint of where another planetary system's building blocks formed, and it looks unlike anything that formed around the Sun.

### One gamma-ray burst, two origins: a short burst and a long burst four minutes apart {#st-a8b0a7a11fda}
**[Nature Astronomy](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41550-026-03007-0)** · Li, Wang, Zhang et al. (Ningbo University; Sapienza University of Rome; ICRANet) · published Friday 23 October 2026
Gamma-ray bursts are usually sorted into two classes. Short bursts, under two seconds, come from neutron-star mergers. Long bursts come from collapsing massive stars, known as collapsars. Reanalysing archival event GRB 160425A, the team finds two sub-bursts four minutes apart. Nearly every standard diagnostic labels the first a merger-type burst and the second a collapsar-type burst: pulse shape, duration, spectral hardness, shortest variability timescale and the known empirical correlations. The paper reports the mismatch and does not explain it. A single source producing both signatures does not fit either standard picture, so the authors call for the classification scheme and progenitor models to be revisited.
*Why it matters:* This adds to evidence that "short = merger, long = collapse" is a rule of thumb rather than physics, which matters for using bursts to find gravitational-wave counterparts.

### Giant impacts help rocky planets keep, not lose, their primordial atmospheres {#st-4130b2eb5da9}
**[Nature Astronomy](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41550-026-03006-1)** · Modirrousta-Galian, Korenaga (Shanghai Jiao Tong University; Yale University) · published Monday 26 October 2026
A late giant impact heats a planet's interior, which should drive off a primordial hydrogen atmosphere, but it also adds mass, which holds the atmosphere more tightly. This model tracks both effects together. The added mass raises gravitational binding fully. Much of the impact energy does not reach the mantle: melting absorbs it as latent heat, and the impactor's iron core stays superheated deep down. The net result is that impacts push planets towards keeping their atmospheres. In this picture, larger planets with atmospheres are preferentially built by collisions, and smaller planets that missed late impacts lose their atmospheres to starlight. That would imply sub-Neptunes have heavier rocky interiors than super-Earths on average. It is a theoretical result, testable against exoplanet population statistics.
*Why it matters:* It offers a formation-history explanation for the gap between super-Earths and sub-Neptunes, one of the clearest features in the exoplanet census.
