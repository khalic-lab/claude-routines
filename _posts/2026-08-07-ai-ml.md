## 📄 ML/AI research (arXiv)

- <a id="st-f2748bffdaad" class="st-a"></a>**Letting models call tools by writing code, not filling in JSON, matches or beats the standard across 14 models** — [arXiv:2608.06370](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06370) · I. Patel, S. Sen, E. Lumer et al. (PwC) · `[preprint]`

  An LLM "agent" acts on the world by calling external tools. The usual method exposes each tool as a rigid JSON form the model fills in. This paper instead exposes tools as typed Python function stubs the model invokes by writing a short script — so it can chain and parallelize several calls in one turn (they call this programmatic tool calling, PTC). They run both head-to-head on BFCL v4 (the Berkeley Function-Calling Leaderboard, a standard tool-use benchmark) across 14 models. PTC matches or beats native JSON calling in 11 of 14 models — the GPT-5.6 family gains 10.6% — matches or wins in 13 of 14 under parallel "fan-out," and stays stable under "context rot" (long, cluttered conversations) where the JSON baseline slips ~2.3%.

  **Why it matters:** as agents take on more multi-step work, how you let them call tools is a real performance lever, not an implementation detail. The result — with its nod to Sutton's "bitter lesson" that general, compute-scaling methods win — says the code-native path improves as models get more capable, while the JSON path does not. _Submitted 2026-08-06._

- <a id="st-ebbf6946b8d4" class="st-a"></a>**A learning algorithm that finally hits the theoretical best-possible error rate for agnostic classification** — [arXiv:2608.06363](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06363) · M. E. Mathiasen, J. Qian, N. Zhivotovskiy (Aarhus University; HKU; UC Berkeley) · `[preprint]`

  PAC learning (Probably Approximately Correct — the standard theoretical model of supervised learning) asks how many labelled examples you need to come close to the best classifier in a given family. In the harder "agnostic" setting — where you do not assume the data can be perfectly separated — a lower bound on the achievable error has been known since the 1990s, but no algorithm had been proven to match it. This paper constructs a learner whose error is provably within a single universal constant of that lower bound, for any hypothesis family of finite VC dimension (VC dimension — a standard measure of a model family's capacity).

  **Why it matters:** it settles a long-standing open question in learning theory — the sample complexity of agnostic PAC learning is now pinned down up to constants at every noise level. This is foundational rather than a product; the payoff is conceptual clarity about the fundamental limits of learning from data. _Submitted 2026-08-06._

- <a id="st-53ff0fc3cde0" class="st-a"></a>**An exact, cheap formula for running the Muon optimizer under orthogonality constraints** — [arXiv:2608.06218](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06218) · M. Solonko, A. Molozhavenko, M. Rakhuba (HSE University) · `[preprint]`

  Muon is a recent "matrix-aware" optimizer — it treats a weight matrix as a matrix rather than a flat list of numbers — that has drawn attention for training large models efficiently. Many problems need weights whose columns are orthonormal, i.e. points on the Stiefel manifold (the set of matrices with orthonormal columns, ubiquitous in ML and scientific computing). Existing ways to run Muon on that manifold relied on approximate or iterative inner steps. This paper shows the corresponding update has an exact closed-form solution and packages it as Skewon, a practical algorithm for orthogonality-constrained optimization, with first-order convergence guarantees in the smooth non-convex setting.

  **Why it matters:** optimizers are where a lot of quiet training efficiency lives, and this is a distinct advance in the active Muon line (the last edition covered a separate Muon-for-diffusion fix): an exact, cheap update means practitioners can enforce orthogonality without paying for an inner solver each step. _Submitted 2026-08-06._

- <a id="st-dcfd870778a8" class="st-a"></a>**Rerouting a frozen model through its own layers — skipping and repeating them — for a free accuracy bump** — [arXiv:2608.05872](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05872) · P. Batorski, A. Pourhadi, A. Aitaza et al. (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]`

  Standard LLMs run their layers once, in order. "Layer routing" asks whether a different path — skipping some layers, repeating others — does better on a given task. Prior methods needed weight updates, an expensive per-example search, or ground-truth labels at inference. MACRO (Markov Chain Routing) instead learns the route as a Markov policy — a probabilistic rule that picks the next move based only on the current state — over skip, repeat and residual-add operations, leaving the model's weights untouched, then decodes high-probability routes with a top-k Viterbi search. It reports +5.0% average accuracy over unrouted baselines (largest gains on small models) and +7.2% over the best prior routing method while cutting route-search time 9.4× (14.8 → 1.6 hours).

  **Why it matters:** it is a way to squeeze more out of a fixed open-weight model without retraining it — most valuable for small models running on constrained hardware, where a few points of accuracy for near-zero extra training cost is a real win. _Submitted 2026-08-06._

- <a id="st-208e3042591c" class="st-a"></a>**A model teaches itself to reason using only its own answers — no ground truth, no larger teacher** — [arXiv:2608.06296](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06296) · Y. Li, B. Wang, Y. Liang et al. (UC San Diego; Georgia Tech; University of Maryland +1 more) · `[preprint]`

  Post-training methods that distill a model into itself ("on-policy self-distillation") usually still lean on an external signal — the correct answer, a reward from the environment, or guidance from a bigger model. U-OPSD (Unsupervised On-Policy Self-Distillation) removes all of it: it samples several of the model's own attempts, forms a pseudo-answer by majority vote when the model is confident enough (self-consistency), then teaches the model to reproduce that reasoning at the exact point in its longest wrong attempt where it first went off track. On math benchmarks (AIME24/25, HMMT25, MATH500, AMC23) it lifts Qwen3 by 8.5% and 10.7% at the 4B and 8B scales, matching or beating supervised methods (OPSD, GRPO) that use ground-truth answers.

  **Why it matters:** if a model can bootstrap its own reasoning from internal consistency alone, you can improve it in domains where labelled answers are scarce or costly. The honest caveat the reader should hold onto: majority-vote self-training can also entrench mistakes the model is confidently wrong about, so gains are not guaranteed to generalize past benchmarks like these. _Submitted 2026-08-06._

- <a id="st-17d5dfcc9e34" class="st-a"></a>**Turning a model that ranks answers into a usable reward signal for reinforcement learning** — [arXiv:2608.06310](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06310) · C. Wang, Z. Zhu, Y. Huo et al. (Northeastern University, Shenyang; NiuTrans Research +3 more) · `[preprint]`

  Reinforcement learning from feedback needs a reward model to score answers. The field is shifting from discriminative reward models (which emit a single number) to generative ones (which write out a comparison or judgement) — but generative reward models are good at ranking answers against each other, not at producing the lone scalar score that RL algorithms expect, so they have underdelivered in RL. RRC (Ranking-based Reward Construction) bridges the gap by deriving the reward from relative rankings: comparing a response against its own sampled peers (self-competitive) or against a small set of reference responses (anchor-guided).

  **Why it matters:** it lets the stronger, more interpretable generative reward models actually drive RL training, where until now their ranking skill did not translate into a useful learning signal. Reward modelling is the quiet bottleneck of most RL-tuned assistants, so a cleaner way to wire it up compounds. _Submitted 2026-08-06._

- <a id="st-ac76b1b6876b" class="st-a"></a>**When a vision model "zooms in to look," a causal audit finds the zoom often changes nothing** — [arXiv:2608.06270](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06270) · Z. Wang, B. Peng, L. Wei, C. Lu (Shanghai AI Laboratory; Shanghai Jiao Tong University; Shanghai Innovation Institute) · `[preprint]`

  The "thinking-with-images" paradigm gives multimodal models active visual operations such as crop-and-zoom, on the theory that looking closer helps. This paper builds a causal test rather than trusting accuracy numbers: it intervenes at three levels — turning tool-use on or off, corrupting everything the model sees during a run, and swapping a single observation while holding the rest of the reasoning fixed — to measure whether the returned visual evidence actually *causes* the answer. Its metric, Visual Evidence Gain, isolates each observation's contribution. Across six models and five perception benchmarks it finds two dominant failure modes — "Calling Without Looking" (the crop has no causal effect on the answer) and "Looking Without Planning" (the crop is informative but the model schedules it incoherently) — and shows the aggregate accuracy gain is concentrated in a small "calibrated" minority of cases.

  **Why it matters:** it is a caution that the headline accuracy gains from elaborate visual-tool pipelines can be an illusion — the model may be paying extra tokens for operations that do not drive its answer. A useful diagnostic to run before deploying or trusting such systems. _Submitted 2026-08-06._

- <a id="st-da8c5a083504" class="st-a"></a>**Borrowing multiplayer-game architecture to make video "world models" consistent across many viewers** — [arXiv:2608.06257](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06257) · Z. Cai, S. Yang, Y. Wang et al. (Alaya Lab; Peking University; Institute of Science Tokyo) · `[preprint]`

  A world model is a learned simulator that predicts how an environment will evolve. Video world models struggle with multiple players because they entangle the shared world state with each player's own view, wasting compute and drifting into view-to-view inconsistencies. MASS (Multiplayer world models with Authoritative Shared State) splits the two, exactly as a multiplayer game server does: a learned "Logic Engine" advances one global, authoritative typed state from everyone's joint actions and serves as the only recurrent memory, while a learned "Rendering Engine" draws each player's view on demand from that shared state. On a matched multiplayer Snake benchmark it beats multi-view baselines on state accuracy and cross-view consistency, and sustains 1,024 concurrent players over 10,000 steps.

  **Why it matters:** it is a concrete recipe for scalable, consistent multi-agent simulation — relevant to game engines, to training environments for agents, and to any setting where many observers must share one evolving world without it fragmenting. _Submitted 2026-08-06._

- <a id="st-b8ad1abff107" class="st-a"></a>**Self-improving agents can quietly poison themselves — and a gate before each new skill is the fix** — [arXiv:2608.05810](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05810) · L. Shang, M. Xu, Y. Sun et al. (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]`

  "Self-evolving" agents get better by distilling reusable skills from their own past runs into a growing library. This paper shows the process is not monotonic: past a critical library size, newly added skills start hurting performance. The cause is structural — once a defective skill enters the decision context it becomes reference material for distilling later skills, forming cross-round contamination chains — and it is largely irreversible: deleting the original culprit afterward recovers only a small fraction of the lost performance. Their fix, Verifier-as-Gatekeeper (VaG), screens each candidate skill with three complementary critics (structural validity, behavioral harmlessness, semantic consistency) before admission. On Terminal-Bench 2, unfiltered accumulation peaks and then decays, while VaG improves every round to 72% pass@1 with a library roughly 5× smaller, and its frozen skill set transfers to other backbones.

  **Why it matters:** it is a sharp warning for the popular "just let the agent keep learning from itself" design — quality control on what enters the skill memory has to be a pre-commit gate, because by the time a bad skill's damage shows up, cleaning it out no longer undoes the harm. _Submitted 2026-08-06._

- <a id="st-4a7e19517b94" class="st-a"></a>**The em-dash gives it away: a population-level LLM fingerprint in Congressional press releases** — [arXiv:2608.05889](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05889) · P. Czuma (Polish Association for Artificial Intelligence in Medicine) · `[preprint]`

  LLM-assisted writing leaves faint stylistic traces; the most-discussed is the unspaced em-dash (word—word), which is normal in typeset prose but unusual in US press writing, where AP style calls for spaced dashes. In a preregistered study of 146,239 congressional press releases (480 House and Senate offices, 2021–2025), the author measured the density of unspaced em-dashes per 1,000 characters. It held at 0.10–0.12 through 2021–2024, then jumped to 0.217 in 2025 — more than double the four-year baseline — with the share of releases containing one rising from ~13% to 24.8%. The rise was net-new (plain hyphen use stayed flat), held within individual offices, and survived placebo cut-offs and falsification tests; it was symmetric across parties and chambers.

  **Why it matters:** it is careful, honest evidence that LLM-assisted writing diffused broadly into official US communications by 2025 — and a model of how to measure such a claim without overclaiming. The author flags that a formal preregistered validation gate was narrowly breached, so the interpretation is offered as exploratory, and stresses the em-dash is a population-level marker, not a per-document detector: you cannot use it to "catch" any single release. _Submitted 2026-08-06._

## 🚀 New models, datasets, & open weights

**MiniMax open-weights a 33B video model — but bars the US, EU, UK and South Korea in the licence itself.** MiniMax released open weights for H3, a 33-billion-parameter dense, single-stream "omni" transformer that understands text, images, video and audio and generates video with native stereo audio at up to 2K resolution and durations up to 15 seconds. The weights — an H3-Base checkpoint in text-to-video/audio and reference-based variants — went up on Hugging Face and ModelScope. The unusual part is the licence: the MiniMax H3 Community License Agreement defines an "Applicable Territory" that excludes the United States, the European Union, the United Kingdom and South Korea, meaning users in those regions are not licensed to run, modify, distribute or even use the outputs of the locally-run weights. MiniMax frames this not as excluding regions but as recognising that video-generation models face a fast-moving regulatory environment — around likeness generation, copyright and content safety — in those jurisdictions, and invites interested parties there to contact it for a bespoke, guardrail-bound licence. ([MiniMaxAI/MiniMax-H3 model card and LICENSE, Hugging Face, accessed 7 Aug 2026](https://huggingface.co/MiniMaxAI/MiniMax-H3) `[vendor PR]` `[via snippet]`)
**Why it matters:** this is a genuinely new twist on "open weights" — a model that is freely downloadable yet legally carved away from exactly the jurisdictions writing the strictest AI-content rules. For a Switzerland-based reader the practical note is that Switzerland is not on the excluded list, so on the face of the licence text it falls inside the Applicable Territory — but anyone actually deploying a video-generation model should read the terms and the QA notes themselves rather than rely on this summary. The territory-scoped open-weight licence is a template other labs may copy as video-generation regulation tightens.

## 💼 Industry, funding, regulation

**The US settles on a voluntary, closed-model-only frontier-AI review — and will not publish the rules.** The White House finalised its framework for how the federal government will review advanced AI models before release, and convened major AI companies — Google, OpenAI, Anthropic and Meta — for a staff-level meeting on Tuesday 4 August 2026 to close out the details. The framework flows from Executive Order 14409, "Promoting Advanced Artificial Intelligence Innovation and Security," signed 2 June 2026, which directed agencies to stand up a voluntary scheme by 1 August 2026 under which developers give the government up to 30 days of early access to a "covered frontier model" so it can be evaluated for national-security risks such as cyber-misuse. The order and officials stress this is explicitly *not* licensing, pre-clearance or a permit. Two design choices stand out: a "covered frontier model" is defined as closed-source, with state-of-the-art capabilities and national-security risk — so open-weight models are excluded, and the framework states nothing in it should be read as restricting open models once they are released — and the administration is keeping the framework's contents non-public, leaving companies that were not invited, per Axios, "in the dark." ([White House, Executive Order 14409, 2 Jun 2026](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/06/promoting-advanced-artificial-intelligence-innovation-and-security/) `[via snippet]`; [Axios, 4 Aug 2026](https://www.axios.com/2026/08/04/trump-ai-framework-open-models) `[via snippet]`)
**Why it matters:** this is the US answer to the question the EU AI Act answers very differently — and the contrast matters for anyone in Switzerland building on or shipping frontier models across both markets. Where the EU's regime (whose general-purpose-AI enforcement began 2 August, covered in the last edition) is broad, binding and public, the US route is voluntary, kept secret, and aimed only at the largest closed labs. The open-weight exclusion lands in the middle of a live policy fight over whether open models are a strategic asset or a security risk. Read the "national security" scoping and the secrecy as the administration's framing and choices, not as settled fact — and note that the rules companies will actually be measured against are, by design, not on the public record.

**Anthropic starts an in-house chip team to co-design silicon with Claude.** Anthropic confirmed on 5 August 2026 — in a statement to Business Insider alongside a Silicon Engineer listing on its own careers site — that it is building an in-house team to design custom chips, the first time it has publicly acknowledged the effort. The stated plan is to co-design hardware and Claude models together, with roles spanning front-end design, pre-silicon verification, physical design, analog/mixed-signal and packaging, at a $320,000–$485,000 salary band. Anthropic says its silicon is meant to sit *inside* a multi-vendor stack rather than replace it — a deliberate contrast with OpenAI's single purpose-built part — and, per TechCrunch, is targeting large cuts in per-token inference cost. It comes weeks after reports that Anthropic was testing AMD hardware, part of a broader loosening of Nvidia's grip on AI compute. ([TechCrunch, 5 Aug 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/05/anthropic-is-hiring-an-ai-chip-design-team/) `[via snippet]`; [Forbes, 6 Aug 2026](https://www.forbes.com/sites/jonmarkman/2026/08/06/anthropic-enters-the-ai-chip-race-with-in-house-chip-team/) `[via snippet]`)
**Why it matters:** frontier-lab economics are increasingly a hardware story — controlling the chip is how you control inference cost and supply. A lab hiring to design its own silicon signals it expects inference demand, and Nvidia dependence, to be a structural cost worth attacking directly. Treat the cost-reduction figure as a stated target, not a delivered result.
