### EPFL theory predicts how much power a fusion plasma needs to switch into high confinement {#st-d0d56f54f709}
**[Physical Review Letters (accepted)](https://journals.aps.org/prl/accepted/10.1103/rw25-wy15)** · B. De Lucca, P. Ricci, B. Labit et al. (EPFL) · accepted Friday 13 November 2026, announced by EPFL on Friday 20 November 2026 via **[EPFL news](https://actu.epfl.ch/news/solving-a-40-year-puzzle-in-fusion-energy/)**
Tokamaks hold a plasma (a gas so hot its electrons are stripped off) inside a doughnut-shaped magnetic cage. Since 1982 experimenters have known that above a certain heating power the plasma abruptly enters "H-mode" (high confinement): turbulence at the edge collapses and heat stays in far better. ITER and every planned power plant depend on it, but the threshold power has so far been predicted only from empirical fits to past machines, and no theory explained together why it first falls and then rises with plasma density and why it depends on the magnetic field's direction. The Swiss Plasma Center team ran flux-driven two-fluid simulations of a tokamak edge, partly on the Daint supercomputer at CSCS in Lugano. They show that the turbulence itself generates sheared flows — neighbouring layers of plasma moving at different speeds — that tear the eddies apart. The field-direction asymmetry, reproduced in simulation for the first time, arises because collisions between particles break the time-reversal symmetry of the problem. From this they derive scaling laws for the power threshold in both the high- and low-density branches, matching or beating the accuracy of the existing empirical scalings. Applied to machines under construction, the model predicts ITER needs at least 43 MW of heating against the 73 MW it will have, but that Commonwealth Fusion Systems' compact SPARC needs about 26 MW against a planned 25 MW.
*Why it matters:* a threshold derived from physics is something reactor designers can extrapolate to machines that do not exist yet, which an empirical fit cannot safely do. The work is Swiss-funded (SNSF, SERI) and EUROfusion-funded, and the SPARC figure is a prediction that operating that machine will test.

### Google's quantum chip wins coordination games no classical strategy can {#st-821a8834ad0a}
**[Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aec3042)** · Google Quantum AI and Collaborators, S. Kumar, E. N. Rosenberg et al. (Google Quantum AI; Princeton University; University of Massachusetts Amherst) · published Thursday 19 November 2026
Contextuality is a basic quantum property: a measurement's outcome cannot be fixed in advance independently of which other compatible measurements are made alongside it. It can be tested with cooperative games in which players who cannot communicate must give answers satisfying a joint rule. In the "magic square" game the best classical strategy wins at most 8 of 9 rounds, while players sharing entangled qubits can in principle always win. On a superconducting processor the team plays that game with a few qubits and quantifies the effect through a Bell–Kochen–Specker inequality violation (a numerical test that rules out pre-assigned answers). It then scales up to the N-player GHZ game and to a two-dimensional hidden-linear-function problem, which shallow quantum circuits provably solve better than shallow classical ones, reporting the best fidelity and system size so far on both. Unlike random-circuit "quantum advantage" claims, these separations rest on proofs rather than on how hard a task is to simulate today. The tasks themselves have no practical use.
*Why it matters:* it gives a way to benchmark quantum processors whose pass mark comes from a theorem, so a faster classical algorithm later cannot retroactively overturn the result.

### Excitons in a lattice form a supersolid {#st-7b2bc6b7967b}
**[Nature Physics](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41567-026-03522-x)** · C. Morin, C. Lagoin, T. Gupta et al. (CNRS; Université Côte d'Azur; University of Padua) · published Wednesday 25 November 2026
A supersolid is a paradoxical state that is crystalline — its particles sit in a regular, symmetry-breaking pattern — and superfluid, flowing without friction, at the same time. Until now the evidence came from a few ultracold atomic gases in the weakly interacting regime. This French–Italian–US team uses dipolar excitons, bound electron–hole pairs in semiconductor layers that behave like tiny electrical bar magnets, confined in an artificial lattice in which they can hop over long distances. At one-quarter, one-third and one-half filling of the lattice sites, the excitons form crystals spanning about 100 sites that spontaneously break the lattice's translational symmetry. Simultaneously the long-range hopping keeps them phase-coherent across the sample, the signature of superfluid order. Numerical simulations of the lattice model confirm that supersolidity is a property of its ground state. The crystals are mesoscopic rather than bulk.
*Why it matters:* it moves supersolids out of the fragile weak-interaction corner into the strongly interacting regime, on a solid-state platform that can be tuned electrically.

### Ethiopian volcano released methane at the rate of an industrial blowout {#st-95897be55f53}
**[Nature Geoscience](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41561-026-02137-4)** · E. W. Dualeh, J. Biggs, S. K. Sharma et al. (University of Bristol; SRON Netherlands Institute for Space Research; McGill University) · published Thursday 19 November 2026
Volcanoes normally emit carbon dioxide and very little methane, so geologists have lacked modern analogues for magma cooking methane out of buried sediments — a process linked to abrupt climate shifts in Earth's deep past. After a mega-dyke intrusion in 2025 (molten rock forcing its way through a crack underground), Fentale volcano in Ethiopia's East African Rift never erupted, but satellites measured at least 38.2 ± 3.9 thousand tonnes of methane released over three months, plus at least 2.9 ± 1.0 million tonnes of CO₂. The peak methane rate, 157 ± 41 tonnes an hour, is comparable to a major industrial gas blowout and roughly four orders of magnitude above other volcanic systems. The emissions coincided with localised ground subsidence and thermal anomalies inside the caldera. The authors propose that the intrusion broke an impermeable cap over methane generated from hydrocarbon-rich sediments. They are explicit that one outburst is small next to annual natural and human emissions.
*Why it matters:* it identifies an unrecognised source of episodic methane, and gives climate scientists a modern, measurable version of a process they have only been able to infer from the rock record.

### A programmable route to fully substituted benzene rings {#st-5e9cf3cbb2cb}
**[Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11449-9)** · G. Mollaert, H.-F. Tu, J. R. King et al. (ICIQ; Universitat Rovira i Virgili; Colorado State University) · published Monday 23 November 2026
The benzene ring is the most common ring system in small-molecule drugs, but rings carrying six substituents are almost absent from screening libraries, because making them with control over which group goes where is hard and the building blocks are not sold. The Tarragona-led team assembles them from aromatic cyclopropenium cations (positively charged three-carbon rings) and silyl-substituted allenes, with all six positions encoded in the starting materials. Density-functional calculations trace the near-perfect selectivity to an unusual "non-classical" carbocation intermediate in which four atoms share four electrons. They use the reaction to make previously inaccessible five- and six-substituted analogues of the cancer drug sonidegib and the blood-pressure drug telmisartan, and show that starting from a single-handed allene preserves that handedness in the product.
*Why it matters:* it opens a region of drug-like chemical space that medicinal chemists have mostly been unable to enter.

### A patch of Wernicke's area plans the words you are about to say {#st-542b677fa961}
**[Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aef8963)** · A. B. Silva, M. K. Leonard, P. W. Hullett et al. (UC San Francisco; UC Berkeley) · published Thursday 19 November 2026
Textbooks call Wernicke's area, in the left temporal lobe, the brain's speech-comprehension centre, yet damage there also badly disrupts speaking — a paradox as old as the diagnosis. Using recordings from electrode grids placed on patients' brains during clinical monitoring, direct cortical stimulation and lesion analysis, the UCSF team isolates a distinct subzone, the left posterior superior temporal gyrus. It becomes active before speech begins, and interfering with it impairs production, so it is causally necessary rather than merely correlated. Its activity encodes features of whole upcoming words, not the sounds of them or the mouth movements needed to say them, and it is distinct from the neighbouring responses to hearing one's own voice. The same signal could be used to decode intended speech in people with paralysis. The participants are a small, clinically specific group.
*Why it matters:* it resolves a long-standing puzzle about aphasia (loss of language after brain injury), and identifies a word-level signal that speech neuroprostheses could read.

### University students drove the spread of new COVID variants {#st-6a4645004d33}
**[Cell](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2026.10.051)** · G. K. Moreno, T. Brock-Fisher, L. A. Krasilnikova et al. (Broad Institute; Harvard University; Massachusetts Department of Public Health) · published Thursday 19 November 2026
The team linked more than 85,000 SARS-CoV-2 genomes collected in Massachusetts between November 2021 and January 2023 to epidemiological records from 666 settings, including schools, colleges and skilled nursing facilities. Genome sequences trace who infected whom far better than case counts do. Elevated transmission turned out to be concentrated in three age-defined groups — older adolescents in schools, undergraduates in colleges and residents of nursing facilities — with little extra risk for other people in the same buildings, staff included. Young adults in colleges played a disproportionate role, and new viral lineages consistently expanded there first before spreading more widely. Spread followed a repeatable urban-to-rural gradient, and vaccination was associated with less onward transmission. The authors also estimate how much sequencing is needed to catch an emerging variant in time. This is one US state during the Omicron era, and the findings are observational associations.
*Why it matters:* it says where to put genomic surveillance for the earliest warning of the next respiratory-virus variant, and which settings generated risk rather than merely recording cases.

### EPFL finds a second system that keeps the genome's ancient viral DNA shut {#st-e29c640fc5d9}
**[Nature Genetics](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-026-02823-4)** · W. Matsushima, J. Duc, S. Sheppard et al. (EPFL; National Institute of Genetics, Japan) · published Tuesday 17 November 2026, announced by EPFL on Monday 23 November 2026 via **[EPFL news](https://actu.epfl.ch/news/how-proteins-from-viruses-control-genes/)**
At least a quarter of the sequences that regulate human genes are derived from transposable elements — mobile pieces of DNA, many of them descended from ancient viruses, that have accumulated over millions of years. They are a source of new genetic switches, but uncontrolled they can wreck a cell, so the genome keeps most of them shut. The known mechanism uses KRAB zinc-finger proteins, which recruit machinery that marks the proteins DNA wraps around and locks the region into a closed state. Only a small fraction of transposable elements are covered this way. Didier Trono's lab at EPFL, with postdoc Wayo Matsushima, traced a second family — SCAN zinc-finger proteins, whose defining domain itself originated as a captured retroviral capsid gene — across 68 vertebrate genomes, and found it expanded independently in different animal lineages, with each protein recognising elements of a matching evolutionary age. Mapping their binding sites in human cells showed these proteins work without the usual chemical silencing marks: the DNA is simply kept inaccessible, and resists being pried open even by "pioneer" factors such as SOX2 and OCT4. Remove one of the proteins for a few hours and the region opens; put it back and it closes again.
*Why it matters:* it describes a distinct, more local way cells keep inherited viral DNA quiet while still allowing it to supply new regulatory switches over evolutionary time — one of the mechanisms that makes species differ in how their genes are controlled.

### Euclid finds the first quasar beyond redshift 8 {#st-bd7032b9a9ad}
**[Astronomy & Astrophysics](https://www.aanda.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202662243)** `[new source]` · J. Wolf, S. Belladitta, D.-M. Yang et al. (European Southern Observatory; Max Planck Institute for Astronomy; INAF) · published Tuesday 24 November 2026; announced by **[ESA](https://www.esa.int/articles/about-us/directorates/science/euclid/euclid-breaks-own-quasar-record-by-40-million-years)** the same day
A quasar is a galaxy's core blazing as gas falls into a supermassive black hole; finding them early constrains how fast such black holes can grow. From about 18 months of Euclid wide-survey imaging, with follow-up at Keck, the Large Binocular Telescope, Hubble and JWST, the team places J1504+6252 (nicknamed Dún Aonghasa) at redshift 8.129 ± 0.005 — light emitted 630 million years after the Big Bang, when the universe was 5% of its present age. That is the first quasar known beyond redshift 8, and pushes the frontier back about 39 million years past Euclid's own previous record. JWST spectra give a black-hole mass of roughly 360 million Suns. Unusually it is feeding slowly now, so it must have grown in earlier, very rapid episodes. Masses from single spectra carry large uncertainties, and this is one object; Euclid's whole survey is expected to yield at most a few tens at this distance.
*Why it matters:* a 360-million-solar-mass black hole that is not currently gorging, seen this early, is hard to assemble in standard growth models, and it is the kind of object that decides between competing accounts of how the first black holes formed. Euclid is an ESA mission, and the discovery paper is led from ESO and MPIA in Germany.

### BepiColombo is in orbit at Mercury after a thruster burn cut out {#st-edb89244fa3b}
**[ESA](https://www.esa.int/articles/about-us/directorates/operations/bepicolombo-safely-in-mercury-orbit-despite-unexpected-challenge)** · published Monday 23 November 2026
The joint ESA–JAXA BepiColombo mission reached Mercury orbit on Saturday 21 November, the second spacecraft ever to do so after NASA's MESSENGER. ESA's own account is more complicated than arrival: the orbit-insertion burn, which started at 18:24 UTC, ended early, delivering about 77% of the planned change in velocity, and the spacecraft entered safe mode — the protective state it adopts when it meets conditions it does not expect. Controllers at ESOC in Darmstadt confirmed capture at about 01:00 local time, and have traced the fault, with Airbus, to a software issue in attitude guidance and thruster control. The resulting orbit has a 75-hour period instead of the planned 58 hours; the remaining burn sequence has been re-planned to absorb that, with no extra burns needed. The mission used "weak stability capture", arriving slowly enough for Mercury's gravity to hold it temporarily — previously used only at the Moon — which is why insertion takes a series of burns. Separation of the two science orbiters is still set for 9 December, with ESA's orbiter reaching its science orbit in March 2027 and observations starting that April.
*Why it matters:* reaching Mercury takes more energy than reaching the outer solar system, and this is only the second orbiter ever to manage it; eight years of cruise survived a failure on the final manoeuvre with the schedule intact.

### The "Norma arm" may be the Milky Way's bar, not a spiral arm {#st-306a48e795f5}
**[Nature Astronomy](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41550-026-03010-5)** · H. Davis, J. Bland-Hawthorn, T. Tepper-García et al. (University of Sydney; Lund University; UNSW Sydney) · published Friday 20 November 2026
We cannot see our own galaxy from outside, so its shape is reconstructed from tracers inside it. Masers — bright natural microwave beacons around young massive stars — have been used to trace a segment called the Norma arm, running from one end of the Milky Way's central bar. Using new N-body and hydrodynamic simulations, this Sydney-led team argues the million-year-old masers instead mark the shock front on the leading edge of the bar, equivalently its dust lane, where gas piles up as it crosses into the bar's orbit. That implies the dust lane is actively forming stars. Fitting the masers to the simulated shock front yields a bar that is long, slowly rotating and star-forming: semi-axes of about 4.3 and 2.3 kiloparsecs, inclined about 20°, with a pattern speed of at most 40 km/s per kiloparsec. It is an inference from simulations matched to tracers, not a direct measurement.
*Why it matters:* if one of the four arms in the standard picture of our galaxy is really part of the bar, both the Milky Way's spiral structure and the bar's disputed size and speed need revising — and the bar becomes a place where stars are born.

### Nature retracts the gigaparsec-anisotropy claim {#st-66842363c6a0}
**[Nature retraction note](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11388-5)** · F. Sylos Labini (Centro Ricerche Enrico Fermi), M. Galoppo (University of Canterbury) · published Thursday 19 November 2026
In June, Nature published a claim of anisotropic structures in the galaxy distribution on gigaparsec scales — a potential violation of the cosmological principle, the assumption that the universe looks statistically the same in every direction, on which modern cosmology rests. The authors have now retracted it, at the editors' suggestion. Shortly after publication Till Sawala identified the error: the analysis compared comoving distances between observed galaxies against luminosity distances between galaxies in the simulated comparison catalogues — two different distance measures. Correcting it drops the discrepancy to 1.48–2.75 sigma depending on how the sample is built, below cosmology's conventional 3-sigma bar. Both authors agree with the retraction, say their method remains valid as tentative evidence, and will submit a corrected version elsewhere.
*Why it matters:* the original claim was carried as a possible crack in the standard cosmological model, including in a daily brief here on 25 June. Five months later it is a 2-sigma hint, retracted — a reminder of how often an extraordinary cosmological signal turns out to be a mismatch between two definitions of distance.
