## 📄 ML/AI research (arXiv)

Once again, the tools used to measure systems are the story. Test suites, monitor evaluations and time-horizon curves each turn out to measure less than people assume. Two agent-security papers from one Sydney–Singapore group show protections that hold for each single decision but fail across a whole session. Three are worth reading in full: the overspecified-tests paper, the monitor-evaluation paper and the permission-reply paper.

- <a id="st-ce62e26d744b" class="st-a"></a>**About a third of SWE-bench Pro tasks test for details the issue never asks for** — [arXiv:2611.31839](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31839) · A. Aggarwal, Y. Hu (Purdue University) · `[preprint]`

  SWE-bench-style benchmarks grade a coding agent's fix with the tests from the original human fix. Those tests can check details the issue never states, so a correct but different fix fails. This is called an overspecified test. The authors had two LLM judges (Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol) score each task, and flagged it only when both agreed. On 206 tasks that earlier human audits had already labelled, the judges caught 89% of the known overspecified tasks and wrongly flagged 4% of the sound ones. Across the full benchmarks they flag 23% of SWE-bench Verified and 35% of the public SWE-bench Pro tasks. The top 30 Verified submissions solve 31% of flagged tasks, against 88% of the rest. After Claude Opus 5.5 loosened the flagged tests, 61% of 2,186 previously failed patches passed.

  **Why it matters:** counting those patches as solved would raise top systems' scores by up to 12 points, so much of the leaderboard's "hard" tail measures test quirks rather than agent skill. The caveat is that both the judging and the loosened tests come from models. It is a distinct paper from Tuesday's SWE-Gym audit but points the same way. _Submitted 24 Nov 2026._

- <a id="st-ea9754313df6" class="st-a"></a>**The way a safety monitor is tested can change its measured miss rate about fortyfold** — [arXiv:2611.34934](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34934) · C. Griffin, O. Makins, A. Cooper Stickland et al. (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]`

  AI monitors are classifiers that read an agent's transcript and flag harmful actions. They are tested on transcripts from "attack policies": models set up to pursue a hidden harmful side task, standing in for real misuse. The authors tested an ex-production OpenAI monitor. They built attacks against proxy monitors only, then measured them on a held-out GPT-5.4 monitor. The false-negative rate (the share of attacks missed) ranged from 0.5% to 20.8% depending on how hard one method, reinforcement learning, was optimised. It ranged from 6.4% to 23.0% across different methods at their best settings. The attack transcripts can also contain giveaways, such as text about a "side task" that never appears in the instructions the monitor sees.

  **Why it matters:** a monitor tested only against basic attacks can look far stronger than it is. Any published monitor score needs an argument for why its attack transcripts resemble real deployment, and the authors say no one yet knows how to make that argument convincingly. _Submitted 25 Nov 2026._

- <a id="st-976808a4b5f0" class="st-a"></a>**An answer to one permission prompt can delete a coding agent's deny rules** — [arXiv:2611.32195](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32195) · Z. Fei, H. Fei, J. Chen et al. (University of Technology Sydney; National University of Singapore; UNSW +1 more) · `[preprint]`

  Coding agents such as Claude Code ask before running risky commands, and can let plugins or remote services answer those prompts. Claude Code's documentation says such an answer cannot override the operator's deny rules, for example a rule blocking reads of a secrets file. The authors show that an answer can carry a policy update that deletes a deny rule, because Claude Code checks only updates that add or replace allow rules. In their test, a remote service approved an unrelated command and attached the deletion. The agent then read the protected secrets in that session and the next, even inside the vendor's strict sandbox. In the records, the deletion looks the same as a local edit by the operator. The authors prove limits on which updates a runtime can safely accept, which depend on whether it records who wrote each rule. Those limits correctly predicted the outcome in 13 permission designs across ten agent systems, including Codex, Gemini CLI, opencode and Goose.

  **Why it matters:** the protection holds for each decision but fails across a run, and the fix is to enforce the limits at the shared policy-update step. Only 2 of 2,227 marketplace plugins surveyed send policy updates, so the exposure today is narrow. But relays that forward such deletions already exist. _Submitted 24 Nov 2026._

- <a id="st-27bda15fcbbe" class="st-a"></a>**Agent context summaries let injected data pass as the user's own words** — [arXiv:2611.32194](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32194) · Z. Fei, H. Fei, Y. Xu et al. (University of Technology Sydney; National University of Singapore; UNSW) · `[preprint]`

  Long agent sessions are periodically "compacted": a model summarises earlier turns so they fit in the context window. The authors observed that Claude Code and Codex insert this summary as a user message. Some prompt-injection defences, such as Progent, decide what an agent may do based on the user's request. After compaction they treat the whole summary as the user speaking, including quoted documents and injected text. In one test, an attacker's notice listed a different bank account as plain data, and the user asked the agent to pay the rent. A policy built from a frontier model's summary allowed the attacker's account in 91% of cases. A policy built from the user's own words never did. Summarisers tend to mark injected instructions but rarely mark injected data.

  **Why it matters:** summarising context turns out to be a security boundary. The proposed fix credits only authenticated user text, carried verbatim through compaction, and it stopped every attacker action in 40 attacked AgentDojo episodes. _Submitted 24 Nov 2026._

- <a id="st-fcc62e4ce1c5" class="st-a"></a>**Rewarding agents for gathering enough evidence before answering lifts accuracy 17%** — [arXiv:2611.32884](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32884) · R. Sin Jing Xuan, P. Feng, W. Wang, S. Poria (Nanyang Technological University) · `[preprint]`

  On long tasks, agents often commit to an answer before the evidence supports it, which the authors call "laziness". A reward given only for the final answer cannot discourage this, because a lucky guess scores the same as a well-supported one. In simulated tasks, the simulator can compute exactly which answers are still possible given what the agent has observed. Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Beliefs (RLVB) uses this. It rewards the agent for continuing while several answers remain possible and for committing once only one fits. It needs no judge model and ignores the "belief" text the agent writes. The authors tested three vision-language models on three benchmarks, two of them unseen in training. RLVB improved the base model in all nine settings, by 17.1% on average. Standard RL methods (GRPO and PPO) with the final-answer reward fell below the base model on average.

  **Why it matters:** the trained agents answered with sufficient evidence in 51%, 12% and 74% of episodes, against 8%, 0.4% and 36% before training, so the gain comes from answering when the evidence supports it. The method needs a simulator that can compute exactly which answers remain possible, so it has not been tested on open-ended real tasks. _Submitted 24 Nov 2026._

- <a id="st-15dbc2df4f9b" class="st-a"></a>**A looped transformer matches OLMo3 with 1.7× fewer training tokens at 700M parameters** — [arXiv:2611.34923](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34923) · L. Zhu, S. Wang, X. Wang et al. (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]`

  A looped transformer runs each token through the same layers several times, which adds depth without adding parameters but costs compute. MoLT (Mixture-of-Loops Transformer) passes keys and values between loops as a memory. These are the cached attention entries a model reads when looking back at earlier tokens. A U-shaped variant skips decoder passes whose outputs would go unused. In 400B-token pretraining it beat OLMo3 at 700M and 1.5B dense sizes and a 3B mixture-of-experts size (a model that routes each token to a few specialised sub-networks). At 700M it reached the same validation loss with 1.7× fewer tokens. At 3B it scored 2.1 points higher across 10 tasks. A ~113M model matched non-looped latency (996.6 vs 1,000.6 ms).

  **Why it matters:** looped designs are spreading, as Tuesday's GLM-5.4 and Inkling 2 Mini items showed, and this paper gives an open recipe with code. The latency match is shown only at small scale. _Submitted 25 Nov 2026._

- <a id="st-57a47b20e07b" class="st-a"></a>**Agent time-horizon curves cannot show whether failure risk falls as a task goes on** — [arXiv:2611.32415](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32415) · R. Zhao (CUHK, Shenzhen) · `[preprint]`

  METR's "time horizon" plots an agent's chance of finishing a task against how long that task takes a human. Several analyses fitted survival models to this curve. Survival models are statistics of when failures happen, and these analyses concluded that an agent's moment-to-moment risk of failing, called the hazard, falls as a task goes on. This paper proves a single curve cannot show that, because many different hazard patterns produce the same curve. Repeated runs of the same task can separate them: the curve for two runs both succeeding is shifted by an amount that reveals the hazard's shape. The author applied this to METR's public run-level data, under assumptions the data cannot verify. For the five frontier models in the TH1.0 release, the results reject a hazard that falls or stays flat. For TH1.1 the answer depends on how human task times were obtained.

  **Why it matters:** claims that agents become more reliable the longer they run rest on a quantity the standard plot cannot measure. This is a single-author statistical argument whose empirical result depends on stated assumptions. _Submitted 24 Nov 2026._

- <a id="st-b90298aeea16" class="st-a"></a>**Coding agents' patches are bigger than developers', but the extra is mostly added tests** — [arXiv:2611.34875](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34875) · A. Goel, B. Steenhoek, Y. Hu (Microsoft) · `[preprint]`

  Agent patches change more lines than developers' patches, which is often read as "over-editing". The authors replayed 7,660 successful GitHub Copilot CLI runs from 18 frontier models. They removed each block of the final patch in turn and reran the tests to check whether it was needed. On SWE-bench Verified, most of the extra edits add tests (61%) or documentation (13%). On DeepSWE most of the extra is code (58%). On average, agents' code is at least as necessary as developers' (0.887 vs 0.864 on Verified). Still, 11% of agent code blocks are unneeded, and 89% of those change behaviour. On DeepSWE, models that solved more tasks left more unneeded code (Spearman −0.59).

  **Why it matters:** patch size is a poor measure of over-editing. The authors argue benchmarks should report how much of a patch is needed alongside the solve rate. Note that the authors work at Microsoft, which ships Copilot. _Submitted 25 Nov 2026._

- <a id="st-4ac01456f59a" class="st-a"></a>**Lossless KV-cache compression makes cache storage act about 1.4× larger** — [arXiv:2611.32954](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32954) · W. Zhu, Z. Sheng, P. Deng et al. (East China Normal University; ZTE) · `[preprint]`

  Serving engines keep the KV cache, the saved attention state for earlier tokens, in GPU memory. They spill it to RAM and SSD so that repeated prompt prefixes can be reused. LoomKV compresses BF16 cache values losslessly to 0.71 of their raw size by compactly encoding the sign-and-exponent byte, and FP8 values to 0.85–0.87. Built on vLLM and LMCache, the compressed storage behaves like raw storage 1.4× larger on an agent workload. When the cache is near the point where it starts evicting useful entries, that gives 2.7–3.6× more useful throughput and 1.9–3.1× lower time to first token. When capacity is plentiful, it costs at most 2.2% throughput.

  **Why it matters:** unlike lossy KV quantization, outputs are unchanged, so this is spare capacity for multi-turn and agent serving at no cost in accuracy. _Submitted 24 Nov 2026._

## 💼 Industry, funding, regulation

- <a id="st-0ddbac9e2265" class="st-a"></a>**42 attorneys general ask a bankruptcy court to block the sale of Character.AI users' chats** — [California Attorney General, press release, 24 Nov 2026](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-opposes-asset-sale-characterai-amid-child-safety-concerns)

  Character.AI filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in October. It plans to auction nearly all its assets on Tuesday 1 December 2026, including the chat histories of more than 21 million US accounts, over 2.1 million of them registered to minors, together with IP addresses and other metadata. A bipartisan coalition of 42 state attorneys general is asking the court to reject the sale. They say it offers no safeguards for users and breaks Character.AI's earlier promise not to sell personal data. According to TechCrunch, the only disclosed bidder is a Delaware company formed on 13 November. Character.AI says user data would stay covered by its privacy policy.

  **Why it matters:** this is an early test of whether chatbot conversations, which are often intimate, can be sold as an ordinary asset when an AI company fails. The court's answer will set expectations for every consumer AI service. Several state chatbot-safety laws take effect on 1 January 2027.

- <a id="st-f74a678400d2" class="st-a"></a>**New York bans AI chatbot toys for five years** — [Office of Governor Kathy Hochul, 24 Nov 2026](https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/consumers-begin-their-holiday-shopping-season-governor-hochul-signs-five-year-ban-ai-chatbot) `[new source]`

  Governor Hochul signed S.9408A/A.11144B, a five-year moratorium on making, distributing and selling toys with built-in AI chatbots in New York. It covers talking stuffed animals, dolls and robots that use the internet to hold conversations with children. The law takes effect 90 days after signing. Attorney General Letitia James said her office will enforce it. A state official told TechCrunch the fines are $15,000 a day. TechCrunch reports New York is the second state, after California, to ban AI toys.

  **Why it matters:** this is a product-category ban rather than a disclosure rule. It shows US states moving from regulating AI companions to banning some uses outright for young children.

- <a id="st-d6e587376ca0" class="st-a"></a>**Nvidia pauses server shipments to four Southeast Asian countries after the US remote-access rule** — [TechCrunch, citing The Wall Street Journal, 26 Nov 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/26/nvidia-hits-pause-on-chip-shipments-to-southeast-asia/)

  According to The Wall Street Journal, Nvidia told manufacturing partners including Dell and Super Micro to pause server shipments to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia. The pause has no stated end date while Nvidia checks whether regional partners meet US export requirements. The trigger is the Commerce Department rule, announced on 16 November and in force since 19 November. It targets Chinese companies renting advanced Nvidia chips in overseas data centres. [CNBC reports](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/25/nvidia-china-ai-chip-remote-access.html) that an unusually large number of Nvidia GPUs, including thousands of B300s, have been listed for resale since the rule was announced. Nvidia declined to comment to CNBC.

  **Why it matters:** this is the rule's first visible effect on supply. The think tank Institute for Progress estimates, as cited by the Journal, that such remote data centres provide about 24% of China's AI computing power.

## 🍎 Apple Silicon / on-device

- <a id="st-c33bf3816c9e" class="st-a"></a>**MLX 0.32.5 adds sliding-window attention and faster 4-bit kernels on Metal** — [ml-explore/mlx, release v0.32.5, 25 Nov 2026](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx/releases/tag/v0.32.5)

  Apple's MLX array framework shipped v0.32.5, its first release since mid-October. It adds sliding-window attention, a fast path for turning FP4 weights back into half precision, new quantized matrix-vector kernels, and split attention kernels aimed at speculative decoding (a small draft model proposes tokens that the large model checks in one pass). It also fixes attention kernels for 256- and 512-wide heads on M1/M2 chips, along with several out-of-bounds reads.

  **Why it matters:** sliding-window attention and quicker FP4 handling matter for running recent open models locally on Macs. Downstream mlx-lm has not had a release since 16 October.
