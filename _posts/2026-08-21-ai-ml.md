## 📄 ML/AI research (arXiv)

This cycle's batch (submitted 20 August) leans skeptical and engineering-minded: several papers ask whether an agent's advertised abilities survive contact with a proper control. Two go straight at the self-improvement narrative — **Phantom Gains** shows that most reported "the model improved itself" results flip sign once you run an honest baseline, and **AI4AI-Bench** builds the first benchmark that isolates the actual crux of recursive self-improvement (can an agent design a *better training algorithm*, not just tune knobs). A second cluster stress-tests agent *memory* — **StateMemBench** and **MemTrapBench** both show that storing and retrieving facts correctly is not the same as *reasoning* correctly with them. A practical efficiency strand (adaptive test-time compute, sparse-attention fine-tuning) keeps chipping at inference cost. If you read three in full: **Phantom Gains** (if you ever measure model improvement), **StateMemBench** (if you build agent memory), and **SWE-bench Science** (for where coding agents actually stand on real code).

- <a id="st-1d0329964362" class="st-a"></a>**Most "self-improvement" gains vanish once you add a control** — [arXiv:2608.20290](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20290) · Cheng Xu, Nan Yan et al. (University College Dublin; Georgia Tech; Dalian University of Technology) · `[preprint]`

  The paper audits a now-common claim: that a model, retrained on its own outputs, has "improved itself." The authors run three rounds of low-rank self-training (rank-32 LoRA — a lightweight fine-tuning method that adjusts a small add-on rather than all the weights) on Qwen3-8B, but critically also push a *frozen, untrained* copy through the identical measurement pipeline as a control. They find seven distinct measurement failures — several of them standard practice — and each one, on its own, inverts a reported finding when the control is missing. One example: scoring with a single greedy decode (taking the model's top-choice answer once) manufactures apparent capability changes even on the untrained model, largely an artifact of how examples are batched at inference.

  **Why it matters:** the field increasingly grades self-improvement not by average accuracy but by *which specific problems* a model newly solves or breaks — and that means subtracting two noisy measurements, exactly where artifacts hide. This is a concrete, reproducible checklist for telling a real gain from a measurement mirage, and a caution that a lot of the "models bootstrapping themselves" literature may be measuring its own pipeline. _Submitted 20 Aug 2026._

- <a id="st-f5e87bae0e17" class="st-a"></a>**A benchmark that asks whether agents can improve the recipe that makes AI** — [arXiv:2608.20318](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20318) · Yizhe Chi, Wenyi Li et al. (Tsinghua University; Einsia.AI) · `[preprint]`

  Recursive self-improvement (RSI) — the idea that an AI could improve the *process* that builds AIs, so each generation inherits the gain — hinges on one specific ability: can an agent design a better *training algorithm* (a better objective or update rule), as opposed to just collecting more data or tuning hyperparameters? AI4AI-Bench isolates exactly that, with 10 frozen research repositories spanning 10 training-algorithm families; a change counts only if it alters *how the model learns*, not how a run is executed. The authors argue no prior suite separates those two.

  **Why it matters:** "recursive self-improvement" is invoked constantly in AI-risk and AGI-timeline debates, usually without a way to measure it. Pairing this with Phantom Gains above, the cycle's message is consistent: the burden of proof on self-improvement claims is being raised, and the honest current answer is "not yet, and here's how we'll know." _Submitted 20 Aug 2026._

- <a id="st-1bd26122381e" class="st-a"></a>**Agent memories can store the right facts and still track the wrong state** — [arXiv:2608.19652](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19652) · Xinyi Fan, Miri Liu et al. (UIUC) · `[preprint]`

  Most memory benchmarks test *recall* — can the agent fetch a fact it saw earlier. This paper argues that real deployments need *state tracking*: as facts, constraints, and decisions get revised over a long interaction, the answer must reflect the *current* state, not a superseded one. StateMemBench is 234 multi-session scenarios with closed-pool grading that explicitly scores whether an answer reflects the current state, a stale (superseded) state, or neither — separating "tracked the wrong version" from other errors by construction. Existing memory systems find it hard.

  **Why it matters:** the single most common way a long-running assistant goes wrong is confidently acting on something you changed three turns ago. Naming and measuring that failure mode — distinct from ordinary recall — gives memory-system builders a target that matches how agents actually break in production. _Submitted 20 Aug 2026._

- <a id="st-fa8800580d12" class="st-a"></a>**Even correct, relevant memories can sabotage the current answer** — [arXiv:2608.20202](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20202) · Mengru Wang, Haozhe Luo et al. (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]`

  The companion failure mode to state-tracking. MemTrapBench shows that even memories that are faithfully recorded *and* semantically relevant can distort a model's reasoning or beliefs and drag down performance on the task in front of it. The authors define two "cognitive traps": *Reasoning Fixation* (a retrieved memory locks the model onto a prior line of thought it should abandon) and *Belief Distortion* (a memory shifts what the model treats as true). Both show up across two model families.

  **Why it matters:** the reflexive fix for agent errors is "give it more memory / better retrieval." This is evidence that more retrieved context is not free — the wrong-but-relevant memory is a live hazard, not just missing memory — which argues for memory systems that decide *whether to use* a retrieved item, not only how to fetch it. _Submitted 20 Aug 2026._

- <a id="st-f961a73c1c1a" class="st-a"></a>**On real scientific code, the best coding agent still fails more than half the time** — [arXiv:2608.19799](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19799) · Zhipeng Xu, Jiahao Lu et al. (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]`

  SWE-bench Science is a repository-level benchmark of 119 tasks from 98 GitHub repositories across 20 scientific domains, sorted into three paradigms (issue-driven, expert-exploratory, engineering-integration). The framing is that scientific software is part of the instrument — a bug can corrupt the *evidence*, not just the program. The headline result: even the strongest agent tested, Claude Code with Opus-5 (max), lands a pass@1 below 50% (pass@1 = solves it on the first attempt). A paired ablation finds scientific guidance is *not* uniformly helpful — well-grounded context helps, but poorly aligned guidance can anchor the agent onto a wrong repair.

  **Why it matters:** the reader works in software, and "can a coding agent fix a real repo" is the concrete version of the agent-hype question. A sub-50% first-try rate on *scientific* code — where domain knowledge and correctness both bite — is a sober, well-measured counterweight to leaderboard-topping SWE-bench numbers, and the failure taxonomy is a practical guide to where these agents break. _Submitted 20 Aug 2026._

- <a id="st-6ab8343d65c8" class="st-a"></a>**Teaching a model to allocate its own thinking budget** — [arXiv:2608.20256](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20256) · Gijs Kassenaar, Zhao Yang, Vincent François-Lavet (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]`

  Reasoning models usually run at a fixed token budget, over-thinking easy problems and under-thinking hard ones. This paper lets the model pick, as its *first token*, one of three modes — NoThink, Short, or Long — learned inside GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization, the reward-based post-training recipe behind recent reasoning models) with no separate router, using a shaped reward plus hard per-mode token caps. On a 1.5B model trained on MATH the modes emerge without collapsing, and accuracy holds (0.782 vs 0.796 base on held-out MATH500) while mean response length drops from 4,796 to 2,811 tokens — a 41% cut. It transfers untrained to other benchmarks, e.g. 76% fewer tokens on GSM8K.

  **Why it matters:** inference cost is roughly proportional to tokens generated, so a model that spends effort in proportion to difficulty — and learns the dial itself, rather than a hand-set "reasoning effort" slider — is a cheap, broadly reusable efficiency lever. _Submitted 20 Aug 2026._

- <a id="st-83da981d33d7" class="st-a"></a>**Fine-tuning a model to co-adapt with whatever KV-cache shortcut you use** — [arXiv:2608.19920](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19920) · Matthias Seeger, Zeyu Zhang et al. (Amazon Web Services; University of Amsterdam; TU Berlin) · `[preprint]`

  Long-context inference is bottlenecked by the KV cache (the stored keys/values for every past token, whose size grows with context). Sparse-attention policies drop most of it to save memory, but the model was never trained to expect that. This method fine-tunes a model to *co-adapt* with any KV-cache policy, runs on a single 40 GB A100, and often ends up *more* accurate than a model trained with exact attention. It ships an efficient kernel for H2O (a leading "keep the heavy-hitter tokens" policy) and open-source code, **KeysAndValues** ([github.com/awslabs/keys_values](https://github.com/awslabs/keys_values)).

  **Why it matters:** most sparse-attention work bolts the shortcut onto a model at inference and eats an accuracy hit; training the model to *live with* the shortcut on a single GPU is a practical recipe for cheaper long-context serving — directly relevant to anyone running long-context models on modest hardware. _Submitted 20 Aug 2026._

- <a id="st-b2c692507b26" class="st-a"></a>**A memory-efficient recipe for agentic RL that outperforms PPO and GRPO** — [arXiv:2608.19842](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19842) · Dayang Liang, Lang Feng, Bo An, Yunlong Liu (Xiamen University; Nanyang Technological University) · `[preprint]`

  Agentic reinforcement learning trains a model over long, multi-step tasks. Critic-free methods like GRPO estimate how good an action was from several sampled rollouts, dodging the memory cost of PPO's separate value network — but they assign credit poorly over long horizons and can suffer "advantage collapse." SAPO folds the policy and value function into one shared autoregressive backbone (predicting both at different points in the same generation), and adds a trajectory-level advantage estimator for per-turn credit. On ALFWorld and WebShop with Qwen2.5-1.5B/7B it beats PPO and GRPO by mean +15.1 and +12.1 points while cutting per-iteration runtime 33.2% versus PPO and dropping the separate critic entirely. This is a *distinct* line from the token-level credit-assignment work covered last edition, converging on the same problem from the value-estimation side.

  **Why it matters:** temporal credit assignment — figuring out *which* step in a long task deserved the reward — is the central difficulty in training agents, and doing it without a memory-hungry critic is exactly the lever that makes agentic RL affordable to run. _Submitted 20 Aug 2026._

- <a id="st-404d4f73fcfa" class="st-a"></a>**LLMs are still far from doing frontier theory research end-to-end** — [arXiv:2608.20153](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20153) · Dingzirui Wang, Xuanliang Zhang et al. (Harbin Institute of Technology) · `[preprint]`

  FormalTCS tests whether LLMs can do realistic theoretical-computer-science research: 175 instances drawn from papers accepted to STOC, FOCS, SODA, and COLT (the top theory venues) in 2025–2026, each with an expert-verified Lean formalization (Lean is a proof assistant that machine-checks mathematical proofs). The bottleneck is *autoformalization* — turning a natural-language claim into a formal theorem statement: the best model scores just 11.5 there, versus 28.6 (Pass@8) when instead asked to *prove* a human-provided formal statement. Worse, when the framework generates its own new claims, only 6 of 64 survive expert evaluation and proof — so "research taste" is a second wall beyond formalization.

  **Why it matters:** amid claims that models are nearing autonomous mathematical research, this pins down two concrete, separable failures — models can't reliably even *state* the problem formally, and can't reliably tell a good conjecture from a bad one. It's a precise, honestly-scoped map of the gap. _Submitted 20 Aug 2026._

## 🚀 New models, datasets & open weights

- <a id="st-5fe6d0c549c1" class="st-a"></a>**A robot foundation model that learns a new task from a single demo, no training step** — [generalistai.com, 19 Aug 2026](https://generalistai.com/blog/gen-1.5) `[new source]` · `[vendor PR]`

  Generalist AI released GEN-1.5, an "embodied foundation model" it describes as the first it knows of to show one-shot and few-shot learning of physical skills at scale. A single demonstration of 3–12 seconds is inserted into the model's context as a "physical prompt," and the robot attempts the task immediately, with no gradient update in between — the same in-context learning that makes language models pick up a pattern from a few examples, applied to physical action. It is a multimodal model that ingests video plus sensor, language, and proprioceptive (body-position) input and emits action trajectories at 100 Hz, holding 30 seconds of memory. Across 10 tasks (opening jars, unzipping pouches, retrieving money from a purse), one-shot in-context prompting averaged 59% success straight from pretraining; ten gradient steps on five minutes of data raised that to 83%. The company reports capabilities that emerged from pretraining rather than being trained for — sim-to-real transfer, novel tool use, ambidextrous work. Figures are company-reported and not yet independently reproduced.

  **Why it matters:** robotics' bottleneck has been data — every new skill traditionally means fresh teleoperation and retraining. If a pretrained model can genuinely acquire a usable skill from one short demo, that changes the economics of deploying robots, which is why commentators are floating a "ChatGPT moment for robotics" framing. Treat that framing as a claim to verify, not a settled result: the 59% one-shot number and the emergent-capability list are the vendor's own. _Announced 19 Aug 2026._

  Multiple outlets report Nvidia has agreed a **non-exclusive** $6 billion deal to license the "Model Factory" — the system Poolside (an AI coding-model startup, maker of the Laguna model) uses to build its models — alongside a separate $1 billion investment at a reported $12 billion pre-money valuation. Roughly 109 Poolside staff, largely researchers, are said to be getting Nvidia job offers, while the three founders stay to run Poolside independently. Note the structure, which the reporting stresses: this is *not* a clean acquisition or acqui-hire — the license is non-exclusive (Poolside can still sell the same technology elsewhere), and Poolside continues as a company. As of writing there is no first-party Nvidia or Poolside press release; the terms rest on sourced reporting. `[disputed]`

  **Why it matters:** it's the window's largest single AI-industry move, and the unusual shape is the story — a chip vendor paying billions to *license the model-building capability and hire the people*, while leaving the startup standing, rather than buying it outright. It echoes the "reverse-acquihire" pattern regulators have scrutinised in earlier AI deals (structure a talent-and-tech absorption to avoid a merger review), and points to Nvidia moving further up the stack from selling compute toward owning the recipe for frontier models. _Reported 20 Aug 2026._

- <a id="st-457b40d1bbe4" class="st-a"></a>**Anthropic plans to loosen its enterprise data-retention rule after customer pushback** — [Bloomberg, 20 Aug 2026](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-20/anthropic-plans-to-change-data-retention-policy-for-advanced-ai) · [the-decoder, 21 Aug 2026](https://the-decoder.com/anthropic-changes-data-retention-policy-after-enterprise-pushback/)

  Per sourced reporting, Anthropic intends to let business customers keep the data its most capable models retain **on their own cloud infrastructure** rather than on Anthropic's — while still requiring a 30-day retention window. This walks back part of a June policy under which Anthropic said it would retain all customer data for its more powerful models for 30 days to detect novel AI-enabled cyberattacks; the company itself acknowledged at the time that the rule would be "unpopular with customers who have come to expect zero retention." The new safety system, developed with 100-plus customers including Salesforce, is expected to roll out later this year. This is a reported plan, not yet a published Anthropic policy. `[single-source]`

  **Why it matters:** data retention is a live procurement and compliance question for anyone deploying frontier models in a regulated or privacy-sensitive setting — including Swiss and EU enterprises bound by strict data-handling rules. The move shows the safety-vs-confidentiality tension being negotiated in the open, with a "keep it on your own cloud" compromise as the emerging middle ground (OpenAI is reported to be testing a similar "private safety processing" approach). _Reported 20 Aug 2026._
