### Sunlight turns seawater and air into ammonia {#st-0fe28f952d34}
**[Nature Chemistry](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41557-026-02218-2)** · D. Zidouhia, T. Tassaing et al. (Univ. Bordeaux; CNRS) · online 25 Aug 2026
Making ammonia (NH₃, the basis of nitrogen fertiliser) normally means the Haber–Bosch process — high heat, high pressure, and a large slice of the world's industrial energy. A Bordeaux team instead drives the reaction with light. Cheap iron(II) salts, hit with ultraviolet light, release solvated electrons (free electrons dissolved in the liquid) that reduce a molybdenum catalyst, which grabs N₂ from the air and turns it into ammonia; the spent iron(III) then absorbs more UV, oxidises water and regenerates the iron(II) — closing a self-sustaining loop. They report a 5.0% quantum yield (the fraction of absorbed photons doing useful chemistry) at room temperature and pressure, rising to 10.8% under higher N₂ pressure, and — the notable part — it still runs in unpurified seawater and in ambient air. The whole cycle is genuinely uphill energetically (+329 kJ/mol), light-driven like natural photosynthesis.
*Why it matters:* A proof of concept that fertiliser chemistry might one day be powered by sunlight and seawater instead of fossil-fired furnaces — though the modest, UV-limited yields are a long way from a practical process.

### Physicists film a water molecule splitting into hydrogen {#st-eb0a928b1626}
**[arXiv:2608.23292](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23292)** · C. Cheng, C.-H. Yuen et al. (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]` `[single-source]` · submitted 24 Aug 2026
When a water molecule is ionised (an electron knocked out), its two hydrogen atoms can pair up and leave together as molecular hydrogen (H₂) — one of the simplest chemical reactions there is, yet no one had watched the electrons and nuclei move together as it happens. Using correlated electron–ion imaging with few-femtosecond (10⁻¹⁵ s) laser pulses on heavy water (D₂O), backed by simulations, the team reconstructed the full pathway. They find the reaction is indirect, running through three channels with formation times around 34 and 72 femtoseconds, and — the striking result — the molecule must first spontaneously break its own symmetry: only a random, lopsided wobble lets it hop between electronic states at a "conical intersection" (a point where two energy surfaces touch and a molecule can switch states) and fuse the two hydrogens before the oxygen–hydrogen bond finishes breaking.
*Why it matters:* A frame-by-frame look at the elementary step behind both radiation damage in water and hydrogen production — showing that even the simplest reactions can hinge on fleeting, spontaneous asymmetry.

### A recipe for weaving knotted magnets out of monopoles {#st-15ad1723172b}
**[arXiv:2608.24116](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.24116)** · S. Kasai, K. Shimizu et al. (University of Tokyo; RIKEN; Hiroshima University) · `[preprint]` `[single-source]` · submitted 25 Aug 2026
*The stakes:* magnetic hopfions are three-dimensional, knotted textures in a material's spins — a step up in complexity from the flat, whirlpool-like skyrmions already studied as candidate memory bits — and they are predicted to have unusual dynamics, but there is no reliable way to create them on demand. *A concrete picture:* imagine the atomic spins as tiny compass needles; a hopfion twists their directions into a closed knot that cannot be smoothly undone. This simulation study proposes a clean route: start from a "toron" (a simpler 3D texture that hosts an emergent monopole–antimonopole pair — point-like sources of magnetic field), then run an electric current that annihilates the pair, converting the toron into a hopfion. The starting toron's length sets how many hopfions form, and the current's direction sets the knot's handedness. *Honest scope:* this is micromagnetic theory only, with no experiment yet — but it hands experimenters concrete control knobs to try.
*Why it matters:* If it works on the bench, controllable knotted spin textures could become a new kind of stable, information-carrying object for spintronics.

### An effective handle on a hard conjecture in number theory {#st-f865a009afee}
**[arXiv:2608.24474](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.24474)** · N. Dogra, S. Pandit (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]` `[single-source]` · submitted 25 Aug 2026
*The stakes:* the Zilber–Pink conjecture is one of the big open problems in arithmetic geometry. It predicts that "unlikely intersections" — rare coincidences where a curve meets special subgroups of a higher-dimensional space more often than plain dimension-counting allows — are finite and controllable, and it would unify and vastly extend classical finiteness theorems (of Manin–Mumford / André–Oort type) about where rational and torsion points can sit. *A concrete anchor:* picture a winding curve drawn through a grid of "special" points; the conjecture says it can pass through only finitely many of the improbable ones. This work gives an explicit, effective bound on the size of that "Zilber–Pink locus" for curves in abelian varieties after reduction modulo a large prime, plus a fresh proof that the locus is finite. *Honest scope:* it makes an existing finiteness result effective — you can actually compute the bound — for curves; a concrete advance, not a proof of the full conjecture.
*Why it matters:* "Effective" results are the ones that can eventually become algorithms and explicit checks, so pinning down real bounds is how abstract finiteness theorems turn usable.

### The FDA approves the first RAS-targeted drug for pancreatic cancer {#st-0ffd3a98b7f8}
**[FDA](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-class-targeted-therapy-metastatic-pancreatic-cancer)** `[new source]` · trial detail via **[Revolution Medicines](https://ir.revmed.com/news-releases/news-release-details/revolution-medicines-new-drug-application-daraxonrasib-accepted)** `[new source]`; corroborated by [STAT](https://www.statnews.com/2026/08/26/pancreatic-cancer-drug-rasonque-approved/) · 26 Aug 2026
On 26 August the US Food and Drug Administration approved daraxonrasib (brand name Rasonque), the first drug to target RAS — the mutated gene that drives roughly 90% of pancreatic tumours — for adults with previously treated metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC, the most common and lethal form). It is a "RAS(ON) multi-selective" inhibitor: rather than blocking a single mutant, it binds the active, switched-on state of several RAS variants at once. In the pivotal Phase 3 RASolute 302 trial (NCT06625320, 500 patients), median overall survival was 13.2 months on the drug versus 6.7 months on standard chemotherapy — close to a doubling. The FDA granted Breakthrough Therapy and Orphan Drug designations and cleared the drug 6.5 months ahead of its review deadline; the most common side effects were rash, diarrhoea, mouth inflammation and nausea.
*Why it matters:* RAS was called "undruggable" for four decades, and pancreatic cancer is among the deadliest common cancers, so the first RAS drug to reach it is a genuine milestone — even as absolute median survival stays around 13 months. European relevance: Revolution Medicines says the European Medicines Agency is running an expedited assessment, so a filing here may follow.

### A recombinant shingles vaccine is linked to fewer heart problems {#st-beef2803af35}
**[Nature Medicine](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04606-0)** · F. Corsi-Zuelli, M. Taquet et al. (University of Oxford; University of Birmingham) · published 26 Aug 2026
When the US abruptly switched from the older live shingles vaccine to the newer recombinant one (marketed as Shingrix), it created a natural experiment: people vaccinated just before and just after the switch were otherwise similar, which sidesteps the "healthy-vaccinee" bias — healthier people tend to get vaccinated, muddying earlier studies — that has dogged this question. Comparing cardiovascular outcomes in adults aged 60 and over, the Oxford–Birmingham team found the recombinant vaccine was associated with a 9% lower cardiovascular burden over seven years (a "restricted-mean-time-lost" ratio of 0.91, 95% CI 0.88–0.95), with significant reductions in ischaemic heart disease and heart failure in both sexes. The authors are careful: this is observational, the effect is modest and fades over time, and they explicitly call for randomised trials to test whether it is truly causal.
*Why it matters:* Shingles vaccination is already recommended for older adults; a real cardiovascular benefit would strengthen that case — but this is an association, not proof, and shouldn't be oversold.

### A driver-agnostic map of the mutations that mark ageing blood {#st-029e88739403}
**[medRxiv](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.08.21.26361040)** · P. S. Reddy, K. N. Conneely, J. S. Weinstock (Emory University) · `[preprint]` · posted 25 Aug 2026
Clonal haematopoiesis (CH) — the age-related expansion of blood stem-cell clones — raises the risk of leukaemia, heart disease and death, but most clones carry no known "driver" mutation, so studies that define CH by drivers miss much of it. This team instead counts "passenger" mutations: harmless hitchhiker mutations whose sheer number marks how far a clone has expanded, giving a continuous, more statistically powerful measure. Across 791,067 blood whole-genome sequences from the UK Biobank and the US All of Us program, a multi-ancestry genome-wide association study (GWAS — a scan linking DNA variants to a trait) found 81 genetic loci tied to passenger-mutation burden, 42 of them new, and flagged rare-variant regulators including MBD2, PRKACB and PUF60. Passenger burden itself tracked strongly with later blood cancers and mortality.
*Why it matters:* A powerful, driver-free way to study why some people's blood ages faster than others — though as an unreviewed preprint of statistical associations, it needs replication and peer review before any clinical use.

## 🌌 Astronomy & cosmology

### Interstellar comet 2I/Borisov looks like no comet on record {#st-4613de4b4194}
**[arXiv:2608.23701](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23701)** · A. N. Bair, D. G. Schleicher et al. (Lowell Observatory; University of Maryland; Villanova University) · `[preprint]` · submitted 24 Aug 2026
2I/Borisov, discovered in 2019, was the first clearly active comet known to arrive from another star system. A full reduction of Lowell Observatory imaging, spectroscopy and narrowband photometry taken over September–December 2019 finds that its carbon-chain molecules (C₂ and C₃) surged about fourfold relative to cyanide (CN) as it neared the Sun — a trend with distance from the Sun unmatched anywhere in Lowell's 50-year database of 230 comets. Water production peaked at 4.5×10²⁶ molecules per second, with unusually high nitrogen- and hydrogen-bearing ratios, and an almost symmetric gas cloud implying the entire sunlit surface was venting. Set against other groups' data, its chemistry looks unlike any comet born in our own Solar System.
*Why it matters:* Comets are frozen leftovers of the systems that made them, so an interstellar one with genuinely alien chemistry is a direct sample of how planet-building differs around other stars. (This is a late, thorough analysis of 2019 data, not a new detection.)

### Flickering starlight hints at "exocomets" watering a planet nursery {#st-d72ef33cfa2e}
**[arXiv:2608.23208](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23208)** · A. Novais, H. J. Hoeijmakers et al. (University of Copenhagen; ESO) · `[preprint]` `[single-source]` · submitted 24 Aug 2026
PDS 70 is a young star famous for two planets caught in the act of forming, and JWST recently found water vapour in its inner disc — but where that water comes from was unclear. Mining archival spectra from ESO's HARPS instrument (a high-precision spectrograph in Chile), the team found sodium absorption lines that flicker erratically in strength and velocity from night to night, the fingerprint of dense gas clumps drifting across the star's face. Their favoured explanation: planetesimals (comet- to asteroid-sized bodies) on stretched, plunging orbits that sublimate as they graze the star — "exocomets" — which could be ferrying water inward to the zone where rocky planets form. They stress this is one interpretation among several the data allow.
*Why it matters:* It offers a concrete, observable mechanism for delivering water to forming planets — akin to the kind of process that may have helped fill Earth's oceans. European angle: built on ESO/HARPS data with European-led authorship.

### JWST's verdict on a nearby Earth-sized world: bare rock {#st-83a8c65a3558}
**[arXiv:2608.24803](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.24803)** · M. Senobar, S. Ataiee (Ferdowsi University of Mashhad) · `[preprint]` `[single-source]` `[disputed]` · submitted 25 Aug 2026
GJ 1132 b is an Earth-sized planet (1.13 Earth radii) orbiting a small, cool star — exactly the kind of world astronomers most want to test for an atmosphere. Combining JWST, Hubble and ground-based transmission spectra (starlight filtered through any atmosphere during a transit) into one broad 0.71–5.16 micron dataset and running Bayesian atmospheric fits, the authors find that no model with an atmosphere beats a flat, featureless line. A marginal hint of ammonia vanished once a previously flagged, contested JWST visit was excluded. The data favour a bare rock with no detectable atmosphere, though a very thin or hidden one cannot be fully ruled out.
*Why it matters:* Whether small planets around small stars can hold onto atmospheres is central to which worlds could be habitable, and each clean "airless" verdict sharpens the picture. (A single-team reanalysis of archival data whose result hinges on one disputed observation.)
