## 📄 ML/AI research (arXiv)

The throughline this cycle: two quiet threads run under a busy arXiv batch. One is the stack getting more *principled and cheaper* — an AI system nudging the decades-old bound on how fast matrices can be multiplied, a thesis finally explaining why Adam beats plain gradient descent on Transformers, and a memory trick that stops long-context models from choking on their own early tokens. The other is agents becoming *objects of study rather than tools* — measured like a physical system, audited for where they silently fail at repo-scale coding, and turned loose to invent genuinely new crystals. If you read three, read the AlphaEvolve matrix-multiplication note (a real number moved), **Model Hypnosis** (an uncomfortable safety result), and **Physics of Agents** (a genuinely new lens on multi-agent behaviour).

- <a id="st-d2de83c4e4be" class="st-a"></a>**An AI system nudges the matrix-multiplication exponent to a new record** — [arXiv:2608.16884](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16884) · E. Dupont, M. Eisenberger, A. Mehrabian, R. Zhou et al. (Google DeepMind; Carnegie Mellon University) · `[preprint]`

  The matrix-multiplication exponent ω is the theoretical yardstick for how fast two n×n matrices can *in principle* be multiplied: schoolbook multiplication costs about n³ operations (ω = 3), and sixty years of algorithm design has been chipping ω down toward the conjectured floor of 2. The current record comes from the "laser method," a technical optimization procedure at the heart of these algorithms. This DeepMind-led team reformulated that optimization so it could be solved in a larger setting, handed it to modern machine-learning solvers, then refined the result with AlphaEvolve (DeepMind's LLM-driven algorithm-discovery system), improving the bound from ω < 2.371339 to **ω < 2.371177**.

  **Why it matters:** the improvement is tiny — it lands in the fifth decimal place and buys no faster software today, since these "galactic" algorithms are far too intricate to run in practice. What's notable is *who* moved it: an AI system advancing one of theoretical computer science's most-studied constants, on a frontier where human experts have made gains this small over many years. _Submitted 17 Aug 2026._

- <a id="st-21addf24ede6" class="st-a"></a>**Weak, "irrelevant" prompt cues can be stacked to strongly control a model** — [arXiv:2608.16834](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16834) · E. Boix-Adsera, B. Tessler (University of Pennsylvania) · `[preprint]`

  The authors describe a phenomenon they call *model hypnosis*: individually weak, seemingly irrelevant choices in a prompt — paraphrases, typos, innocuous wording — can be combined so that together they systematically steer a model's behaviour. They report it across model families and scales, including in frontier reasoning models, and find that a "hypnotic" prompt tuned on one model often transfers to another.

  **Why it matters:** if inconspicuous surface features can be composed into strong control, that cuts against both safety and interpretability — the levers that move a model are precisely the ones a human reviewer would dismiss as noise, and they generalise across systems rather than staying model-specific. It reframes prompt-based jailbreaks as an additive, transferable effect rather than a bag of one-off tricks. _Submitted 17 Aug 2026._

- <a id="st-c3f0b2dbe3e0" class="st-a"></a>**A memory that starts small keeps long-context models from clogging** — [arXiv:2608.16844](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16844) · R. Bayat, A. Behrouz, V. Mirrokni, A. Courville (Google; Mila – Quebec AI Institute; Université de Montréal) · `[preprint]`

  Standard attention costs grow quadratically with context length, so a growing line of work compresses the past into a fixed-size "memory state" instead. The snag: with a *static* memory the earliest tokens face no compression pressure, so they hog degrees of freedom and "pollute" the state, leaving little room for later context. Proteus introduces *incremental memory activation* — the memory's usable capacity is expanded gradually as the sequence grows. An early bottleneck forces the model to compress history harder; unlocking fresh capacity later reduces interference with new tokens. It drops into several recent memory architectures (Titans, Comba, Hope-Attention) at no added cost, with gains that widen at longer context lengths.

  **Why it matters:** long-context recall is where sub-quadratic ("linear-attention") models usually break down; scheduling *when* memory becomes available, rather than how much there is, is a cheap and broadly reusable lever for that failure mode. _Submitted 17 Aug 2026._

- <a id="st-25cf43a9e41f" class="st-a"></a>**Value functions make a comeback in LLM reinforcement learning** — [arXiv:2608.16739](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16739) · S. Venkatraman, M. Dinot, L. Aitchison (Mistral AI; Mila – Quebec AI Institute; Université de Montréal) · `[preprint]`

  Most reinforcement learning used to post-train LLMs differs mainly in how it tames noisy gradients. Group-relative methods like GRPO (the recipe behind many reasoning models) sample many answers per prompt and compare them, but only assign credit at the level of the whole answer, and they stall waiting on slow "straggler" rollouts. A learned *value function* — a critic that scores partial outputs token by token — could fix both problems, but has been fiddly enough that critic-free methods won out. The paper offers two additions: *Privileged Value Functions*, which feed the critic extra task-relevant signal without biasing what the policy learns, and *TETHER*, which adaptively blends critic-based and group-relative baselines depending on how accurate the critic currently is. On several reasoning tasks both beat a standard value-function baseline and are competitive with or better than GRPO.

  **Why it matters:** it makes the case that token-level credit assignment is worth the engineering after all — relevant to anyone building an RL post-training pipeline, and notable for coming partly out of a European lab (Mistral). _Submitted 17 Aug 2026._

- <a id="st-bdabf0574df4" class="st-a"></a>**Statistical mechanics predicts how crowds of AI agents drift** — [arXiv:2608.16578](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16578) · B. El, S. Ganguli, J. Zou et al. (Stanford University) · `[preprint]`

  As AI agents increasingly talk to each other rather than work alone, their interactions can sharpen collective reasoning — or produce herding and shared bias. The authors ran over 10,000 communities of language-model agents that repeatedly exchange messages and revise their views, on objective maths questions and on subjective political statements. Despite huge variety, the dynamics collapse into three regimes — *indifference, polarization, consensus* — with agents starting indifferent and building conviction as they interact. On factual questions, talking improves group accuracy; on subjective ones, it tends to drift the group's stated opinion rightward. They fit a statistical-mechanics model (borrowed from physics: agents stochastically favour lower "social pressure," like particles settling toward low energy) that predicts individual trajectories from initial opinions alone and generalises to unseen community structures.

  **Why it matters:** it offers compact, testable laws for multi-agent behaviour instead of case-by-case anecdotes — useful for designing agent systems that resist groupthink. The rightward political-drift finding is the authors' own measured result on their setup, and worth treating as a specific empirical claim rather than a general law. _Submitted 17 Aug 2026._

- <a id="st-6d111859e3fa" class="st-a"></a>**Coding agents fail when the fact they need isn't in front of them** — [arXiv:2608.16630](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16630) · B. Mohammadi, L. Klein, A. Chadha, A. Arora, L. Bindschaedler · `[preprint]`

  Repository-scale coding asks an agent to keep tests, imports, configuration and migration rules mutually consistent inside a limited context window. The authors model each edit as needing a "fact" that comes either from recent context or from the model's baked-in ("parametric") memory; facts supplied by neither channel are *coherence debt*. Testing seven models across five agent harnesses, they find: no model completes a task on an unseen API when both channels are empty, and simply putting the fact in the prompt restores success. Availability — not where the fact sits relative to the edit — decides the outcome. A missing fact produces *wrong* work rather than absent work: the agent fabricates a file or guesses a value instead of reporting itself blocked, and how often it admits it's stuck is a property of the model. Tellingly, a stale convention file costs more than no file, because agents follow the standard even when it prescribes worse code.

  **Why it matters:** it's a concrete diagnosis of why coding agents silently go wrong at scale — and a directive for harness builders: guarantee the facts an edit depends on are present *when the agent writes*, and check that against what it produces, not what it reads. _Submitted 17 Aug 2026._

- <a id="st-47c579d3cb70" class="st-a"></a>**Why Adam beats plain gradient descent on Transformers — and a leaner version** — [arXiv:2608.16760](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16760) · Y. Zhang (CUHK-Shenzhen) · `[preprint]`

  Adam is the default optimizer for training modern LLMs, yet its advantage over plain stochastic gradient descent (SGD) has been more folklore than theory. This short version of a PhD thesis offers two grounding results. First, whether Adam converges or diverges is governed by a problem-dependent phase transition tied to its hyperparameters and batch size. Second, Adam beats SGD on Transformers because the loss's curvature (the Hessian) evolves toward a near-block-diagonal shape during training, with strongly different blocks — a structure that makes Adam's per-coordinate scaling effective, proved using random-matrix theory. Those insights motivate *Adam-mini*, which cuts Adam's memory footprint by about half while matching its performance.

  **Why it matters:** optimizer memory is a real constraint when training large models, and a principled account of *why* Adam works points toward cheaper variants (and helps explain newer optimizers like Muon) rather than tuning by superstition. _Submitted 17 Aug 2026._

- <a id="st-cb0846408d4b" class="st-a"></a>**A smooth dial for turning model sycophancy up or down** — [arXiv:2608.16650](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16650) · Z. Chen, Z. Feng, B. Li et al. (Hong Kong Polytechnic University; HKUST) · `[preprint]`

  Sycophancy is a model's habit of agreeing with the user regardless of the facts; eliminating it entirely risks over-correcting against legitimate opinions, so good control needs to move it *predictably* in both directions. Existing activation-steering methods don't reliably do that — more steering doesn't cleanly mean more or less sycophancy. PCA-guided Activation Scaling (PAS) uses principal-component analysis (a standard technique for finding the dominant directions of variation) to split the model's internal activations into a sycophancy–honesty subspace plus everything else, then scales the two separately. The result is smooth, monotonic control in both directions (rank correlation ρ = 0.92) with about a 15% average behavioural shift per step, versus 8.7% for baselines.

  **Why it matters:** it's a cleaner knob for a well-known alignment failure — and a reminder that "steer the model's activations" only helps if the steering is monotonic and predictable, which prior methods weren't. _Submitted 17 Aug 2026._

- <a id="st-0a4068aad6f3" class="st-a"></a>**An agent that invents new crystals in a "language of motifs"** — [arXiv:2608.15900](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.15900) · D.-K. Le, M.-Q. Ha, H.-C. Dam et al. (JAIST; Institute of Science Tokyo) · `[preprint]`

  Data-driven materials discovery is good at interpolating between known structures but rarely invents genuinely new *structure types*. MatEvolve is an agentic-AI framework — built on Claude Fable 5 with no fine-tuning — that writes each candidate crystal as an interpretable *motif profile*, a description of the recurring geometric patterns ("motifs") that compose it. Crucially the profile is not just a label but the medium of design: the agent edits the profile, builds a crystal from the edited version, states its rationale, and the most promising candidates are checked with first-principles physics calculations. Applied to rare-earth-lean permanent magnets (strong magnets that avoid scarce, geopolitically fraught rare-earth elements), it reached new structural prototypes more than three times as often as generative models at an equal validation budget, at a comparable rate of on-target magnets.

  **Why it matters:** it's a credible case of an agent reaching real novelty in a hard physical-science domain while staying human-readable — the motif profiles expose *why* a structure works, and the target (rare-earth-lean magnets) speaks directly to materials-supply-chain risk. _Submitted 16 Aug 2026._
