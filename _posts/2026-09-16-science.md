### A quantum system keeps a "birthmark" of where it began {#st-875e28437fbb}
**[Phys. Rev. X (2026), 10.1103/dhzb-28rb](https://doi.org/10.1103/dhzb-28rb)** · A. M. Graf, S. Atwood, M. Xiao, R. Ketzmerick, E. J. Heller (Harvard; TU Dresden) · pub. 2026-09-10
Classical chaos erases history: a ball bouncing on an irregular billiard table eventually visits everywhere with equal probability, so its long-run position tells you nothing about where it started. The authors show the quantum version does *not* fully forget. For a wave packet launched inside a chaotic "stadium" billiard (a racetrack-shaped box that is a textbook model of classical chaos), the long-time-averaged probability of finding the system back in its exact starting state is at least twice as high as for any unrelated state — an enhancement that survives even in the infinite-time limit. The effect is pure quantum interference (the overlapping waves reinforce for the origin state), and it rises to a factor of ~8 when the packet starts on a short repeating orbit, connecting the result to "quantum scarring" — enhanced probability along unstable periodic paths, a phenomenon Heller himself discovered in the 1980s. The team calls the residual memory a *quantum birthmark* and demonstrates it in simulations of the stadium billiard.
*Why it matters:* Ergodicity — the assumption that a system explores all its states evenly over time — is the bedrock of statistical mechanics; this puts a hard, universal limit (a factor of ≥2) on how completely a quantum system can lose track of its past, sharpening where the classical picture of chaos breaks down.

### Physicists forge a new phase of "ice giant" ice in the lab {#st-3ec528ba5f70}
**[Phys. Rev. Lett. (2026), 10.1103/sdrk-3m4t](https://doi.org/10.1103/sdrk-3m4t)** · A. Forestier, G. Weck, S. Ninet, G. Garbarino, M. Mezouar, F. Datchi, P. Loubeyre (CEA; ESRF; Sorbonne University) · pub. 2026-09-09
Uranus and Neptune have bizarre, lopsided magnetic fields, and the leading explanation is a deep mantle of *superionic ice* — a state where hydrogen ions (protons) flow like a liquid through a fixed crystalline cage of oxygen atoms, part-solid and part-liquid at once. Squeezing ultrapure water in a diamond-anvil cell (two gem tips pressed together) while laser-heating it, the French-led team reached 80–229 gigapascals (roughly a million-plus atmospheres) and up to 2,630 K, then read the crystal structure from X-ray diffraction patterns at the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility. Above ~1,800 K and ~160 GPa they saw superionic ice switch from a face-centred-cubic to a hexagonal-close-packed arrangement of its oxygen lattice — a long-predicted phase now directly observed at conditions that actually occur inside the ice giants.
*Why it matters:* The two packing geometries can conduct heat and charge differently, so pinning down which one fills a planet's mantle feeds directly into models of how Uranus, Neptune and their many exoplanet cousins generate their magnetic fields.

## 🧬 Biology, medicine & neuroscience

### One brain implant decodes speech and gesture at the same time {#st-560c31d84281}
**[Nature Neuroscience (2026), 10.1038/s41593-026-02446-2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-026-02446-2)** · S. C. Brosler, J. R. Liu, A. B. Silva et al. (UCSF) · pub. 2026-09-14
Human communication is not just words — we nod, point and shrug. Existing brain–computer interfaces (BCIs — implants that read neural activity and turn intent into output) have restored one channel at a time: intended speech, a cursor, or a robotic arm. The UCSF team reports the first system to decode intended speech *and* gesture simultaneously in a person with severe paralysis, translating brain signals into on-screen text while driving a personalized animated avatar to move, within seconds of intent. The hard part is that the neural signals for speaking and gesturing overlap and shift when the two are attempted together; the decoder is trained to separate them. The authors and outside commenters frame it explicitly as a proof of concept, not a clinical device.
*Why it matters:* Restoring the non-verbal layer of communication — not just the words — is a concrete step toward BCIs expressive enough for everyday use by people who cannot speak or move.

### The largest map yet of the brain's overlooked "microproteins," with an Alzheimer's signal {#st-6f113366bc3f}
**[Nature Aging (2026), 10.1038/s43587-026-01207-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43587-026-01207-x)** · B. Miller, E. Vieira de Souza, C. Lau et al. (Salk Institute) · pub. 2026-09-14
Microproteins — proteins shorter than 150 amino acids — slip past standard gene- and protein-sequencing methods, so they are largely absent from reference databases. Combining several detection techniques on post-mortem samples of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (a region tied to cognitive control) from donors with and without Alzheimer's disease, the Salk-led team catalogued more than 4,300 microproteins — the largest such brain atlas to date — and found dozens with altered abundance in Alzheimer's tissue. The result is an association in donated tissue, not evidence that these molecules cause the disease; its value is the reference map itself, which lets others test whether specific microproteins drive neurodegeneration.
*Why it matters:* If a whole layer of small proteins has been invisible to standard tools, disease mechanisms and drug targets may be hiding in it — this atlas is the starting map for looking.

## 🌌 Astronomy & cosmology

### First fast radio burst caught below 300 MHz without knowing where to look {#st-89e397594bd4}
**[arXiv:2609.17275](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17275)** · C. Di Pietrantonio, M. Sokolowski, C. W. James, N. D. R. Bhat et al. (Pawsey Supercomputing Research Centre; ICRAR / Curtin University; SKAO) · `[preprint]` · sub. 2026-09-15
Fast radio bursts (FRBs) are millisecond flashes of radio waves from far outside our galaxy, and their engines remain unknown. Almost all of the several thousand known were found above 400 MHz; low-frequency searches are punishing because a burst's *dispersion delay* — the frequency-dependent lag as the signal crosses intervening plasma — stretches to tens of seconds and smears the pulse, making conventional searches computationally prohibitive. Until now the only confirmed detection below 300 MHz came from a campaign deliberately pointed at a source already known to flash at higher frequencies. Using a wide-field, imaging-based survey with the Murchison Widefield Array in Western Australia, the team reports the first *non-targeted* FRB detection below 300 MHz. The implied event rate matches what higher-frequency surveys see — meaning wide-field low-frequency surveys can harvest an unbiased population rather than a lucky few, and that severe scattering or absorption is not, after all, hiding these bursts.
*Why it matters:* It opens a new, cheaper observational window on FRBs and undercuts models that assumed the low-frequency sky was blank — useful just as the much larger SKA-Low telescope comes online in the same part of Australia.

### JWST's "little red dots": a rival explanation lands mid-debate {#st-10340a59db75}
**[arXiv:2609.12049](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12049)** · D. J. Whalen, M. A. Latif, K. Topalakis, F. Cullen, D. Nandal, S. Khochfar (University of Portsmouth; UAE University; University of Edinburgh) · `[preprint]` · sub. 2026-09-10
"Little red dots" are compact, blindingly red points of light that JWST finds scattered across the early universe; their V-shaped spectra and tiny sizes (100–200 parsecs) defy easy classification, and interpretations range from dense star clusters to dust-shrouded black holes to the exotic "black hole star" — a bloated envelope of hydrogen shining like billions of suns with a black hole buried in its core. This week's paper argues for a different picture: the dots are *direct-collapse black hole* galaxies, where gas collapses straight into a massive black hole (skipping the usual star-then-supernova route) that is still wrapped in the dense disk that made it. Cosmological simulations reproduce the observed spectra because the disk's high central density traps X-rays and imprints the telltale hydrogen (Balmer) absorption, while UV and infrared light partly escapes; the models also match the objects' abundances and a specific dot, RUBIES-EGS-42046. It is one entry in an actively contested field — a Quanta feature this week catalogues the "polarized" state of the debate — and, like its rivals, rests on modelling a handful of pixels of light.
*Why it matters:* How little red dots formed bears on one of JWST's biggest puzzles — how supermassive black holes grew so fast so early — and the arrival of competing, testable models is how that question gets settled rather than asserted.

## 🧠 Why it matters

- Two of this week's astronomy results cut the same way: what a single detection *seems* to show can mislead until a wider or deeper survey reframes it. The Murchison array found low-frequency FRBs at the rate higher-frequency surveys predicted — killing the idea that the low band was intrinsically empty — while the little-red-dots fight turns on wringing whole formation histories from a few pixels, where each new spectrum or simulation shifts the odds. In both, instruments and statistics, not any one image, are doing the arbitration.
