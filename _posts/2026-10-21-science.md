### Google grows high-fidelity magic states on a quantum chip {#st-37f473ceb56d}
**[Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aee7961)** · E. Rosenfeld, C. Gidney et al. (Google Research) · published Thursday 15 October 2026 · context: **[arXiv:2610.06828](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06828)** · R. Mehta, V. Menon, H. Zhou · submitted Monday 5 October 2026 · `[preprint]`
Error-corrected quantum computers can do the "easy" class of operations (Clifford gates) cheaply on encoded qubits, but those alone cannot outperform a classical computer. Universal computing also needs at least one non-Clifford gate, such as the T gate. The usual route is to prepare special "magic states" and consume them, and making them clean enough has meant magic state distillation: a factory that burns many noisy copies to produce one good one, often the most expensive part of a fault-tolerant design. Magic state cultivation, proposed by Google researchers in 2024, instead grows a single magic state inside a small error-correcting code, repeatedly checks it and throws it away at the first sign of an error, then transfers ("code-switches") it into a standard surface code. This paper is the first experimental test of the whole procedure on a superconducting processor. Cultivation cut the error by a factor of 40, reaching a magic-state fidelity of at least 0.9999(1), bounded with a fault-tolerant measurement protocol the team developed for the purpose. The cost is visible in the numbers: only 8% of attempts were kept. A separate theory preprint from early October argues that realistic, non-destructive checking can make cultivation's logical error rates orders of magnitude worse at the costs previously reported, so the overhead question is not closed.
*Why it matters:* This moves the cheapest known recipe for the hardest ingredient of a useful quantum computer from simulation to hardware. A 92% discard rate is affordable only if attempts are fast and cheap, which is the next thing to establish.

### COHERENT makes its most precise measurement of neutrinos bouncing off whole nuclei {#st-08f00a33bab6}
**[Physical Review Letters](https://doi.org/10.1103/999m-b763)** · COHERENT Collaboration, M. Adhikari, M. Ahn et al. (University of South Dakota; Seoul National University; Florida State University) · published Tuesday 20 October 2026
Neutrinos interact so weakly that detectors usually need tonnes of material. At low energies, though, a neutrino can scatter off an entire nucleus at once, with all its protons and neutrons responding together. This "coherent elastic neutrino-nucleus scattering" (CEvNS) is much more likely than other interactions but leaves only a tiny nuclear recoil to detect. COHERENT first observed it in 2017. Here the collaboration used Ge-Mini, a germanium detector array at Oak Ridge's Spallation Neutron Source, a pulsed neutrino source whose energy and timing are well understood. From 4.69×10²² protons on target they counted 124 (+14/−12) events and measured a cross-section 0.98 ± 0.10 (statistical) ± 0.10 (systematic) times the Standard Model value. They describe it as the most precise CEvNS measurement to date. The source's timing also let them separate muon-neutrino and electron-neutrino scattering rates, tightening limits on "non-standard" neutrino interactions carried by heavy new particles.
*Why it matters:* The Standard Model passes again, now at roughly 15% precision. The same recoils form the "neutrino fog" that next-generation dark-matter detectors will hit, so pinning down the rate on germanium helps those experiments too.

### A levitated nanoparticle feels the kick of single gas molecules {#st-a7c9233e9eaf}
**[Physical Review Letters](https://doi.org/10.1103/xtzh-6t1f)** · Y.-H. Tseng, C. A. Hardy et al. (Yale University) · published Monday 19 October 2026 · preprint [arXiv:2604.18371](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.18371) · with an APS [Physics Viewpoint by J. A. Zielińska, 19 Oct](https://physics.aps.org/articles/v19/136)
A glass nanoparticle held in a laser beam in vacuum ("optically levitated") is isolated from almost everything, which makes it an extremely sensitive force sensor. The Yale group let individual krypton, xenon and sulphur hexafluoride molecules hit such a particle and resolved each collision as a separate momentum kick, with reconstructed impulses as small as 200 keV/c. That is roughly the momentum a single heavy molecule at room temperature carries. The measured rates matched theory and gave the partial pressure of each gas directly. The shape of the kick spectrum also revealed properties of the particle's surface, including its temperature.
*Why it matters:* Counting collisions one by one is a new way to measure pressure from first principles, without calibrating against another gauge. The same sensitivity is what levitated-sensor proposals need to detect recoils from dark-matter particles or neutrinos.

### Fast warming could tip two East Antarctic basins before their thresholds {#st-a2ddd9151399}
**[Nature Geoscience](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41561-026-02117-8)** · J. Swierczek-Jereczek, J. Blasco et al. (Universidad Complutense de Madrid; CSIC; Alfred Wegener Institute) · published Tuesday 20 October 2026
Where Antarctic ice rests on bedrock below sea level and the bed slopes downward inland, retreat can become self-sustaining: the further the grounding line (where ice lifts off the bed and floats) retreats, the thicker the ice at the edge and the faster it flows out. This "marine ice-sheet instability" is usually studied as a threshold, a warming level past which retreat runs away. As ice thins, the bedrock slowly rises back up ("glacial isostatic adjustment"), which pushes against retreat. The Madrid–AWI team coupled an ice-sheet model to a bedrock model and sampled the uncertain viscosity of the mantle underneath. If warming is fast compared with that rebound, the stabilising uplift arrives too late, and retreat can start below the equilibrium threshold. This is "rate-induced tipping". West Antarctica is unlikely to tip this way because its mantle is runny enough to rebound quickly. The Recovery and Aurora subglacial basins in East Antarctica are likely to tip this way even at warming rates lower than today's. Depending on mantle viscosity, the effect lowers the critical global warming level by up to 0.5 K.
*Why it matters:* East Antarctica is usually seen as the stable part of the continent. This is a model result, but it says the speed of warming matters as well as its peak, and it singles out two basins that hold a large amount of ice.

### Ancient sea-ice records point to less Arctic winter ice than models project {#st-89ab73501f86}
**[Nature Geoscience](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41561-026-02123-w)** · C. Y. Fu, M. B. Osman, M. A. Aquino-López (University of Cambridge; CIMAT) · published Friday 16 October 2026
Climate models disagree most about Arctic sea ice in winter, the season when ice cover controls how much heat escapes from ocean to atmosphere. The team compiled an Arctic-wide database of sediment biomarkers (molecules left by algae that live in or near sea ice) from the mid-Holocene, 5,000–7,000 years ago, a period warmer than pre-industrial. They used a statistical "proxy system model" to compare these records quantitatively with each climate model's simulation of the same period, then weighted the models by how well they matched. The weighting accounts for models that share code and for the season the biomarkers actually record. It removes the ensemble's bias and cuts its uncertainty by 85%, down to the level of normal year-to-year variability. Applied to the future, the weighted projections put winter sea-ice extent nearly 1 million km² below previous estimates across the coming decades and emissions scenarios.
*Why it matters:* Less winter ice means more ocean heat reaching the Arctic atmosphere, which feeds into Arctic ecosystems and mid-latitude weather, Europe's included. The result depends on how far the mid-Holocene, driven by orbital changes rather than CO₂, is a fair test for the future.

### A water-splitting catalyst lasts longest right at the freezing point {#st-2cc0ed3e9ace}
**[Nature Chemistry](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41557-026-02274-8)** · A. C. Wardhana, H. Ooka et al. (RIKEN; NIMS; USTC) · published Wednesday 21 October 2026
Splitting water into hydrogen and oxygen needs a catalyst for the oxygen side. Cheap manganese dioxide works but corrodes, dissolving as permanganate. Usually lowering the temperature just slows everything down. Here it slowed the corrosion more than the useful reaction, so lifetime improved more than activity fell. Below 0 °C, in supercooled electrolyte, degradation sped up again, the opposite of normal (Arrhenius) temperature behaviour. The best balance sits near 0 °C.
*Why it matters:* Temperature is a cheap control knob that is usually ignored. The authors point to electrolysers in winter or polar conditions. This is a lab result on a single catalyst.

## 🧬 Biology, medicine & neuroscience

### A one-gram wireless microscope records 8,500 neurons in free-roaming mice {#st-d35040462915}
**[Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11293-x)** · Y. Zhang, A. Li et al. (Tsinghua University; Fudan University; Princeton University) · published online Monday 19 October 2026
Imaging large parts of the brain at single-neuron resolution normally means a heavy benchtop microscope and a head-fixed animal. Head-mounted miniscopes free the animal but are tethered by cables and see a small patch. Airscope is a 1.0-gram wireless "mesoscope" (a wide-field microscope) with 4 µm resolution across a 6 mm field of view at 12.5 frames per second. An aspheric lens design makes it 2.84 times shorter than previous miniature microscopes, and a custom wireless link streams calcium imaging, in which neurons light up when active, from more than 8,500 neurons per mouse. The authors say that is a 25-fold increase. They imaged mice in room-sized arenas, while swimming, and several animals at once. Activity across the dorsal cortex predicted which of two competing mice would win, and simultaneous brain and spinal-cord recordings showed the spinal cord influencing the cortex during fear conditioning.
*Why it matters:* Much of what is known about cortical activity comes from restrained animals doing simple tasks. A device light enough for natural social behaviour opens those questions to large-scale recording. The behavioural findings are demonstrations of the tool, not settled neuroscience.

### Psilocybin's synapse remodelling and its "trip" use different neurons {#st-3a55a0fc1b8f}
**[Nature Neuroscience](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-026-02490-y)** · J. J. Baker, E. Kogan et al. (UC Santa Cruz; Lehigh University) · published Thursday 15 October 2026
Psilocybin acts mainly on serotonin 2A receptors (5-HT2A) and is being tested for depression. A central question is whether its lasting benefit needs the hallucinogenic experience, or whether its effect on synapses (neuroplasticity) can be separated out. In mice, the team found that psilocybin both builds and matures new synapses and speeds the removal of existing ones. Switching 5-HT2A on or off only in cortical layer-5 pyramidal neurons showed that signalling in those cells is necessary and sufficient for the rewiring. It was not needed for the head-twitch response, the standard mouse stand-in for a hallucination.
*Why it matters:* This supports the idea that non-hallucinogenic psychedelic-like drugs could keep the plasticity without the trip. The head-twitch response is only a proxy for human hallucinations, and the study measures synapses, not mood.

### An antibody-toxin infused into recurrent brain tumours passes a phase 1 test {#st-f408221c08af}
**[Nature Medicine](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-026-04715-w)** · V. Chandramohan, J. E. Herndon et al. (Duke University) · published online Friday 16 October 2026
Glioblastoma almost always recurs, and few drugs reach the tumour through the blood–brain barrier. D2C7-ATC is an antibody that recognises both normal EGFR and its tumour-specific mutant EGFRvIII, fused to a bacterial toxin. Rather than injecting it into the blood, the Duke team infused it directly into the tumour through catheters, using slow pressure-driven "convection-enhanced delivery". The single-site trial treated 81 patients with recurrent malignant glioma with a single infusion. Most treatment-related side effects were mild to moderate (grades 1–2), and four patients had dose-limiting toxicities at the highest dose. Among 72 patients with IDH-wild-type recurrent glioblastoma, the most common and aggressive form, median overall survival was 7.9 months. In exploratory subgroups (low tumour antibody reactivity, or low IL-8 in blood after treatment), median survival was about 17 months. Biomarkers point to an immune response inside and outside the tumour.
*Why it matters:* The trial sets a recommended dose and shows the delivery method is tolerable. With one arm and no controls, the authors' claim that it prolongs survival in a subset rests on post-hoc biomarker splits that a randomised trial would need to confirm.

### LIGO, Virgo and KAGRA find no sub-solar-mass black holes, tightening dark-matter limits {#st-a8c81d3a297a}
**[arXiv:2610.20276](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20276)** · The LIGO Scientific, Virgo and KAGRA Collaborations (affiliation not listed) · submitted Wednesday 14 October 2026 · `[preprint]`
Stars cannot collapse into black holes lighter than the Sun, so a merger involving one would point to new physics. One candidate is primordial black holes, formed in the early universe and proposed as part of dark matter. Another is "dark black holes" made by dark matter that can cool and collapse. The detectors searched data from April 2024 to January 2025 (O4b, the first stretch of the current run in which Virgo joined both LIGO sites) for binaries with a lighter component of 0.2–1 solar masses. Two of three independent search pipelines flagged outlier candidates, but none was significant enough to count as evidence. The resulting merger-rate limits range from 50 to 5,000 per cubic gigaparsec per year, depending on the masses. For primordial black holes that formed binaries early, at most 1.8–3.2% of dark matter can be in 1-solar-mass objects, and at most 10–18% at 0.35 solar masses. The collaboration's own [science summary](https://www.ligo.org/science-summaries/O4b_SSM/) `[new source]` describes O4b as the most sensitive network it has run so far.
*Why it matters:* The collaboration calls these the most stringent limits to date on sub-solar-mass mergers. Each null result narrows the range of masses where primordial black holes could still make up a large share of dark matter. Virgo is a European detector, run from Italy.

### TESS finds the first hot Jupiter locked in an orbital resonance {#st-b1cabe4ed978}
**[arXiv:2610.28533](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28533)** · S. W. Yee, T. Dziire et al. (UCLA; Harvard–Smithsonian CfA; NYU Shanghai) · submitted Tuesday 20 October 2026 · `[preprint]`
Hot Jupiters, gas giants orbiting their stars in days, usually have no close neighbours. That has been read as a sign that they arrived through violent scattering that cleared out other planets. TOI-3686 c is a hot Jupiter on a 5.08-day orbit whose transits arrive up to 40 minutes early or late, one of the largest such timing swings seen for a hot Jupiter. Those swings led the team to a second planet in the NASA TESS data, a sub-Neptune on a 2.54-day orbit whose transits swing by 10 hours, about 16% of its period. Modelling the transits together with radial-velocity measurements shows the pair sits deep in a 2:1 resonance: the inner planet completes exactly two orbits for each of the outer's. It is the only hot Jupiter currently known in a truly resonant configuration, and the system is younger than about 1 billion years.
*Why it matters:* A resonance this tidy is hard to get from scattering. It points instead to a quiet migration through the gas disk, the same path thought to build compact chains of small planets. At least some hot Jupiters may have formed the gentle way.

### Ionising light escapes young starbursts early, before supernova winds shut the door {#st-01c55895c676}
**[Nature Astronomy](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41550-026-02999-z)** · C. Carr, R. Cen et al. (Institute for Advanced Study; Zhejiang University; University of Michigan) · published online Thursday 15 October 2026
About a billion years after the Big Bang, ultraviolet light ionised the hydrogen between galaxies ("reionisation"). Small, compact star-forming galaxies are the main suspects, but how much of their ionising "Lyman continuum" light gets out, and what controls it, is uncertain. The team took medium-resolution spectra of six similar nearby compact starbursts of different ages and followed both the launch of a galactic wind and the fraction of Lyman continuum that escapes. Escape peaks early, within about 3 million years, while radiation and stellar winds dominate and before a fast galactic wind has developed. In older systems they see faster, dustier winds and less escape. Their reading is that supernova-driven winds loaded with gas and dust suppress escape within the first ~10 million years.
*Why it matters:* JWST is finding many such compact galaxies in the early universe. Knowing that the ionising light escapes in a brief early window changes how their contribution to reionisation should be counted. Six galaxies is a small sample.

### A bulge globular cluster dates to 13.4 billion years {#st-cabfc232c973}
**[arXiv:2610.27969](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27969)** · J. Olivares Carvajal, Á. Valenzuela Navarro et al. (INAF; Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile) · submitted Tuesday 20 October 2026 · `[preprint]` (accepted by A&A)
VVV CL001 is a very metal-poor globular cluster behind heavy dust in the Galactic bulge, so its distance and age have been poorly known. Combining deep ground-based infrared imaging with Hubble photometry, the team derives a distance of 7.34 ± 0.46 kpc and an age of 13.4 ± 0.4 billion years. That makes it a strong candidate for the oldest globular cluster in the Milky Way.
*Why it matters:* The oldest clusters set a lower limit on the age of the Galaxy and the universe (13.8 billion years). The age's uncertainty overlaps with other very old clusters, so "oldest" is a candidacy, not a ranking.

### The Large Magellanic Cloud is still ringing from a collision with its neighbour {#st-98497aec61df}
**[arXiv:2610.20287](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20287)** · S. J. Oden, B. Garver et al. (Montana State University; Lund University) · submitted Wednesday 14 October 2026 · `[preprint]`
Distance maps built from "red clump" stars, a type of star with a known brightness, show that the outer disk of the Large Magellanic Cloud sits about 1.2 kpc out of the plane of its centre and bends into an S-shaped warp reaching 3.6 kpc. A simulation of a direct collision with the Small Magellanic Cloud sets the outer disk oscillating vertically with a ~600-million-year period and matches the observed offset about 260 million years after impact. It underpredicts the warp's size by a factor of 4–5.
*Why it matters:* This adds to evidence that the two Clouds collided recently, and shows that encounters between dwarf galaxies can reshape whole disks. The warp mismatch shows the model is still incomplete.
