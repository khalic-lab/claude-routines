## 📄 ML/AI research (arXiv)

This cycle's papers cluster on two themes: making learning and inference spend compute better, and being more honest about where models are brittle. Two results argue the field simply wastes compute — one shows reinforcement learning for LLMs over-invests in problems the model already solves, the other that a from-scratch 7B model can lean on tools instead of memorization. A skeptical strand probes reliability: reasoning models fail structurally identical twins of tasks they just solved, and two agent papers tackle diagnosing and safely resuming long-horizon failures. Worth reading in full: the "Never Give Up" RL paper, the systematicity study, and the scalable data-attribution work.

- <a id="st-80679e5bf061" class="st-a"></a>**RL for LLMs wastes compute on easy problems — a "never give up" sampler reallocates it** — [arXiv:2609.13443](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13443) · M. Noukhovitch, H. Ivison, N. Lambert, A. Courville (Mila – Université de Montréal; Allen Institute for AI; University of Washington; +1 more) · `[preprint]`

  Reinforcement learning (RL — training a model by rewarding correct answers) improves an LLM far more on problems it can already usually solve than on genuinely hard ones — a pattern the authors name the "Matthew effect" (the rich get richer). They argue standard RL squanders compute re-solving easy problems, and introduce Never Give Up (NGU): keep sampling a problem until a correct answer appears, then, using asynchronous RL, spend fewer samples on the easy problems and pour the freed budget into the hard ones.

  **Why it matters:** Frontier RL runs are enormously expensive, and this reframes part of that spend as misallocated rather than merely insufficient — a scheduling change, not more GPUs, closes some of the hard-problem gap. _Submitted 11 September 2026._

- <a id="st-65c76df0baff" class="st-a"></a>**A fully open 7B model built to lean on tools instead of memorizing the web** — [arXiv:2609.13356](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13356) · ZGCM Team (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]`

  ZGCM-1 is a 7-billion-parameter dense model trained from scratch with a fully open recipe, on the premise that a small model cannot memorize the open web but can compensate by pairing internal reasoning with external tool use (search, code) over a 256K-token context. The recipe combines an interleaved sliding-window/full-attention design, an FP8 Muon optimizer, and a staged curriculum that scales context from 16K to 256K and recasts tool-interaction traces as Markov decision processes (a formalism for sequential decisions) during mid-training.

  **Why it matters:** Open, reproducible recipes for capable small models are scarce — most frontier work is closed — so a from-scratch, documented 7B aimed at math and agentic search lowers the barrier to study and build on. The paper is bylined to an unnamed "ZGCM Team" and the efficiency claims are the authors' own; treat them as unverified until independently reproduced. _Submitted 11 September 2026._

- <a id="st-b0b4e37f2999" class="st-a"></a>**Reasoning models solve a task, then fail its logical twin** — [arXiv:2609.13948](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13948) · S. Schug, B. M. Lake (Princeton University) · `[preprint]`

  "Systematicity" is the principle that understanding a concept implies understanding close variations of it. The authors take rule-induction tasks from cognitive science and build structurally equivalent variants — the same underlying rule, recombined or with symbols substituted — then check whether a model that solves one variant also solves its isomorphs. Often it does not: models fail variants that are logically identical to problems they just got right.

  **Why it matters:** It is direct evidence that apparent reasoning can be brittle and non-compositional — getting a benchmark item right does not mean the model has grasped the rule behind it, which bears on any use that assumes it will generalize. _Submitted 12 September 2026._

- <a id="st-e25485f75f46" class="st-a"></a>**Cheaper answers to "which training example caused this prediction?"** — [arXiv:2609.15044](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.15044) · Y. Chen, A. Maleki, A. Ilyas et al. (Columbia University; Carnegie Mellon University) · `[preprint]`

  Data attribution measures how much each training example shaped a given prediction — the basis for data valuation, machine unlearning (removing a datapoint's influence after the fact), and interpretability. Exact "metagradient" methods such as MAGIC differentiate through the whole training run but need a separate run per prediction, which is prohibitive. This work recasts budgeted attribution as estimating a large "influence matrix" from a small number of measurements, and shows which measurements best recover it.

  **Why it matters:** If influence can be approximated from a few measurements instead of one run per query, attribution becomes tractable at model scale — practical for unlearning requests, copyright and data-valuation disputes, and debugging which data drove a behavior. (MAGIC co-author Andrew Ilyas is on the paper.) _Submitted 14 September 2026._

- <a id="st-2d13da620105" class="st-a"></a>**Diagnosing agent failures by searching the trace, not judging it once** — [arXiv:2609.13463](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13463) · H. Raj, D. Lee, V. Gupta et al. (Scale AI) · `[preprint]`

  Long-horizon AI agents produce huge execution logs, and the true cause of a failure is often a single early action far from where things visibly broke. Existing root-cause methods hand an LLM the whole trace and ask for a one-shot verdict, which degrades as traces grow and relevant clues become sparse and scattered. The authors reframe diagnosis as a search problem — continually searching the trace for the responsible step rather than committing to an early guess.

  **Why it matters:** Reliable agents need to turn "it failed" into "it failed here, because of this", and one-shot judging does not scale to long traces; treating attribution as search is a route to debugging that holds up as agents run longer. _Submitted 11 September 2026._

- <a id="st-8047832e989b" class="st-a"></a>**When an interrupted agent can safely resume — and when it shouldn't** — [arXiv:2609.13672](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13672) · Z. Zhang, W. Liu (independent; Beijing Information Science and Technology University) · `[preprint]`

  Agents get interrupted mid-task; restarting repeats completed work, but blindly continuing from a saved state can carry forward unverified or stale progress. The paper introduces "recoverability" as an explicit system primitive: a saved state is paired with a permitted recovery action and a contract binding that choice to supporting evidence and independent checks, so resuming is a deliberate decision rather than an automatic one. Their challenge set shows accurate-looking restoration can quietly hide disallowed reuse.

  **Why it matters:** As agents run longer and touch real files and tools, "resume from checkpoint" becomes a correctness question, not just a convenience — this frames the safeguards a runtime needs before it trusts its own saved state. _Submitted 12 September 2026._

- <a id="st-0b3d2e48987d" class="st-a"></a>**A small language for compiling new attention variants into fast GPU kernels** — [arXiv:2609.13612](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13612) · V. K. Dasoju, T. Zhao (University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee) · `[preprint]`

  Attention dominates transformer compute, and every new attention variant normally needs hand-written GPU code to run at usable speed. PyTorch's flex_attention lets researchers describe custom attention in Python but only handles changes applied after the core matrix multiply — which excludes RoPE (rotary position embedding, the positional scheme used by every major LLM), because RoPE acts before it. AttnFuse is a domain-specific language that makes such pre-multiplication transforms first-class, composing ten high-level building blocks into fused kernels.

  **Why it matters:** Lowering the cost of turning a new attention idea into production-speed code shortens the loop between architecture research and usable models — and it targets exactly the gap (RoPE) that existing tooling left open. _Submitted 11 September 2026._

- <a id="st-7387bb06b030" class="st-a"></a>**Scoring attention edits exactly from a single backward pass** — [arXiv:2609.14127](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.14127) · J. Huang, M. Chlon, G. Gutin, L. Chlon (Hassana Labs; Royal Holloway, University of London; University of Oxford) · `[preprint]`

  This is an interpretability-and-editing result: it derives the exact local response of attention to a positional intervention, so a candidate edit can be scored from a cached baseline plus one backward pass instead of re-running the model. Starting from how attention output changes with position (the RoPE derivative), it carries the displacement through the softmax without linearizing, and it keeps the interaction term when keys and values are edited together — a cross effect naive attributions drop. It reports ~95–96% sign accuracy across 92,160 positional edits.

  **Why it matters:** Cheap, exact scoring of "what if I nudged this position or token?" makes attention-level interpretability and targeted editing far more practical, and it shows the cross term that separate attributions ignore is not negligible. _Submitted 12 September 2026._

- <a id="st-b252fbc44f1a" class="st-a"></a>**One formal framework for both policy iteration and recursive self-improvement** — [arXiv:2609.13406](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13406) · H. Tang, Y. Ma, P. Li, Y. Yuan (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]`

  Recursive self-improvement (RSI — a system getting better at making itself better) is invoked loosely and has no agreed formal description, unlike its classical cousin, generalized policy iteration, where the improvement rule sits outside the agent. Generalized Agent Iteration (GAI) treats the agent as a configuration of modifiable components and models learning as updates to those components, casting ordinary iterative policy improvement and RSI as two cases of a single paradigm.

  **Why it matters:** A precise vocabulary for what "self-improving AI" actually means helps separate genuine mechanisms from hype — useful for judging which RSI claims are substantive. _Submitted 11 September 2026._
