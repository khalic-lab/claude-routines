### Two labs push self-correcting qubits past break-even {#st-14aecd6dafea}
**[Nature Physics](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41567-026-03493-z)** · Z. Ni, L. Hu, Y. Cai et al. (International Quantum Academy; SUSTech; Hefei National Laboratory) · published Monday 16 November 2026; companion paper **[Nature Physics](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41567-026-03528-5)** · L. Sun, Y. Xu, Y. Zhou et al. (Tsinghua University; USTC; Beijing Academy of Quantum Information Sciences) · published Monday 16 November 2026
Quantum error correction (QEC) spreads one "logical" qubit across many physical degrees of freedom so that errors can be spotted and undone. The usual way is active: measure an error signature, then have classical electronics apply a fix, which is slow and needs high-fidelity readout. Autonomous QEC instead builds the fix into the hardware, using engineered drives and dissipation (deliberately leaking the error's entropy into a helper qubit that is then reset), with no measurement or feedback loop at all. The benchmark is "break-even": a logical qubit that lives longer than the best physical qubit in the same device. Both groups store the qubit as photons in a superconducting microwave cavity and protect it against photon loss, the dominant error. The Shenzhen team's protected qubit lives about 2.0 ms, 18% longer than its best physical qubit, and they use it as a sensor to gain 6.3 dB in precision on a small frequency shift. The Tsinghua team uses a general recipe derived from quantum channel simulation that can in principle implement any recovery operation, and reaches 1.04 times break-even. Both margins are small, and active QEC on bosonic codes has already gone further beyond break-even.
*Why it matters:* crossing break-even without measurement shows that error correction can, in part, be wired into the physics of the device rather than run by fast classical control, which could ease one of the hardest scaling problems in quantum computers.

### Super-Kamiokande takes the temperature of the Sun's core with neutrinos {#st-df9dadb74bab}
**[arXiv:2611.17651](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17651)** · Super-Kamiokande Collaboration, Y. Jiang, K. Abe et al. (UC Irvine; University of Tokyo; Kavli IPMU) · posted Friday 13 November 2026 · `[preprint]`
Neutrinos from the Sun's core escape straight through it, so they are the only direct messenger from where fusion happens. Boron-8 neutrinos are a useful probe because the rate at which they are made is very sensitive to core temperature, and the way they change "flavour" on the way out depends on the electron density they cross. Using 5,805 days of data from the Super-Kamiokande water detector in Japan (phases I to IV), the collaboration works backwards to an outer bound on the size of the fusion core and to its density and temperature. For the standard high-metallicity solar model, the core is bounded at 0.499 solar radii at 90% confidence, tightening to 0.268 solar radii when combined with Canada's SNO experiment. The inferred central temperature is 15.599 million K, with a quoted uncertainty of about 1,600 K. That precision holds only within the assumed solar model; with other models of the Sun's composition, the bounds shift.
*Why it matters:* it turns a solar-neutrino detector into a thermometer for the Sun's interior. That is a direct check on the solar models used across astrophysics, including the long-running "solar abundance problem" about how much heavy elements the Sun contains.

### Zurich chemists arylate bare C–H bonds with a tuned palladium catalyst {#st-4e055bd119a4}
**[Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11391-w)** · M. Zhang, E. González-Fernández, J. Dhankhar et al. (University of Zurich) · published Wednesday 11 November 2026
Joining two aromatic rings is one of the most common steps in making drugs and materials, and the standard method, cross-coupling, needs both partners to carry pre-installed reactive "handles" such as halogens or boron groups. Direct C–H arylation would skip that step by attaching an aryl group straight onto an ordinary carbon–hydrogen bond, but native molecules offer many similar C–H bonds and little reactivity, so yields and site control have been poor. The University of Zurich team builds a tailored chemical microenvironment around a palladium dicarboxylate catalyst so it picks the least crowded C–H site, and pairs an advanced palladium(II) precatalyst with designed diaryliodonium reagents to deliver the aryl group efficiently. The method works on electron-rich and electron-poor arenes, and even plain benzene, normally a hard case, takes successive aryl groups in a controlled way. Because conditions are mild, it tolerates groups that would react in ordinary cross-coupling, such as iodides, triflates and boronate esters, which makes "late-stage" modification of complex molecules possible.
*Why it matters:* removing the pre-functionalisation step can shorten synthetic routes in pharmaceutical chemistry. The work comes from a University of Zurich group.

### A curved nanocarbon gets an aromatic five-membered ring {#st-f93eb1ff74af}
**[Nature Chemistry](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41557-026-02293-5)** · N. Luo, C. Ye, Z. Zhou et al. (Sun Yat-sen University; Kyoto University) · published Monday 16 November 2026
Five-membered carbon rings are what make sheets of graphene-like carbon curve into bowls and balls such as fullerenes, but in those structures the five-membered rings are usually not aromatic (they lack the extra stability of benzene-like electron sharing). The team made pentabenzocorannulenide, a bowl-shaped carbon anion whose central ring is a cyclopentadienide, the negatively charged, aromatic five-membered ring familiar from organometallic chemistry. They produce it by cleaving a carbon–carbon bond in a precursor, a step driven by the stability gained on becoming aromatic. X-ray crystallography and quantum chemical calculations show bonding and electronic properties unlike corannulene, fullerenes or nanotube end caps. The molecule's conjugate acid is exceptionally strong, and the bowl can bind a metal atom across its central ring.
*Why it matters:* it adds a new building block to curved carbon chemistry, a field that feeds into organic electronics and molecular materials.

### Later-born generations reach old age with better memory and decline more slowly {#st-b9c000d89ccf}
**[Nature Medicine](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-026-04759-y)** · A. M. Fjell, E. O. S. Grødem, G. Lunansky et al. (University of Oslo; Oslo University Hospital; Amsterdam UMC) · published Tuesday 17 November 2026
The chance of developing dementia at any given age has been falling in Europe and the US, by about 13% per decade in pooled estimates, but it has been unclear why. One possibility is that later generations start old age with more cognitive reserve; another is that they decline more slowly. The Oslo-led team analysed about 783,000 episodic-memory tests from about 219,000 people in five long-running studies, some followed for 30 years. They find both: later-born generations enter old age with higher memory scores and lose memory more slowly afterwards, a "double dividend" large enough, they argue, to account for the fall in dementia incidence. The gains are concentrated among people who would otherwise decline fastest, and slower memory decline goes with lower rates of clinical impairment and slower shrinkage of the hippocampus, a memory centre. The authors argue that trends affecting everyone at the same calendar time do not explain the pattern. This is an observational analysis, so it shows associations across birth cohorts rather than which specific changes caused them.
*Why it matters:* memory level is built mostly in childhood and early life, while the rate of decline is shaped by adult conditions, so the authors conclude that keeping dementia rates falling needs brain-health policy across the whole lifespan, not only interventions in old age.

### A Neanderthal gene variant shrinks the brain's visual lobe and lowers schizophrenia risk {#st-e112b1f30b71}
**[PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2624837123)** · H. Zeberg, S. Pääbo (Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology; Karolinska Institutet; Okinawa Institute of Science and Technology) · published Monday 16 November 2026
Neanderthal skulls were longer and less rounded than ours, hinting at different proportions of brain regions, and most people outside Africa carry a few percent of Neanderthal DNA from interbreeding. Hugo Zeberg and Nobel laureate Svante Pääbo report that a common genetic variant affecting the size of the occipital lobe, the brain region at the back that processes vision, was inherited from Neanderthals. It sits in predicted transcription-factor binding sites near DAAM1, a gene involved in brain development, and carriers show altered DAAM1 activity. Each copy of the variant goes with an occipital lobe about 0.85 cm³ smaller. People with two copies have almost 10% lower schizophrenia risk, an association not seen for Neanderthal ancestry as a whole. Any link to cognition or schooling is small or absent, at most about two weeks of education per copy against an average of about 15 years. These are genetic associations in present-day people; the paper does not show the mechanism linking lobe size and schizophrenia risk.
*Why it matters:* it is one of the clearest cases of a single archaic variant visibly changing human brain anatomy today, and it cuts against the idea that Neanderthal-derived brain variants must have cognitive costs.

### The blood–brain barrier imports protective proteins, and that import fails early in Alzheimer's models {#st-e9244974b175}
**[Cell](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2026.10.039)** · H. Zhou, J. C. Maynard, A. J. Pennacchio et al. (Gladstone Institutes) · published Wednesday 11 November 2026; details via **[Gladstone Institutes release](https://gladstone.org/news/study-rewrites-rules-blood-brain-barrier/)** `[new source]`
The blood–brain barrier, the lining of the brain's blood vessels, is usually described by what it keeps out, and Alzheimer's research has focused on how it becomes leaky late in disease. Andrew Yang's group at the Gladstone Institutes labelled the full set of blood proteins in mice, put them back into the bloodstream, and tracked which ones ended up in brain tissue. Of 861 proteins tracked, 92 crossed, most of them made in distant organs, especially the liver. One, apolipoprotein A1 (APOA1), is carried in through receptors on the barrier and on astrocytes (support cells), where it triggers cholesterol release that helps maintain synapses, the connections between neurons. In a mouse model of Alzheimer's disease, the barrier stopped importing APOA1 and similar proteins before it started to leak, alongside loss of synapses and behavioural changes. Mice engineered to make extra APOA1 in the liver showed fewer disease markers. All of this is in mice. The Cell full text was not reachable here, so details come from the institute's release and Nature's news report.
*Why it matters:* if Alzheimer's begins partly as a supply failure from the body to the brain, therapies could aim to restore that import rather than only clear amyloid. That idea now needs testing in humans.

### Flashing-light-and-sound headset fails its pivotal Alzheimer's trial {#st-47c23a0f8e93}
**[Nature news](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03770-0)** · reporting on the HOPE trial results presented by Cognito Therapeutics at the Clinical Trials on Alzheimer's Disease meeting, Boston, Monday 16 November 2026; company release via **[Cognito Therapeutics](https://www.cognitotx.com/news)** `[new source]` · `[single-source]`
Spectris AD is a headset of goggles and headphones that flickers light and clicks at 40 Hz for an hour a day, meant to induce "gamma" brain rhythms. Mouse studies suggested this could reduce Alzheimer's pathology. In the HOPE trial, 673 people with Alzheimer's disease used an active or a sham device for 12 months. On the 156-point combined scale of cognition and daily function, the active group declined by 8.97 points and the sham group by 8.82, so there was no benefit on the primary measure. Cognito's release is headlined as showing "consistent slowing" of decline in early symptomatic disease, and its chief executive points to a mild-impairment subgroup and says the company will seek US approval next year. An independent neurologist quoted by Nature calls the trial negative, and post-hoc subgroup findings are hypotheses, not evidence. The full data have not been published in a journal.
*Why it matters:* it is the largest test yet of gamma stimulation, and on its primary endpoint the result was null. Any regulatory filing would rest on a subgroup analysis, and readers should weigh the company's framing against that.

### Phone data show how human movement spread the Bundibugyo Ebola outbreak early on {#st-49b22ce7fb41}
**[Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aej4586)** · D. M. Ebengo, P. Akilimali, T. Bishola Tshitenge et al. (INRB Kinshasa; University of Kinshasa; INSP Kinshasa) · published Thursday 12 November 2026
The Democratic Republic of the Congo declared an outbreak of Bundibugyo virus, one of the Ebola-causing viruses, on 15 May 2026 in Ituri province. Nature reports it has since passed 10,000 confirmed infections and 5,198 deaths, making it the largest Bundibugyo outbreak recorded, with no licensed vaccine or treatment for this species. This Congolese-led team paired detailed case and testing records with anonymised mobile-phone mobility data. Between 10 May and 1 June, the number of health zones reporting cases rose by 12 and of smaller health areas by 49, and human travel out of the first hotspots predicted where infections appeared. Early case numbers doubled roughly every 10 days. The index case had symptoms by 25 April, weeks before detection, and risk rose again 150–200 km away as people travelled along established routes to larger towns.
*Why it matters:* it quantifies how weeks of missed detection let the virus travel, and it shows mobility data could guide where to put testing and vaccination when the next outbreak starts.

### A 25-million-cell atlas maps the primate basal ganglia {#st-ee32ed8c7b7c}
**[Cell](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2026.10.012)** · N. J. Johansen, Y. Fu, M. Schmitz et al. (affiliation not listed) · published Thursday 12 November 2026; overview via **[Nature news](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03565-3)** · `[single-source]`
The basal ganglia are deep-brain structures involved in movement, mood and decision-making, and they are implicated in Parkinson's and Huntington's disease, addiction and schizophrenia. The NIH-funded BRAIN Initiative Cell Atlas Network released a package of 26 papers across Cell Press journals mapping 25.5 million cells: 21 million from six human brains, about 3 million from macaques and 1.5 million from marmosets. The data combine gene expression, chemical DNA marks, electrical recordings and DNA folding with each cell's location. Across primates the teams identify 61 cell types conserved over about 76 million years. One study of 1.1 million cells from the striatum, which has no clear anatomical borders, finds six molecularly distinct zones, whose differences blur with age, especially in regions affected by Huntington's disease. The paper texts were not reachable here, so details rest on Nature's report.
*Why it matters:* it is a reference map for locating which cell types go wrong in these brain diseases, and a scaffold the project intends to extend to the rest of the brain.

### The poxvirus core has a gated pore {#st-4982b5b47092}
**[Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11399-2)** · Y. Hong, S. Liu, K. Wang et al. (Tsinghua University; Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention; Peking University) · published Monday 16 November 2026
Poxviruses, the family that includes smallpox and mpox, are unusual in copying their genes inside a protein shell, the viral core, after entering a cell. That means molecules must pass in and out of the core, but how was unclear. Using cryo-electron tomography (3D imaging of frozen samples) on vaccinia virus, the team resolved a portal complex in the core wall at 6.7 Å and its central pore at 4.4 Å. Three viral proteins, E8, E6 and L3, form the pore, which narrows to a positively charged constriction lined by conserved lysines from L3. Mutating those residues impaired viral gene expression and growth.
*Why it matters:* a conserved, essential channel in the poxvirus core is a possible drug target against a virus family that includes mpox.

### An independent method confirms the ring around M87's black hole {#st-4040c0f9a7d7}
**[Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11241-9)** · S. Lai, N. Thyagarajan, O. I. Wong, F. Diakogiannis (CSIRO; ICRAR) · published Wednesday 11 November 2026
The Event Horizon Telescope's 2019 image of the black hole M87*, a bright ring around a dark "shadow", was built from very sparse data from a planet-wide array of radio dishes, and turning such data into a picture involves choices. Different analyses produced different fine detail, and some outside groups argued the ring itself might be an artefact of the imaging method. This Australian team developed an independent algorithm that uses no assumptions about the source's shape and relies on "closure" quantities, combinations of measurements from three or more telescopes in which each dish's calibration errors cancel. Applied to the original M87* data, it recovers a ring 42.2 ± 1.5 microarcseconds across, which implies a black hole of about 6.5 ± 0.5 billion solar masses viewed about 20° from its jet. This settles the shape question, not the finer structure within the ring.
*Why it matters:* it removes the main methodological doubt about one of astronomy's best-known images, using a technique free of the calibration choices the critics challenged.

### A nearby Type Ia supernova is reaching peak brightness this week {#st-38c6b739e92e}
**[NSF NOIRLab release](https://noirlab.edu/public/news/noirlab2628/)** · NSF NOIRLab (Blanco/DECam and Gemini South observations) · released Thursday 12 November 2026
The ATLAS survey caught supernova SN 2026ahvu on Monday 2 November 2026 in NGC 1433, a barred spiral galaxy about 50 million light-years away. Keck spectroscopy a day later classified it as a young Type Ia, the thermonuclear explosion of a white dwarf, and it brightened from magnitude 17 to about 13.5 within days. NOIRLab predicts a peak of magnitude 10.5–12.1 between 18 and 23 November 2026 and is coordinating follow-up with DECam, Gemini and SOAR, inviting other observatories to join. It is one of the nearest Type Ia supernovae since SN 2011fe in 2011, and archival Dark Energy Survey images show the site before the explosion. Because Type Ia supernovae are standard candles for measuring cosmic distances, catching one this early helps test how they ignite. At declination −47°, it never rises above the horizon from Switzerland; observers need to be in the Southern Hemisphere.
*Why it matters:* a well-observed nearby Type Ia, followed from before peak, helps calibrate the distance ladder behind measurements of the Universe's expansion rate.

### Dual quasar sits inside a bubble of overheated gas {#st-c1aa2d15de30}
**[Nature Astronomy](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41550-026-03025-y)** · K. R. Hall, N. L. Zakamska, J. S. Bennett et al. (Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian; Johns Hopkins University; University of Arizona) · published Wednesday 18 November 2026
Galaxy-formation models need quasars, feeding supermassive black holes, to blow hot winds that stop their galaxies forming stars, but that hot, diffuse gas is very hard to see. One way is the thermal Sunyaev–Zel'dovich effect: hot electrons slightly change cosmic-microwave-background light passing through them, leaving a measurable dip at millimetre wavelengths. The team found a pair of quasars at redshift 2.37, seen 2.8 billion years after the Big Bang, inside a hot-gas bubble detected at 6σ. Its thermal energy, 9.7 × 10⁶⁰ erg, is 3.6 times what all the hot gas in the host's dark-matter halo should hold, and at least 0.6% of the quasars' lifetime energy output. There are also hints of hot gas 100–600 kiloparsecs out. It is a single system.
*Why it matters:* it is direct calorimetry of quasar feedback, the process models invoke to explain why the most massive galaxies stopped forming stars.
