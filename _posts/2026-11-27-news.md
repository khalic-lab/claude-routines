- <a id="st-b93516d76bfe" class="st-a"></a>**EU leaders broke off their special budget summit in Brussels late on Thursday 26 November, a day early and without agreement on the 2028–2034 budget (the multiannual financial framework, or MFF). The latest proposal is about €1.7 trillion over seven years, down from roughly €2 trillion at the start and more than €200 billion below that. Merz wants a few hundred billion more in cuts, rules out new joint EU borrowing and still ties his consent to Ukraine aid. He called the €200 billion cut a "lower limit". Germany now negotiates alongside the Netherlands, Austria, Sweden, Finland, Denmark and Belgium, a group that calls itself the "reasonable seven". On the other side, net recipients in the south and east, and net payers France and Italy, want to protect farm and regional spending. Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni said the sides were "light years" apart. French President Emmanuel Macron said further cuts would be a "strategic mistake" that leaves EU investment falling behind China and the US, and promised to defend the full farm-policy budget. - <a id="st-c6eef4bacf55" class="st-a"></a>**The Federal Council admitted on Thursday 26 November that Swiss authorities did not act properly in 1994 when Félicien Kabuga, an alleged financier of the Rwandan genocide, entered Switzerland. It found the then head of the Federal Aliens Office used his discretion "arbitrarily" in granting the visa.** The visa for Kabuga and eight relatives was issued on 9 June 1994, while the genocide was under way, and the family entered legally on 22 July, despite repeated attempts by the foreign ministry to have the visa cancelled. The report answers National Council postulate 23.4287 and draws on a University of Basel historical study. It finds that expelling Kabuga in August 1994 was lawful, because officials judged the evidence too thin for a prosecution in Switzerland to succeed. It also says the decision weighed domestic fears of a suspected war criminal living freely in the country more heavily than Switzerland's international responsibility. The government says another decision "would have been possible" and that foreign-policy considerations must carry more weight in future cases with international-law implications. Kabuga evaded arrest until 2020 in France. ([Federal Council via admin.ch, 26 Nov 2026](https://www.admin.ch/fr/newnsb/vnJhm6kXC02Q) [official PR])
  - Why it matters: it is a rare formal government admission that Swiss migration authorities failed in a genocide case, and it sets a stated rule for weighing foreign-policy duties in future visa and expulsion decisions.
