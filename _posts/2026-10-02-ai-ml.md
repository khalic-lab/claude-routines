## 📄 ML/AI research (arXiv)

This cycle's batch is unusually self-critical about reasoning. Four papers interrogate whether a model's visible "thinking" is what it appears — its chain-of-thought, its recursive refinement, its best-of-k scaling curve, its looped computation — and each lands on "less than you'd think." Running alongside is a surprising-transfer cluster: train agents in invented worlds, or models on protein folding, or on a reasoning *method* rather than a topic, and the skill shows up where you didn't train it. If you read three: **PhantomEnvironments** (cheapest useful idea), **Fold2Reason** (most surprising), and the **recursive-reasoning** study (most immediately useful if you build small reasoning modules). Nine papers, ranked by likely interest to this reader.

- <a id="st-p1" class="st-a"></a>**Agents trained in rule-built fictional worlds transfer to real-world search** — [arXiv:2609.40221](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.40221) · A. Kabra, K. Q. Weinberger et al. (Cornell) · `[preprint]`

  Training LLM agents with reinforcement learning (RL — learning by trial-and-error against a reward) is bottlenecked by environments: you need tasks with checkable answers, long enough to be interesting, and cheap to generate at scale. The authors build PhantomEnvironments — multi-turn search worlds whose "articles" are generated entirely by templated rules, with no LLM in the loop and zero marginal cost per world — and train agents to answer multi-hop questions by searching that invented corpus.

  **Why it matters:** the invented worlds share no facts with reality, yet agents trained on them transfer to real-world multi-hop search benchmarks, sometimes beating agents trained on real data — and they learn to scale their search effort with question difficulty. It is a cheap, contamination-free way to make search agents, sidestepping the expensive human-curated or LLM-generated environments the field has leaned on. _Submitted 2026-09-30._

- <a id="st-p2" class="st-a"></a>**Teaching a model to fold proteins makes it better at general reasoning** — [arXiv:2609.38879](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38879) · Y. Liu, S. Zheng et al. (Shanghai Jiao Tong University; Fudan University; Shanghai Innovation Institute +1 more) · `[preprint]`

  Human text usually states answers without the spatial logic behind them, so the authors use protein folding as a teacher: one solved 3D structure yields thousands of exactly-checkable spatial and topological facts. Their recipe (Fold2Reason) post-trains a model on a protein-folding question-answer set using two signals at once — discrete structural answers through the normal text head, and continuous 3D geometry decoded from the same internal representation.

  **Why it matters:** besides tripling structure-prediction scores, folding training lifted accuracy on all ten tasks of a broad reasoning suite (spatial, graph, scientific, general), raising the macro-average from 45.1% to 48.3% — concrete evidence that a model can learn reusable *reasoning* from a hard, checkable physical domain rather than from more text. _Submitted 2026-09-30._

- <a id="st-p3" class="st-a"></a>**A linear-attention layer that switches between experts closes the gap with softmax** — [arXiv:2609.39034](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.39034) · H. D. Lee, S. W. Linderman et al. (Stanford) · `[preprint]`

  Standard softmax attention is expressive but needs a key-value cache (KV-cache — stored context that grows with sequence length), which caps how long a context you can serve. Linear attention keeps a fixed-size recurrent state instead (constant memory) but is usually weaker. Switching Linear Attention (SwiLA) lets each output dimension pick among several linear-attention components per token, derived as online expectation-maximization over a mixture of linear regressions — adding expressive power while keeping the constant-memory state.

  **Why it matters:** the KV-cache is the main reason long-context inference gets expensive, so a linear-attention variant that actually competes on recall and language modeling is directly relevant to anyone running long contexts on constrained hardware — an architecture lever, not a wrapper. _Submitted 2026-09-30._

- <a id="st-p4" class="st-a"></a>**What actually makes recursive reasoning models work** — [arXiv:2609.39967](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.39967) · Y. Shakhvalieva, I. Oseledets et al. (DAIMLD) · `[preprint]`

  Recursive reasoning models apply one small shared transformer block many times to refine a latent state, buying large effective depth with few parameters — strong on algorithmic puzzles and natural as tools an LLM could call. But existing models (HRM, TRM, URM) differ in architecture, gradient handling and training all at once, so nobody knew what mattered. This paper runs them through one controlled pipeline across six algorithmic domains and isolates the variables.

  **Why it matters:** the recipe for stable, generalizable recursive reasoning turns out to be simple — an intermediate gradient horizon, large physical batches, and controlled updates of the recurrent state — which demystifies a line of work that had a reputation for unstable training. If small callable reasoning modules become part of the agent stack, this is the practical how-to. _Submitted 2026-09-30._

- <a id="st-p5" class="st-a"></a>**A way to measure whether a model's chain-of-thought matches what it actually computes** — [arXiv:2609.38972](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38972) · Y. Hong, E. Choi et al. (NYU; NYU Shanghai) · `[preprint]`

  A chain-of-thought (CoT — the step-by-step text a model emits before answering) is often read as a window into its reasoning, but it can be edited without changing the answer, so it is not always faithful. The authors propose CoT-Interpretability Alignment (CIA), a metric comparing the strategy the CoT describes against the model's internal reasoning as read out by interpretability tools, then try to improve it with post-training rewards.

  **Why it matters:** across three tasks and three models, agreement was only 44.8–75.9% — a blunt reminder that a plausible CoT is not proof a model reasoned that way, which matters wherever CoT is used for oversight. Post-training on a faithfulness reward raised alignment without hurting accuracy. It extends last edition's CoT-monitor work from a *distinct* angle — measuring faithfulness rather than gaming a monitor. _Submitted 2026-09-30._

- <a id="st-p6" class="st-a"></a>**Faster agentic RL by warming up on the student's own mistakes first** — [arXiv:2609.39436](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.39436) · Y. Qiao, Z. Chu et al. (Zhejiang University; Ant Group) · `[preprint]`

  Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR — training on tasks whose answers can be auto-checked) gives agents little signal early, when sparse rewards leave the policy adrift. On-Policy Warmup (OPW) first trains the student with teacher supervision on the student's *own* interaction trajectories — including its imperfect actions and recoveries — before switching to RLVR. This was one of several on-policy-distillation papers in the batch, a theme clearly in the water this cycle.

  **Why it matters:** warming up on states the student actually visits (rather than clean teacher demonstrations) reaches high performance earlier and ends higher than the baselines, and the authors connect it theoretically to trajectory-level distribution matching. It is a practical knob for the expensive, slow early phase of agent training. _Submitted 2026-09-30._

- <a id="st-p7" class="st-a"></a>**Looped transformers don't repeat the same step each loop** — [arXiv:2609.39892](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.39892) · J. Wu, Y. Hu, M. Zhang (Peking University) · `[preprint]`

  A looped transformer reuses the same layers over and over, and the appealing story is that it learns one algorithm and reruns it each loop. Using graph-walk tasks, the authors show that is not what happens: a single loop can advance by different numbers of steps or stall, so the shared weights are being *routed* to different operations. They find a learned linear map on the entering hidden state that selects which transition a frozen loop performs.

  **Why it matters:** it is a clean interpretability result on a trendy architecture — weight-sharing does not mean computation-sharing — and the steering handle (change the hidden state, change the operation) is a concrete lever for controlling latent computation. A caution against reading too much into "the model just repeats its reasoning." _Submitted 2026-09-30._

- <a id="st-p8" class="st-a"></a>**Trusting a test-time-scaling curve is far more expensive than drawing one** — [arXiv:2609.40190](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.40190) · S. Sarkar, S. Baichoo (Peter Munk Cardiac Centre; University Health Network) · `[preprint]`

  Best-of-k sampling — generate k answers, keep the one a verifier likes most — is reported as a scaling curve of accuracy against k. The paper's point is statistical: because you pick a compute budget after seeing every point on the curve, you need a confidence band covering all budgets at once, and a naive design to certify 64 budgets to ±1/32 on a 100-question benchmark would need ~192,000 generated answers.

  **Why it matters:** most of that cost pays for the wrong uncertainty — on a fixed benchmark, about three-quarters of the variance is *between* questions, which a smarter audit need not re-pay. The authors derive the minimum cost of certifying the whole curve, which is exactly the kind of rigor missing when labs wave test-time-scaling plots around as evidence. _Submitted 2026-09-30._

- <a id="st-p9" class="st-a"></a>**Math skill transfers by reasoning method, not by topic** — [arXiv:2610.00331](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00331) · S. Goudarzi, H. Rahimian et al. (Clemson University; Coastal Carolina University) · `[preprint]`

  When choosing math training data, the obvious principle is topic (use probability problems to improve probability). The alternative is reasoning approach (use worked solutions that share a *method* — say, pigeonhole or double-counting — even across topics). Using two carefully counterbalanced designs where each cell takes a turn as the held-out target, the authors measure which relation produces more transfer after fine-tuning.

  **Why it matters:** same-approach sources beat same-topic ones in all 40 model–target comparisons across five base models — a clean, actionable data-selection finding (curate by how a problem is solved, not what it's about) with unusually tight experimental controls. _Submitted 2026-10-01._

## 📊 Benchmarks & evaluations

- <a id="st-b1" class="st-a"></a>**AI cracks Stratego, one of the last board games where humans still held out** — An academic team's system, Ataraxos, beat Pim Niemeijer — four-time world champion and 600-plus weeks the top-ranked player — 15 games to 1 with four draws, and trained on just 16 GPUs for a few thousand dollars. [(Nature, 2026-10-01)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11036-y)

  Stratego is an imperfect-information game (you see where opponents' pieces are but not what they are), with more than a decillion possible setups, games running up to ~2,000 moves, and bluffing central to play — a combination that defeated even DeepMind's 2022 DeepNash. Researchers from Carnegie Mellon, MIT, NYU and Stanford cracked it with two ideas: a self-play schedule that makes big strategy changes early and small ones late (to stop hidden-information learning from going in circles), and a separate "belief model" that guesses the opponent's hidden pieces, letting the system search ahead by sampling plausible boards instead of enumerating the astronomically many.

  **Why it matters:** this is the kind of milestone the field marks by decades — Deep Blue (1997), AlphaGo (2016) — and it fell not to a hyperscaler's budget but to an academic group for a few thousand dollars, the more striking part. Imperfect-information decision-making under bluffing is the shape of many real strategic problems, from negotiation to security.

## 💼 Industry, funding, regulation

- <a id="st-i1" class="st-a"></a>**AMD buys Fei-Fei Li's World Labs for $8.2 billion** — AMD announced it will acquire the world-models startup by year-end, pending regulatory approval, with founder Fei-Fei Li becoming AMD's executive vice-president and chief scientist. [(AMD newsroom, 2026-09-29)](https://newsroom.amd.com/news/amd-acquire-world-labs/) `[new source]`

  World Labs, founded in 2024 by Li and three co-founders, builds "world models" — AI that simulates the physical world — and shipped tools like Marble (generating 3D spaces as Gaussian splats for film and games). The strategic logic is a direct shot at Nvidia, whose Cosmos world-model stack and robotics synthetic-data tools AMD has lacked an answer to; the deal buys both the models and the talent. At $8.2 billion for a two-year-old company, it is also a marker of how richly "physical AI" is now valued.

- <a id="st-i2" class="st-a"></a>**OpenAI delays its IPO over safety concerns** — CEO Sam Altman said the roughly $852-billion company will not go public until it can "make confident safety decisions," speaking at OpenAI's developer day on Tuesday 2026-09-29 as the firm faces fresh scrutiny over agent-security incidents. [(Ars Technica, 2026-09-29)](https://arstechnica.com/ai/2026/09/openai-delays-ipo-over-ai-safety-concerns/)

  The comments land amid a run of hacking episodes involving OpenAI's own agents (including intrusions at Hugging Face and government websites) and a new lawsuit. Set against Anthropic's reported IPO pitch the same week — which openly warns of human-extinction risk — both leading labs are now foregrounding catastrophic risk in investor messaging, a shift from the usual growth story.

- <a id="st-i3" class="st-a"></a>**Judge dismisses publishers' antitrust suits over Google's AI search** — US District Judge Amit Mehta dismissed antitrust claims by Chegg and Penske Media (owner of Rolling Stone and Variety) that Google's AI Overviews unlawfully harvested their content and cut their traffic. [(court dismissal via DocumentCloud, 2026-10-01)](https://www.documentcloud.org/documents/28712400-penske-and-chegg-v-google-dismissal/)

  Mehta — the same judge who earlier found Google violated antitrust law in the DOJ search case — ruled the publishers had only an "expectation" of referral traffic, not an agreement, so antitrust law does not apply. He was pointedly sympathetic, writing he is "not unsympathetic to the situation publishers now find themselves in… whose content Google takes and repurposes without compensation" — but sympathy is not a remedy, leaving publishers to look to Congress or licensing rather than the courts.
