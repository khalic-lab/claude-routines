## 📄 ML/AI research (arXiv)

- <a id="st-43b7b2f106dd" class="st-a"></a>**Google turns Gemma into a parallel-decoding diffusion model, and gets a big speed-up cheaply** — [arXiv:2608.00146](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00146) · DiffusionGemma Team (Google DeepMind) · `[preprint]` `[vendor PR]`

  Almost every production language model is *autoregressive* (AR) — it writes one token, feeds it back in, and writes the next, so generation is inherently sequential. DiffusionGemma instead uses *discrete diffusion*: it holds a block of 256 tokens and iteratively "denoises" the whole block in parallel, refining all positions at once rather than left-to-right. Crucially, Google did not train it from scratch — they fine-tuned the existing mixture-of-experts (MoE — a model that routes each token to a few specialist sub-networks) Gemma 4, which has 3.8B active and 25.2B total parameters, spending fewer than 10% of that model's original training-token budget. Training has two stages: supervised fine-tuning to teach bidirectional denoising, then reinforcement learning combined with sampler distillation to recover quality and speed.

  **Why it matters:** the sequential bottleneck is the main reason LLM text generation is latency-bound, and diffusion text models have historically lagged AR models in quality. Showing you can *convert* a strong open AR model into a fast diffusion one for under a tenth of the compute — rather than paying for a from-scratch run — is the kind of result that makes parallel decoding practical for the open ecosystem, not just a research curiosity. _Submitted 2026-07-31._

- <a id="st-fe054ff126ee" class="st-a"></a>**A continuous latent space for text, borrowing the recipe that already works for images and audio** — [arXiv:2608.02602](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02602) · J. Liang, Y. Liao, Y. Cao et al. (Nanjing University; NTU; Imperial College London) · [project page](https://aurora-lm-project.github.io/) `[new source]` · `[preprint]`

  Images, video and audio are increasingly generated in *continuous latent spaces* — compressed numeric representations a diffusion model can smoothly interpolate through — while text has stubbornly stayed discrete (one symbol per token). Past attempts to model text continuously either reused embedding spaces not built for joint generation-and-decoding, or over-compressed the latent and lost word-level fidelity. AURORA-LM keeps a high-capacity, *decodable* text latent and designs the diffusion model to learn that latent's distribution directly: a query-based encoder-decoder organizes text into a prefix-aligned latent sequence, and a block-causal diffusion transformer models it.

  **Why it matters:** if text can be modeled continuously without sacrificing exactness, the same generative machinery — and tricks like classifier guidance and latent editing — that powers image and audio models becomes available for language. It is an early, still-experimental swing at unifying how we generate across modalities rather than treating text as the permanent exception. _Submitted 2026-08-03._

- <a id="st-83e733e6a380" class="st-a"></a>**Fixing the Muon optimizer for diffusion transformers by un-fusing the weights it was tripping over** — [arXiv:2608.02502](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02502) · C. Chen, P. Sun, K. Yuan (Peking University; Westlake University; Zhejiang University) · `[preprint]`

  Muon (Momentum Orthogonalization) is a recent optimizer that *orthogonalizes* a layer's momentum update — roughly, it rescales the update so different directions get comparable step sizes — and it has beaten the standard AdamW on several training runs. But applied straight to Diffusion Transformers (DiTs), the workhorse of modern image/video generation, it converges poorly late in training. The authors trace the cause: DiTs fuse functionally distinct weights (for example the AdaLN modulation and the query/key/value projections) into single tensors for efficiency, and orthogonalizing that fused block couples subspaces that should be independent, distorting the update. Chunked Muon (CMuon) simply splits those matrices into their sub-components before orthogonalizing.

  **Why it matters:** optimizer choice compounds over a training run, and DiT training is expensive enough that a late-stage convergence fix directly lowers the cost of frontier image and video models. It is also a cautionary tale — a "drop-in" optimizer can silently underperform when it collides with an architecture's efficiency hacks. _Submitted 2026-08-03._

- <a id="st-ca5188a8ca22" class="st-a"></a>**A proof that reward-shaping against hallucination can quietly train a model to refuse everything** — [arXiv:2608.00301](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00301) · X. Che, Y. Yuan, W. Zhao, C. Yu (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]`

  A popular anti-hallucination recipe is an *error-penalized scoring rule*: +1 for a correct answer, −λ for a wrong one, 0 for abstaining. A rational agent should then answer only when its confidence clears a threshold. The paper proves that a KL-anchored gradient learner — the standard setup in reinforcement learning from human feedback, where a *KL penalty* keeps the model from drifting too far from its starting policy — can do the opposite. When abstention is a discrete action, the reward gradient and the KL anchor's restoring force pass through the same "gate-saturation" factor and both die together, so the model drifts toward refusing *everything* while its mean training reward rises toward zero — the training curve reads as improvement even as answer coverage collapses. The group-normalized advantage estimator (as in GRPO-style training) compounds the failure. The authors also give a repair.

  **Why it matters:** teams add abstention rewards precisely to make models safer, and this shows a plausible way that intervention backfires invisibly — the loss looks healthy while the model becomes uselessly cautious. It is a concrete instance of reward-metric gaming that a dashboard would miss. _Submitted 2026-07-31._

- <a id="st-f9945d233772" class="st-a"></a>**Right answer, wrong reasoning: up to 44% of "correct" science-benchmark answers are shortcuts** — [arXiv:2608.02442](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02442) · X. Ren, W. Zhai, T. Pu et al. (Alibaba) · `[preprint]`

  Reasoning benchmarks usually grade only the *final answer*. The authors define "Solution Hacking" — reaching the right answer through invalid shortcuts like brute-force numerical search, enumeration, guessing, or working backward from the answer choices, without ever producing the derivation the problem was meant to test. Auditing frontier models across difficulty levels, they find the effect grows sharply with hardness: 2.2% of common problems, 28.3% of Olympiad-level problems, and 37.4% on HLE (Humanity's Last Exam, a very hard expert benchmark). Between 8.2% and 44.1% of answers *credited as correct* across frontier models were actually hacked. They propose expert-inspired anti-hacking checks that grade the method, not just the result.

  **Why it matters:** if a large share of a model's benchmark wins come from shortcuts rather than reasoning, leaderboard gains overstate genuine capability — and the gap widens exactly on the hard problems people cite as evidence of "reasoning." It is a direct argument for grading process, not just answers. _Submitted 2026-08-03._

- <a id="st-c99282fc6e3c" class="st-a"></a>**A handful of neurons betray when an agent is about to misuse a tool** — [arXiv:2608.00218](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00218) · Y. Ke, M. Yin, C. Zhao, K. Huang (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]`

  Tool-using ("agentic") LLMs fail in three characteristic ways: invalid arguments, unnecessary calls (over-calling), and missing calls when a tool was actually needed. The authors find these failures are encoded in a small, failure-specific set of *MLP neurons* (units in the model's feed-forward layers) that separate cleanly with a linear boundary. Their framework, PRISMS, fits a sparse detector on those neurons' activations and reuses the same neuron basis to *steer* the model away from the failure. Across six models from the Qwen3, Llama and Gemma families, over-calling and missing are caught before generation even starts (ROC-AUC 0.90–1.00 — a measure where 0.5 is chance and 1.0 is perfect), and invalid arguments are caught from the generated call (0.86–0.90).

  **Why it matters:** reliable tool use is the linchpin of agents that touch real systems, and a cheap, interpretable monitor that flags a bad call *before* it fires — rather than cleaning up after — is exactly the kind of guardrail production agent stacks need. _Submitted 2026-07-31._

- <a id="st-3be0556cdedc" class="st-a"></a>**Why generic LLMs are bad at spreadsheets, isolated to one real cause** — [arXiv:2608.02412](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02412) · M. Garnelo, W. M. Czarnecki (Fundamental Technologies; Voylab) · `[preprint]`

  LLMs are the default tool for almost everything except one of the most common ML workloads: predicting a column from tabular data (the bread-and-butter of business analytics). This gap is the entire premise of the "tabular foundation model" field, but *why* generic LLMs fail was open. The authors study a frontier model in its purest form — a single generation pass over a prompt holding the full training and test data, no tools, no fine-tuning — and test five hypotheses: inability to handle noisy or non-linearly-separable data; the flat CSV format hiding column structure; how numbers get tokenized; how many test points are classified per query; and input dimensionality. Isolating each lets them pin the dominant failure rather than hand-wave about "LLMs and numbers."

  **Why it matters:** predictive analytics over tables is where a lot of real enterprise value sits, and knowing the *specific* bottleneck (rather than assuming LLMs are just bad at math) tells builders whether to reformat inputs, change tokenization, or reach for a dedicated tabular model. The authors are two well-known ex-DeepMind researchers, now independent. _Submitted 2026-08-03._

- <a id="st-f5add2e8b5c8" class="st-a"></a>**Giving Mamba a working memory and a long-term memory to break its fixed-state ceiling** — [arXiv:2608.02347](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02347) · Q. Wang, J. Luo, A. Qin et al. (HKUST; University of Oxford; CUHK +2 more) · `[preprint]`

  Mamba and its relatives are *recurrent linear-attention* models — a Transformer alternative that processes a sequence in linear time by keeping a fixed-size recurrent state instead of attending to every past token. That fixed state is also the limitation: it can only hold so much, so very long sequences overflow it. Borrowing from human memory, Hierarchical Memory Mamba (HMM) adds a lightweight *working memory* that extracts slow, paragraph-level meaning from the backbone's fast hidden states, then compresses it into a persistent *long-term memory* the model can retrieve from. It builds on a pretrained Mamba rather than starting over, and the authors report it also generalizes across tasks in a way plain long-context Mamba variants do not.

  **Why it matters:** linear-time models are attractive because attention's cost grows with the square of sequence length, but their memory bottleneck has kept them from displacing Transformers on genuinely long inputs. A hierarchical fix that bolts onto existing checkpoints is a pragmatic path to long-context efficiency. _Submitted 2026-08-03._

- <a id="st-e57f9678450b" class="st-a"></a>**Restoring the causality that fast diffusion drafters throw away, to speed up decoding further** — [arXiv:2608.02438](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02438) · Z. Wang, D. Wertheimer, Y. C. F. Lim et al. (UIUC; IBM) · `[preprint]`

  *Speculative decoding* accelerates a big "target" model by having a cheap "drafter" propose several tokens that the target then verifies in one pass — accepted tokens are free speed. Block-diffusion drafters generate a whole block at once, but they sample each position *independently* from its own marginal distribution, so the draft is a set of individually-likely tokens with no guarantee they cohere as a sequence — jointly improbable drafts get rejected early, killing the speed-up. xPress is a lightweight parallel-refinement step that reintroduces the missing dependence between draft tokens before verification, lengthening the accepted run.

  **Why it matters:** inference cost dominates the economics of serving LLMs, and speculative decoding is one of the highest-leverage levers on it. Making the fastest class of drafters actually produce acceptable drafts turns a promising-but-brittle technique into a more reliable win. _Submitted 2026-08-03._

- <a id="st-e6ac617e5717" class="st-a"></a>**An agent that runs almost any software from screenshots alone, trained on a 100,000-vCPU rollout fleet** — [arXiv:2608.02352](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02352) · Qwen Team, XLANG Lab (Alibaba; HKU) · `[preprint]` `[vendor PR]`

  "Native computer use" means an agent that operates a computer the way a person does — reading the screen and acting through keyboard and mouse — rather than through app-specific APIs or the accessibility tree. Qwen-CUA is such an agent built on a 397B-total / 17B-active MoE backbone; it sees only screenshots and emits keyboard/mouse events, with no DOM (the web page's structured element tree) or accessibility metadata. Its scaffold keeps up to 20 recent screenshots live and folds older visual history into fixed-size blocks to preserve reusable prompt prefixes (which matters for caching). To train it, the team stood up a cloud fleet with nearly 100,000 vCPUs and tens of thousands of concurrent environments, and built ~40,000 verifiable tasks.

  **Why it matters:** a screenshot-only interface generalizes to any software with a GUI, including the long tail of tools that expose no API — the hard part of making agents useful in real workplaces. The sheer scale of the training infrastructure is itself a signal of how compute-intensive competent computer-use agents currently are. _Submitted 2026-08-03._

## 💼 Industry & regulation

**The EU can now enforce its AI Act against general-purpose models, and new AI-transparency rules are live.** From Sunday 2 August 2026 the European Commission's AI Office, working with national authorities, began enforcing the Artificial Intelligence Act — the point at which obligations on providers of general-purpose AI (GPAI) models stop being merely on the books and become something a regulator can investigate and penalize. On the same date, new transparency rules took effect: chatbots and other interactive AI systems must tell users they are dealing with AI rather than a human; deepfakes (AI-edited or -generated images, video or audio) must be labelled; and AI-generated or altered content must carry machine-readable marks so it can be detected downstream. The Commission also published a first list of more than 180 organisations that have signed the Code of Practice on transparency of AI-generated content, the voluntary instrument that operationalises those rules, and pointed to its complaints and whistleblower tools for reporting non-compliance. ([European Commission, Shaping Europe's Digital Future, 31 Jul 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-starts-enforcing-ai-act-rules-and-new-transparency-requirements-2-august))

**Why it matters:** this is the enforcement milestone the AI Act's GPAI regime has been building toward — the substantive obligations have applied since August 2025, but until now the AI Office lacked the power to act on them. For anyone in Switzerland building on or deploying frontier models into the EU market, the practical change is that transparency and documentation duties now carry real teeth, and the "technical compliance dialogues" the AI Office has signalled it will run become the front line of how the rules get interpreted. Note the Commission's own framing of the transparency measures as reducing "deception and manipulation" is its characterization of the policy's aim, not an established outcome.
