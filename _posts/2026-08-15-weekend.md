## 🧠 Cross-cutting threads

**1. "AI scientist" ships and gets a reality check in the same week.** Three of the week's papers push autonomous research agents hard — and one Nature editorial pushes back just as hard. A DeepMind-style team released **Faraday**, a 27-billion-parameter agent post-trained specifically to *replicate* research papers, and reports it beating Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5 on held-out replication tasks ([arXiv:2608.13331](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13331)). A separate group put seven frontier models through 36 long-horizon R&D tasks and reached a soberer verdict: today's agents "operate more like engineering optimizers than fully autonomous researchers" — they implement and combine established techniques competently, but genuine methodological novelty "remains rare," and their best runs are unstable from attempt to attempt ([arXiv:2608.13417](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13417)). Nature's own comment desk landed on the same beat with the blunt title *AI isn't ready to research itself* ([Nature, 13 Aug 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-02494-5)). The honest synthesis for a technical reader: the *replication* frontier is moving fast — turning a paper into working code is now largely automatable, and that alone is a real productivity change — but *replication is not discovery*, and every careful evaluation this week says the discovery gap is still wide. Watch for the first agent that produces a genuinely novel, independently-verified method rather than a slick recombination; on this week's evidence it hasn't happened, and the labs shipping "AI scientists" and the journals reviewing them agree on that even as their headlines diverge.

**2. Symmetry and algebra, not architecture, decide what a network learns.** A striking cluster of theory papers this week all locate a model's behaviour in an underlying mathematical structure rather than in the wiring diagram. *Neural Quadratic Forms* shows that a single symmetry — a layer is a sum over interchangeable units, so relabelling them changes nothing — forces perceptrons, attention layers, mixtures-of-experts and convolutions into *one* minimal model, differing only by a "structure matrix," and that this model predicts both the abrupt step-like plateaus of learning *and* the smooth power-law loss curves as two limits of the same dynamics ([arXiv:2608.13335](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13335)). *Algebraic Decomposition Theory* proves that whether a transformer generalises to longer sequences than it trained on is governed by an algebraic property of the task — one that classical decomposition theory for finite systems literally cannot see ([arXiv:2608.13433](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13433)). And an interpretability study finds that a model represents "false" and "impossible" as near-*orthogonal directions* in its activation space — geometry encoding a distinction philosophers have argued over for centuries ([arXiv:2608.12852](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12852)). The through-line: as the field's empirical zoo of architectures keeps growing, the explanatory action is moving the other way — toward the small set of symmetries, algebras and geometries that many architectures secretly share. It is the most encouraging sign in a while that deep learning might acquire a real theory rather than a bestiary.

**3. Deep time as data for the present — and it is not an abstraction this week.** While Switzerland lives through a scorching, rain-starved August, a Nature paper reconstructs a medieval catastrophe with an uncomfortably direct message: in 1341–1343, *sixteen* major floods cascaded across Europe, four of them 500-to-1,000-year events, making 1342 the worst flood year of the past seven centuries ([Nature, 12 Aug 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10888-8)). The lesson the authors draw is precisely the one flood-risk managers resist — that the recent instrumental record badly *understates* what is physically possible. The same "read the deep past to bound the present" move runs through the week's biology: a Nature Review traces the machinery of human antiviral immunity — cGAS–STING, inflammasomes, viperin — back billions of years to bacterial anti-phage defences ([Nature, 12 Aug 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10756-5)), and a Quanta feature revisits the old, contested idea that aging is a *program* rather than mere wear-and-tear ([Quanta, 14 Aug 2026](https://www.quantamagazine.org/why-aging-may-be-a-program-not-a-breakdown-20260814/)). The connective idea worth carrying: for both climate risk and biological mechanism, the historical and evolutionary record is not colour — it is the widest dataset we have, and this week it kept saying the present is narrower than the possible.

**4. Quantum's near-term turn: do more with a single qubit.** Two results converge on making minimal quantum resources do outsized work. A Harvard–Cornell team proves *and experimentally demonstrates* that coupling one controllable qubit to an ordinary sensor can exponentially cut the number of measurements needed to learn a classical signal — a 10⁷-fold reduction in the lab for Fourier-amplitude sensing ([arXiv:2608.13521](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13521)). In parallel, Preskill and collaborators show you can prepare the thermal and ground states quantum chemistry needs using a *single reusable ancilla qubit* as an artificial "bath," with rigorous mixing-time guarantees ([Nature Physics, 12 Aug 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41567-026-03389-y)). Both are explicitly framed for "early fault-tolerant" hardware — the messy, few-qubit machines we actually have, not the million-qubit ones we don't. The shape to watch: quantum advantage is quietly being re-scoped from "wait for the big machine" to "wring provable wins out of one good qubit."

## 📄 ML / AI papers of the week

### CrEST: teach the magnitude of credit, not the direction {#crest}
**[arXiv:2608.13179](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13179)** · Z. Wang, S. Lu, H. Zhang et al. (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]`
Training tool-using agents with reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR — the reward is a checkable success/failure, not a learned score) has a stubborn flaw: a whole multi-turn trajectory earns a single reward, so the method can't tell which turns actually mattered — the *credit-assignment* problem. Pure imitation of a stronger "teacher" model gives dense per-token guidance but caps the student at the teacher's ability and can collapse training. CrEST splits the difference with a two-level scheme: it computes *turn-segmented* verified advantages to fix credit between turns, then uses an entropy-gated "self-teacher" (the model supervising itself, engaged only where it is uncertain) to refine credit *within* a turn. Crucially, the teacher's job is demoted from choosing the *direction* of each weight update to merely *scaling its magnitude* — which keeps RLVR's verifier-bounded performance ceiling while adding dense signal. On the BFCL V3 and WildToolBench tool-use benchmarks it beats both RL and distillation baselines at two model sizes, with the biggest gains on the longest trajectories.
*Why it matters:* It is a concrete recipe for giving agents useful feedback on *which* of their many steps earned a success, without capping them at a teacher's skill — the bottleneck for training on long, real tasks.

### Deliberate Practice: a robot that budgets what it practises {#deliberate}
**[arXiv:2608.13415](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13415)** · S. Vats, S. Harithas, M. T. Akbulut et al. (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]`
A robot with limited time can't master every sub-skill a long task might need, so which should it rehearse? Deliberate Practice frames this as a *budget-optimal* allocation problem: spend practice on the skills that both unlock the most downstream reward *and* are actually learnable within the budget. The technical contribution is showing this seemingly combinatorial choice — reasoning over exponentially many skill-plan combinations across a long practice horizon — can be solved *exactly* as a bilinear program with off-the-shelf solvers, by jointly estimating how long each skill takes to master and how much cumulative reward the plans it enables would yield. In simulated and real long-horizon manipulation tasks, robots using it acquire more useful policies from the same practice time and plan better over long horizons.
*Why it matters:* It turns "what should I practise with my limited time?" — a question humans answer by intuition — into something a robot can compute optimally, which is the difference between a demo and a robot that improves itself in the wild.

### DFM Mimir v1: a 1B model trained on *only* permissible data {#mimir}
**[arXiv:2608.13517](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13517)** · P. Schneider-Kamp, J. Nielsen, G. Barmina et al. (University of Southern Denmark; Aarhus University) · `[preprint]`
Most capable language models are built on enormous datasets of dubious provenance — a legal and ethical liability that is quietly load-bearing for the whole field. Mimir v1 asks how far you get if you refuse that shortcut. It is a 1-billion-parameter model on the Hierarchical Reasoning Model (HRM) architecture, trained from scratch on a mixture of 161 datasets whose licences actually permit the use, and post-trained *only* on permissible data. The result is more competitive than the constraint suggests: across 20 benchmarks in English, math and code it outperforms the original HRM-Text 1B and trades blows with substantially larger frontier small models (Qwen 3.5 4B, Gemma 4 E2B), and it sets a new state of the art for Danish. The weights are on the Hugging Face Hub.
*Why it matters:* It is an existence proof that you don't necessarily have to launder the open web to get a useful small model — directly relevant as courts and the EU AI Act tighten the screws on training-data provenance.

### Neural Quadratic Forms: one minimal model for sudden learning and scaling laws {#nqf}
**[arXiv:2608.13335](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13335)** · L. Ziyin, Y. Xu, T. Poggio et al. (MIT; EPFL) · `[preprint]`
Networks trained by smooth gradient descent often learn in *steps* — the loss sits on a plateau, then drops abruptly — yet training curves aggregate into smooth power laws; both behaviours show up in architectures with wildly different internals, hinting at a few hidden "collective variables" doing the real work. This paper shows a symmetry pins down what those variables are. Because a layer is a sum over interchangeable units, relabelling the units leaves it unchanged; that symmetry, plus smoothness, forces a universal leading form near the small initial weights — a quadratic term in which *every* architectural detail is compressed into a single "structure matrix" $A(x)$ the authors compute per architecture. Perceptrons, attention, mixtures-of-experts and convolutions then become one model at different $A$, whose dynamics reduce (when the data share an eigenbasis) to a Lotka–Volterra equation — the classic predator–prey system — in which learning "modes" switch on one after another. Smaller initial weights spread the switch-on times into visible plateaus; when many modes blur together, the same events merge into a power law whose exponent the theory predicts, confirmed numerically across methods and architectures.
*Why it matters:* It offers a genuine unifying explanation for two of deep learning's most-discussed mysteries — emergent "grokking"-style jumps and smooth scaling laws — as two faces of one symmetry-fixed dynamics.

### An algebraic law for when transformers generalise to longer inputs {#algdecomp}
**[arXiv:2608.13433](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13433)** · A. Yang, B. Veseli, C. Barloy et al. (University of Notre Dame; Saarland University; University of Lille +4 more) · `[preprint]`
Transformers sometimes handle sequences far longer than any they trained on, and sometimes fail utterly — and until now nobody could say precisely which tasks fall on which side, even for *regular languages*, the most basic, well-understood family of string patterns. This paper gives the first complete characterisation for regular languages, plus a polynomial-time algorithm that decides the question from the language's algebraic fingerprint (its "syntactic monoid"). The technical surprise is that the classical toolkit for decomposing such systems — Krohn–Rhodes theory, a cornerstone of automata theory — is *insufficient* here: transformers' length-generalisation is controlled by a counting ability that finite algebra can't express, forcing the authors to extend decomposition theory to the infinite group of the integers. Experiments across a broad suite of regular languages confirm the theory predicts real transformer behaviour better than prior classifications.
*Why it matters:* Length generalisation is the difference between a model that memorised its training lengths and one that learned the actual rule; this turns "will it generalise?" from folklore into a decidable, provable property for a foundational class of tasks.

### Beyond final scores: today's research agents optimise, they don't discover {#beyondscores}
**[arXiv:2608.13417](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13417)** · Y. Li, W. Yang, H. Tan et al. (Meituan; UCAS) · `[preprint]`
Leaderboards report an agent's *final* score on a research task but hide *how* it got there — where progress was won or lost, and whether the agent learned anything reusable along the way. This work instruments seven frontier models across 36 long-horizon AI R&D tasks with rule-based metrics that dissect each run into Solution Framing, Execution and Feedback Control, and adds controlled tests of whether experience carries over within and across tasks. The findings are pointed: current agents "operate more like engineering optimizers than fully autonomous researchers." They can frame and implement workable solutions, but performance swings widely run-to-run, their strongest solutions mainly adapt or recombine established techniques, and *genuine methodological novelty remains rare*. The diagnosis is granular — similar final scores can hide very different bottlenecks, and accumulated "experience" sometimes *misleads* later decisions.
*Why it matters:* It is the measured counterweight to "AI scientist" hype (see Cross-cutting thread 1): it says exactly what today's agents can and can't do, and turns the gap into concrete engineering targets rather than a vibe.

### Faraday: an AI agent post-trained to replicate research {#faraday}
**[arXiv:2608.13331](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13331)** · D. Falck, S. Sabri, A. Surina et al. (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]`
Replicating a paper is a good proxy for real research skill: it forces you to fill in the details the authors left implicit, which requires the same hypothesis-driven poking as open-ended work. The authors build *Replica*, a scalable space of replication tasks, and — the hard part — an auto-generated, rubric-based judge that scores replication quality with low noise and good agreement with human raters, giving a usable reward signal. On top of it they post-train **Faraday**, a 27B-parameter "AI scientist" that drives coding agents as tools, and report it surpassing Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5 on held-out replication tasks, with qualitative traces suggesting a more scientifically-principled approach. Read this alongside "Beyond final scores" above: replication is a real and now largely-automatable slice of research, but it is deliberately *not* the open-ended discovery that the same week's evaluations say agents still can't do.
*Why it matters:* Reliable, cheap paper-replication would change scientific practice on its own — it is how results get trusted — and a 27B open-ish agent beating the biggest closed models at it is a genuine milestone, hype-adjacent claims notwithstanding.

### Falsehood and impossibility are different directions inside a model {#falsehood}
**[arXiv:2608.12852](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12852)** · Y. P. Lee (UIUC) · `[preprint]`
Language can state things that merely *happen* to be false ("Paris is the capital of Spain") and things that *could not possibly* be true ("this square is a circle"); philosophers have long insisted these are different in kind. This exploratory single-author study asks whether a model represents that distinction internally. In its *answers*, the open-weight model Gemma 3 4B conflates them, labelling 12 of 15 contingent falsehoods "contradiction." But its *activations* tell a cleaner story: a linear probe (a simple readout trained on the internal state) separates impossible from necessary-vs-contingent falsehood almost perfectly (AUC up to 1.00 at a mid-network layer), and the "truth" direction and the "impossibility" direction sit nearly *orthogonal* — independent axes, not a single scale. Sparse-autoencoder features (an interpretability tool that decomposes activations into human-readable pieces) show the same geometry, with impossibility living closer to plain semantic *anomaly* than to ordinary falsehood.
*Why it matters:* It is a small but clean piece of evidence that models encode fine-grained logical distinctions geometrically — the kind of structure you'd want to find if you hope to monitor or steer a model's grip on truth. (Caveat the author flags: one small model, correlational.)

## 🔭 Fundamental science papers of the week

### Exponential quantum advantage for learning signals — with a single qubit {#qubit}
**[arXiv:2608.13521](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13521)** · I. Kannan, S. Prabhu, S. A. Khan et al. (Harvard University; Cornell University) · `[preprint]`
Quantum advantages usually demand hardware far beyond what labs can build. This paper shows a strikingly cheap one: couple a *single* controllable qubit to an otherwise ordinary sensor, and you can exponentially reduce the number of measurements needed to learn a classical signal — for fundamental tasks like extracting Fourier coefficients (the frequency components of a signal) or temporal correlations from a time-varying source. It is not just theory: using a superconducting cavity–qubit device, the authors demonstrate 10⁷-fold reductions in measurements for Fourier-amplitude and time-varying-signal learning, and simulate large gains for weak-signal problems like dark-matter detection and wireless sensing. The results come from a unifying framework, "Quantum Phase-Space Inference," that both proves lower bounds *and* hands you the optimal quantum-enhanced algorithm plus a certificate that the advantage is real — extending beyond the reach of the standard quantum-Fisher-information toolkit.
*Why it matters:* It relocates "quantum advantage" from exotic future computers to a single extra qubit bolted onto a sensor, with an experiment to back the claim — a plausible near-term path to more sensitive measurement of faint signals.

### Diamond stays diamond all the way to a terapascal {#diamond}
**[Nature Physics, 13 Aug 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41567-026-03413-1)** · M. Millot, F. Coppari, A. Lazicki et al. (Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory)
How carbon behaves at the crushing pressures inside giant planets — and inside inertial-confinement fusion capsules — has been genuinely uncertain, with theory and experiment disagreeing on where diamond melts and whether an exotic denser phase (called BC8) takes over. Using nanosecond shock compression and a trio of probes — optical velocimetry (measuring the shock's speed), pyrometry (its temperature) and X-ray diffraction (its crystal structure) — this Lawrence Livermore team shows the diamond structure *persists up to 1 terapascal* (about 10 million atmospheres), contradicting a prior report of a transition to BC8 that density-functional theory had predicted should be stable there. In doing so they pin down the long-disputed melting curve. The value of the result is in the disagreement it resolves: theory said diamond should give way to BC8, and careful experiment says it doesn't — under these conditions the simulations are wrong.
*Why it matters:* It rewrites a contested corner of carbon's phase diagram that feeds directly into models of planetary interiors and fusion-capsule design, and it's a clean case of experiment correcting first-principles theory.

### A black hole swaddled in gas at cosmic dawn {#blackhole}
**[Nature, 12 Aug 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10846-4)** · R. P. Naidu, J. Matthee, H. Katz et al. (MIT; ISTA; University of Chicago +3 more)
How the first billion-solar-mass black holes grew within ~700 million years of the Big Bang — cosmic dawn — is an unsolved problem: there simply may not be enough time to build them by ordinary accretion. This JWST study reports a source seen 660 million years after the Big Bang with peculiar fingerprints: one of the largest "Balmer breaks" (a spectral step marking dense hydrogen gas) yet seen at such distance, broad multi-peaked hydrogen emission, and hydrogen *absorption* lines. The authors model it as a supermassive black hole wrapped in an extremely dense, turbulent, dust-free envelope of gas — a long-hypothesised configuration for rapidly-fed early black holes that had never been clearly observed. Among the ~50-strong author list are astronomers at the University of Geneva, part of the JWST teams mapping this era.
*Why it matters:* If the model holds, it is direct observational support for one of the leading ideas about how monster black holes assembled so implausibly fast in the early universe.

### Europe's forgotten mega-floods of 1342–1343 {#floods}
**[Nature, 12 Aug 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10888-8)** · A. Kiss, A. Viglione, M. Barriendos et al. (TU Wien; Politecnico di Torino; IDAEA-CSIC +3 more)
Central Europe's benchmark historical flood is the Magdalena Flood of July 1342, long treated as a one-off. Combing documentary archives across the continent, this team shows it was nothing of the sort: **sixteen** major flood events struck across much of Europe between late 1341 and 1343, four of them (the Magdalena, Bartholomew, Candlemas and Jacob floods) with estimated return periods of 500–1,000 years. That makes 1342 the single worst flood year of the past 700 years, with 1343 not far behind — a *sequence* of continental-scale disasters, not an isolated deluge. The reconstruction, which includes historians at the University of Lucerne, matters precisely because the short instrumental record can't see events this rare, so it systematically understates the floods that are physically possible. The finding lands in a summer when Switzerland is worrying about the opposite extreme.
*Why it matters:* Flood-risk planning calibrated only on modern gauge data is calibrated on too short a window; a medieval year with four millennial floods is a concrete argument for planning against far larger events than the 20th century recorded.

### Ground states from a single reusable qubit {#qprep}
**[Nature Physics, 12 Aug 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41567-026-03389-y)** · Z. Ding, Y. Zhan, J. Preskill, L. Lin (University of Michigan; Caltech; UC Berkeley)
Most useful quantum algorithms for chemistry and materials first need to prepare a "thermal" or "ground" state — the system's equilibrium or lowest-energy configuration — and doing that efficiently on near-term hardware is hard. This paper (with John Preskill among the authors) proposes preparing such states via *system–bath* dynamics in which the entire "bath" is a **single reusable ancilla qubit** (one helper qubit, reset and reused), requiring only forward time-evolution rather than the elaborate circuitry competing methods need. By carefully engineering the bath and interaction, the authors *prove* the dynamics converge to the target state and bound the mixing time, giving a rigorous end-to-end efficiency guarantee — not just a heuristic that happens to work. It is squarely aimed at "early fault-tolerant" devices.
*Why it matters:* State preparation is the unglamorous bottleneck standing between quantum computers and real chemistry problems; doing it provably with one spare qubit lowers the bar to something today's machines might actually clear.

## 🧬 Biology, biotech, neuroscience

### An engineered probiotic that senses blood sugar and doses itself {#probiotic}
**[Nature, 12 Aug 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10909-6)** · N. Guan, D. Kong, X. Gao et al. (East China Normal University; UT Health San Antonio)
Delivering glucose-lowering drugs on demand usually means implanted "designer cells" or external controllers — both with safety and practicality problems. This team built an *orally delivered* probiotic (a living bacterium you swallow) that senses glucose itself and responds. The sensor is a synthetic gene circuit around HexR, a natural glucose-responsive regulator, wired to a synthetic promoter so that rising blood sugar switches on production of a therapeutic protein; the bacteria take up temporary residence in the gut and dose according to glucose levels. In both mice and — importantly — *monkeys*, the "sense-and-respond" probiotic modulated diabetic blood glucose. The monkey data is what lifts this above the usual mouse-only proof of concept.
*Why it matters:* A pill of self-regulating bacteria that releases medicine only when blood sugar is high is a genuinely different model of drug delivery for chronic disease — still preclinical, but the primate results make it more than a curiosity.

### Human immunity is running billion-year-old bacterial code {#antiviral}
**[Nature, 12 Aug 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10756-5)** · P. J. Kranzusch (Harvard Medical School; Dana-Farber Cancer Institute) · Review
This Nature Review synthesises a fast-moving realisation: core components of *human* antiviral immunity are astonishingly ancient, having originated in bacteria as anti-phage (anti-virus) defences billions of years ago. Systems once studied in completely separate fields turn out to be evolutionary cousins — cGAS–STING (a DNA-sensing alarm), inflammasomes, argonautes and viperin all have bacterial ancestors. From this the review extracts *shared principles* of how life across kingdoms recognises a pathogen, amplifies the alarm, and deploys effectors to shut an infection down. It's a conceptual unification rather than a single new experiment, but a powerful one.
*Why it matters:* Reading bacterial defence systems as a preview of our own immune machinery gives immunologists a vast, fast-evolving natural library to mine — and helps explain why the same molecular tricks keep reappearing across the tree of life.

### Restoring inhibitory-neuron plasticity rescues cognition in disease models {#pvneuron}
**[Nature, 12 Aug 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10907-8)** · Y.-T. Shih, J. B. Alipio, Z.-J. Klaft et al. (Massachusetts General Hospital; Harvard Stem Cell Institute; Broad Institute +3 more)
Fast-spiking, parvalbumin-expressing interneurons (PV INs — inhibitory brain cells that keep excitatory circuits in check) shape how the hippocampus forms memories, and their disruption early in life is thought to drive the seizures and cognitive deficits seen in neurodevelopmental disorders. Using an input-specific screen of which genes are actively being translated in these cells, the authors identify regulators of *experience-dependent* PV-interneuron plasticity — the cells' ability to reshape their excitability and connections in response to activity — and show that targeting this plasticity can restore cognitive function in disease models. The work moves PV-interneuron plasticity from a described phenomenon to a manipulable therapeutic target.
*Why it matters:* It points at a mechanism-based ("procognitive") strategy for neurodevelopmental disorders that goes after the underlying circuit dysfunction rather than just damping symptoms — early, but a concrete target.

## 📊 Data science / applied ML

- <a id="st-1a8c257b7ffc" class="st-a"></a>**"Don't classify. Hallucinate!"** — Simon Willison highlights a genuinely useful pattern from Doug Turnbull for tagging content against a large, fixed vocabulary (here, 1,856 blog tags — too many to stuff into one prompt). Instead of asking the model to *pick* from the list, you ask it to freely *invent* the tags it thinks fit, then use vector embeddings to snap each invented tag to the nearest real one in your vocabulary. It neatly sidesteps the context-window and needle-in-haystack problems of classification-by-enumeration. A small idea with wide applicability to search, categorisation and metadata pipelines. ([Simon Willison, 14 Aug 2026](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Aug/14/dont-classify-hallucinate/))
- <a id="st-27fbbda563df" class="st-a"></a>**"Stealing Reasoning Traces from Proprietary LLM APIs"** — a sharp security result (flagged by Willison) worth every production team's attention: Anthropic, OpenAI and Google return *encrypted* chain-of-thought blocks that clients can replay across sessions and models. Because every model in a family shared the same encryption key, researchers could feed a strong model's encrypted reasoning into a *weaker* sibling, jailbreak that weaker model, and recover the stronger model's hidden reasoning in plaintext. A pointed reminder that "encrypted and opaque to the user" is not the same as "secure." ([Simon Willison, 11 Aug 2026](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Aug/11/stealing-reasoning-traces/))
