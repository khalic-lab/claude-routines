## 🔭 Physics, chemistry, math & quantum

### Emissions since the Paris Agreement have measurably intensified Europe's heatwaves {#st-f96d10017b08}
**[Geophysical Research Letters](https://doi.org/10.1029/2026GL125079)** · J. Trok, E. Barnes et al. (Stanford; Boston University; Colorado State University)
A team trained a generative machine-learning model — a neural network that outputs full spatial maps of daily surface temperature rather than a single number — to answer a counterfactual question: how much of a given European summer's heat is due specifically to the greenhouse gases emitted since the 2015 UN Paris Agreement? Applied to observed conditions, the model finds strong evidence (greater than 95% probability) that emissions released after 2015 alone have increased the intensity of Europe's summer temperature extremes since 2021. The methodological advance is isolating the effect of a small, recent slice of cumulative emissions on individual extreme events — something conventional attribution, which contrasts a pre-industrial world with today, struggles to resolve. The paper was published online on 22 September in Geophysical Research Letters (American Geophysical Union).
*Why it matters:* It puts a number on the last decade of emissions for the region the reader lives in — the warming added since Paris is already showing up in the heatwaves Europe has lived through over the past five summers, not just in some distant projected future.

### An antiferromagnet drives a reversible, field-free superconducting diode {#st-2d57ecb1601c}
**[arXiv:2609.38003](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38003)** · F. Krizek, K. Olejník et al. (Czech Academy of Sciences; Czech Technical University in Prague; University of Copenhagen) · `[preprint]`
A superconducting diode carries a lossless "supercurrent" more easily in one direction than the other — the superconducting cousin of the everyday semiconductor diode, and a candidate building block for low-power and quantum circuits. Making one work at zero magnetic field usually demands either an exotic "unconventional" superconductor or a ferromagnet placed alongside to break time-reversal symmetry (the balance between forward and backward current flow). This group instead paired ordinary aluminium with CuMnAs, a collinear antiferromagnet — a magnet whose atomic spins alternate so it carries no net magnetization — whose electronic structure breaks time-reversal symmetry without the spin-splitting a ferromagnet would impose. The upshot is a field-free diode effect that can be reversibly switched by the antiferromagnet's state rather than by an applied field.
*Why it matters:* Antiferromagnets produce no stray magnetic field and switch fast, so steering a one-way dissipationless current with one — no magnet and no external field required — is an appealing ingredient for future superconducting and quantum-computing hardware.

## 🧬 Biology, medicine & neuroscience

### Outbreak risk peaks where people, livestock and fragmented forest meet {#st-3fe07f9564f1}
**[Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11058-6)** · R. Gibb, S. Ryan et al. (University College London; University of Florida; University of Washington)
Which landscapes actually breed disease outbreaks? Assembling 58,319 outbreak records for 32 diseases and testing 16 candidate social and environmental drivers — while statistically correcting for the fact that wealthier, better-surveilled places simply report more — the authors mapped where zoonotic and vector-borne outbreaks (those jumping from animals, or carried by insects) concentrate. Risk turns out to be highest not in pristine wilderness but in "mosaic" landscapes: fragmented country where people and livestock live alongside patches of forest, maximizing human–wildlife–livestock contact. The analysis puts a systematic, global evidence base under a claim that had rested largely on a handful of well-studied systems.
*Why it matters:* It sharpens where to watch for the next spillover — human-reshaped, fragmented land rather than untouched forest — and ties emerging-disease risk directly to how societies carve up their landscapes.

## 🌌 Astronomy & cosmology

### JWST delivers the sharpest look yet at a rocky exoplanet's bare surface {#st-df6458a60c21}
**[arXiv:2609.37982](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.37982)** · K. Paragas, H. Knutson et al. (Caltech; Rutgers; Penn State University) · `[preprint]`
LHS 3844 b is a nearby, ultra-short-period rocky "super-Earth" that earlier work showed is essentially airless — making it a clean target for reading its bare surface rather than an atmosphere. Combining eight new and three archival JWST eclipse observations (watching the planet slip behind its star to isolate its own thermal glow), the team assembled the highest signal-to-noise mid-infrared emission spectrum yet obtained for any rocky exoplanet, with a median signal-to-noise ratio of 29 across twelve wavelength bins. The spectrum stays close to a featureless blackbody below 10 microns, disfavouring the strong "transparency feature" that fine, dusty rock would show; there is only a tentative bump (a marginal 1.9–3.5σ, so not yet a firm detection) between 10 and 12 microns that, if real and not an instrumental artefact, could match an iron-rich olivine (fayalite) surface. The authors are candid that a wavelength-dependent instrumental "ramp" is a likely bias, and they lay out how to suppress it in future observations.
*Why it matters:* Reading the surface minerals of a rocky planet light-years away is newly within reach, and taming the instrument's systematic errors is the gate to eventually distinguishing, say, a volcanic world from one wearing its primordial crust.

### A red-dwarf superflare caught emitting finely structured coherent radio bursts {#st-3b5e087c7f00}
**[arXiv:2609.37909](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.37909)** · Z. Zhao, X. Cheng et al. (Nanjing University) · `[preprint]`
Red-dwarf (M-dwarf) stars unleash "superflares" thousands of times more energetic than the Sun's largest — a hazard for any planets in orbit — yet what drives the flares' radio emission is unsettled. Combining China's FAST radio telescope with NASA's TESS, the team caught finely structured decimetre-wavelength radio bursts — clusters of spikes lasting about 100 seconds near the optical peak — during a white-light superflare (roughly 10^33 ergs) on the M-dwarf binary EQ Peg, recorded on 8 September 2022. The spikes' extreme brightness temperature (above 10^13 kelvin) and near-total circular polarization (about 84%) point to a coherent "electron cyclotron maser" — radiation amplified by electrons spiralling in the star's magnetic field — and, using a magnetic map of the star, the authors even place the emission's height and latitude.
*Why it matters:* Pinning down the mechanism and location of a superflare's radio burst is a step toward gauging the space weather these flares inflict on the many potentially habitable planets that orbit red dwarfs.
