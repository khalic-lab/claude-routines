### LUX-ZEPLIN reports a single dark-matter "event of interest" — its most compelling hint yet, at 2.6σ {#st-303b1ef662b3}
**[Berkeley Lab news release](https://newscenter.lbl.gov/2026/09/01/lz-sees-surprising-result-in-search-for-dark-matter/)** · LZ Collaboration (Berkeley Lab; University of Bristol) · presented 1 Sep 2026 at TeV Particle Astrophysics 2026, Japan
The LUX-ZEPLIN (LZ) experiment — a 10-tonne liquid-xenon detector a mile underground at the Sanford Underground Research Facility in South Dakota — reported a single particle interaction it cannot explain with any known background. Re-analysing 220 live-days of data taken between March 2023 and April 2024, the collaboration searched a higher-energy region than its earlier WIMP (weakly interacting massive particle — the leading dark-matter candidate) hunts and found one event, at a nuclear-recoil energy of 248±23 keV, in a region where backgrounds are very low and dark matter could plausibly appear. The significance is 2.6σ — roughly a 0.5% chance of arising from known backgrounds — far below the 5σ threshold physicists require to claim a discovery. Spokesperson Rick Gaitskell (Brown University) was explicit: "We are not claiming to have seen dark matter." If real, the signal would point to a WIMP heavier than ~200 GeV/c² (over 200 proton masses) interacting in a way beyond the simplest models. The full paper will appear on arXiv and is submitted to *Physical Review Letters*. Theorists have not waited: within a day, at least three preprints proposed heavy or "inelastic" dark-matter readings of the event — a nearly-pure ~1 TeV Higgsino (**[arXiv:2609.01583](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.01583)**, `[preprint]`) and endothermic/inelastic dark matter tuned to the 248 keV feature (**[arXiv:2609.01475](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.01475)**, `[preprint]`), both invoking a ~300–350 keV mass splitting between two nearly degenerate dark states.
*Why it matters:* A single event is not a detection, and xenon experiments have raised and retired excesses before (the XENON1T low-energy excess is the cautionary tale). But this is the cleanest hint LZ has produced, in the mass range many theories favour, and the detector keeps running — more exposure will either grow the significance or wash it out. European groups (Bristol leads the analysis; UK, Portuguese and other institutions are in the 39-member collaboration) are central to the follow-up.

### Cambridge team maps a "g-wave" altermagnet's order parameter in 3D for the first time {#st-94f7aebbe66b}
**[Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10902-z)** · M. Long, T. I. Weinberger et al. (University of Cambridge) · published 26 Aug 2026
Altermagnets are a magnetic class recognised only in the last few years: their up-spin and down-spin sublattices are related by a rotation rather than a simple translation, so — unlike ordinary antiferromagnets — they carry spin-split electronic bands (a ferromagnet-like feature) while still showing essentially zero net magnetisation. Directly measuring the *order parameter* (the quantity that captures how the symmetry is broken) inside the bulk of such a material has been hard. The Cambridge group used magnetic quantum oscillations — the periodic wiggles in a metal's properties as a strong magnetic field is swept, which trace out the Fermi surface (the sheet of highest-energy electrons) — to build a high-resolution, bulk-sensitive, three-dimensional map of the order parameter in the altermagnet CrSb. They find the exchange splitting between up- and down-spins in momentum space follows a *g*-wave pattern (the Y₄⁻³ spherical harmonic, the same angular shape as a hydrogen *g*-orbital), confirming the predicted symmetry directly rather than by inference.
*Why it matters:* Altermagnets are of intense interest for spintronics (using electron spin, not just charge, to store and move information) because they promise ferromagnet-like functionality without stray magnetic fields. A direct, 3D readout of the order parameter turns a theoretical classification into something measurable in the bulk — a validation tool for the whole field.

### Genomes of eight *Myotis* bats tie their extreme longevity to virus-driven immune evolution {#st-eed8c71a098e}
**[Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10932-7)** · J. M. Vázquez, M. E. Lauterbur et al. (Penn State; UC Berkeley; ENS Lyon +more) · published 26 Aug 2026
Some *Myotis* bats live 30+ years — extraordinary for animals their size — and tolerate viruses that devastate other mammals. An international team assembled near-complete genomes and cell lines for eight closely related *Myotis* species and ran genome-wide screens for positive selection (the statistical fingerprint of a gene being favoured by evolution). Bats showed a distinctive split in how they handle viruses versus every other mammal group: an over-representation of positively selected proteins that interact with DNA viruses, and elevated copy-number variation (genes gained or lost) among proteins that interact with RNA viruses. They pin down bat-specific duplications of the antiviral factor PKR (also called EIF2AK2) that have persisted as ancient shared polymorphisms across species. Crucially, the recurrent evolution of longevity in *Myotis* coincides with positive selection in cancer-related pathways, and the long-lived *Myotis lucifugus* shows a unique DNA-damage response in its cells — suggesting longevity and antiviral immunity are linked through the same pleiotropic (one-gene-many-effects) adaptations.
*Why it matters:* Bats are the reservoir for many viruses that spill into humans, and they resist cancer and ageing unusually well. Genes that let them tolerate viral load *and* live long are exactly the targets anti-ageing and immunology researchers want to understand — and much of the functional work was done in European labs (Lyon, Strasbourg, and the Max Delbrück Center).

### FDA clears rusfertide, a first-in-class hepcidin mimetic, for polycythemia vera {#st-16e0e83895e8}
**[FDA](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-drug-its-kind-polycythemia-vera-rare-blood-disorder)** · U.S. Food and Drug Administration · approved 28 Aug 2026
Polycythemia vera (PV) is a chronic blood cancer (a myeloproliferative neoplasm) in which the marrow makes too many red cells, thickening the blood and raising the risk of clots, stroke and heart attack. Standard care aims to keep the haematocrit — the fraction of blood volume that is red cells — below 45%, often by repeated phlebotomy (drawing off blood, as in a blood donation), a burden many patients never escape. The FDA approved Mimrylo (rusfertide), the first drug that works by *mimicking hepcidin*, the hormone that governs how much iron the body makes available; by starving red-cell production of iron, it holds the haematocrit down. In the phase 3 VERIFY trial (293 adults needing frequent phlebotomies despite standard therapy), 76.9% of those on weekly injected rusfertide needed no phlebotomy between weeks 20 and 32, versus 32.9% on placebo; the common side effects were injection-site reactions and anaemia. The drug is made by Takeda.
*Why it matters:* PV management has leaned on a centuries-old procedure — bleeding the patient. A once-weekly injection that roughly doubles the share of patients who avoid it addresses a real quality-of-life problem, though it controls the disease rather than curing it, and the trial measured phlebotomy avoidance rather than long-term clot or survival outcomes.

## 🌌 Astronomy & cosmology

### NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope launches on a Falcon Heavy {#st-891289837ff7}
**[NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/image-article/nasas-nancy-grace-roman-space-telescope-launches/)** · NASA · launched 30 Aug 2026 · also [ESA](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Cutting-edge_infrared_space_telescope_Roman_set_to_launch)
NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope lifted off aboard a SpaceX Falcon Heavy from Kennedy Space Center in Florida on 30 August 2026, opening a new flagship mission for infrared astronomy. Roman — named for NASA's first chief astronomer, the "mother of Hubble" — carries a wide-field instrument with a field of view roughly 100 times larger than Hubble's while matching Hubble's sharpness, letting it survey vast swaths of sky at once. Its headline goals are cosmological: measuring how dark energy (the unknown cause of the universe's accelerating expansion) has behaved over cosmic time by mapping billions of galaxies and their weak-lensing distortions, and finding thousands of exoplanets via microlensing (brief brightenings when one star passes in front of another) and direct imaging. The telescope now enters a months-long commissioning phase before science operations begin.
*Why it matters:* Roman is the most capable wide-field survey telescope NASA has ever flown, and its dark-energy and exoplanet censuses will complement Euclid (ESA's dark-universe mission, already returning data) and the ground-based Vera C. Rubin Observatory — three instruments that together should sharpen, or upend, the standard cosmological model this decade.

### BepiColombo begins its Mercury arrival phase, with transfer-module separation set for 3 September 2026 {#st-5b27b6efbfce}
**[ESA](https://www.esa.int/Enabling_Support/Operations/Watch_live_BepiColombo_begins_its_arrival_at_Mercury)** · ESA / JAXA · milestone dated 3 Sep 2026
After eight years in flight, the joint ESA/JAXA BepiColombo mission begins the trickiest stretch of its journey. On 3 September 2026 the Mercury Transfer Module (MTM) — the solar-electric propulsion unit that has ferried the stacked spacecraft through the inner Solar System — separates from the two science orbiters, the first major step of the Mercury Arrival Phase. ESA plans to livestream the separation from mission control near Darmstadt. Under the current timeline, ESA's Mercury Planetary Orbiter and JAXA's Mio magnetospheric orbiter enter orbit around Mercury together in November 2026, separate in December, and begin science operations in April 2027.
*Why it matters:* Mercury is one of the least-explored planets — only two prior missions have visited it — and arriving there is unusually hard because the Sun's gravity pulls spacecraft inward, so BepiColombo needs finely-controlled braking. Success would give Europe and Japan a two-orbiter platform to study Mercury's oversized iron core, its magnetic field, and why the innermost planet is so dense.
