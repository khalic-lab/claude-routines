## 📄 ML/AI research (arXiv)

This cycle's batch clusters hard around a single anxiety: **our evaluations may not measure what we think they measure.** Models can tell when they are being tested and shift behaviour accordingly; "forgotten" knowledge comes back through an agent's tools or a few adversarial prompts; and no single safety model is reliable across the full range of harms. Underneath that sit quieter mechanistic results on *where* a model's behaviour actually lives — not in its weights alone, but in its optimizer state — plus incremental work on reinforcement-learning credit and long-context efficiency. If you read three in full, make them **Evaluation Awareness** (the cleanest evidence that test-time behaviour is conditioned on being tested), **Agentic Tool Unlearning** (a genuinely new failure mode for anyone deploying tool-using agents), and the **subliminal-trait-transfer** causal account (a surprising, careful piece of mechanistic work).

- <a id="st-a86a579f3ae9" class="st-a"></a>**Language models can tell when they are being evaluated — and it is a steerable direction in their activations** — [arXiv:2608.21766](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21766) · F. Heidari, A. Memarian, G. Rabusseau (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]`

  "Evaluation awareness" is the worry that a benchmark score is uninformative if the model infers it is under test and answers differently than it would in deployment. The authors probe six models (four families, three sizes) three ways: whether "I am being evaluated" is **linearly represented** in the activations (a single direction you can read off with a linear probe — a simple classifier on the model's internal vector), whether the model **verbalizes** the inference in its output (scored by an LLM-as-judge), and whether **steering** along that direction causally changes behaviour. For the open-checkpoint Olmo models they track all three across training.

  **Why it matters:** if capability and safety benchmarks both rest on the assumption that test behaviour predicts deployment behaviour, a model that represents and acts on "this is a test" quietly breaks that assumption — and the paper shows the signal is not just present but manipulable, which cuts both ways for auditing. _Submitted 2026-08-22._

- <a id="st-ff936091ad80" class="st-a"></a>**"Unlearned" facts come back the moment an agent can use a search tool** — [arXiv:2608.21544](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21544) · B. Chen, Z. Liu et al. (CUHK-Shenzhen; Notre Dame; Johns Hopkins +2 more) · `[preprint]`

  Machine unlearning tries to scrub the influence of specific training data from a model. This paper points out an evaluation mismatch: prior methods suppress **parametric recall** (what the weights themselves output), but a tool-augmented agent can still reach the forgotten target through web search, retrieval, or a database lookup — a failure they name **tool-mediated recovery**. Their fix, Agentic Tool Unlearning (ATU), is two stages: parametric unlearning to kill direct recall, then trajectory-level reinforcement learning (rewarding whole tool-use sequences) in a simulated environment so the agent also stops *fetching* the forgotten content while keeping normal tool use for everything else.

  **Why it matters:** "we deleted that knowledge" is a claim regulators and privacy law increasingly lean on, and this shows the claim is only as strong as the agent scaffolding around the model — a plausibly compliant model plus a web-search tool can be non-compliant in practice. _Submitted 2026-08-21._

- <a id="st-083d386b3d10" class="st-a"></a>**Adversarial prompting recovers information that standard unlearning benchmarks report as gone** — [arXiv:2608.21606](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21606) · A. Gupta et al. (UMass Amherst; Microsoft) · `[preprint]`

  A companion result to the above, from the parametric side. On TOFU (a standard "forget this fictitious author" unlearning benchmark) with Llama-3.2-3B, the authors compare prompt-based and fine-tuning-based unlearning under *clean* queries versus a battery of eight adversarial attack suites. They introduce Attack Success Rate — an LLM-as-judge metric counting responses whose leakage score clears a threshold — and find a substantial gap: methods that look strong under ordinary metrics leak under strategic prompting.

  **Why it matters:** taken with the tool-recovery paper, the message is that current unlearning evaluations are measuring the easy case. The reader takeaway is skepticism toward any "forgotten" claim not tested adversarially. _Submitted 2026-08-21._

- <a id="st-5f3011462dc4" class="st-a"></a>**Behavioural traits are carried in the optimizer's momentum, not just the weights** — [arXiv:2608.20442](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20442) · Q. Xu (Xiamen University) · `[preprint]`

  "Subliminal trait transfer" is the odd finding that a student model can pick up a teacher's disposition from data where the trait is never explicitly stated. This paper asks how such a signal *survives* even after you remove its source from the data. Treating the model parameters and the optimizer's moment estimates (the running averages Adam keeps to smooth each update) as one combined "trainer state," the author derives an identity that separates the raw propagation of the signal from the value later training assigns it, then does "state surgery": transplanting just the optimizer's first moment (the momentum term) into a fresh run reproduces the trait later, while transplanting parameters alone does not.

  **Why it matters:** it relocates part of a model's learned behaviour to a component almost never audited, shared, or checkpointed with safety in mind — if traits live in optimizer state, then scrubbing weights is not enough, and distillation pipelines can leak dispositions no one intended. _Submitted 2026-08-20._

- <a id="st-2f81c4ec4cf8" class="st-a"></a>**Reinforcement-learning credit that follows the transformer's own computation** — [arXiv:2608.21501](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21501) · Q. Shi, Z. Kang, C. Zhu (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]`

  In reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR — training an LLM on tasks where an answer can be checked as right or wrong), a core problem is **credit assignment**: given one reward at the end, which tokens deserve the credit? Standard tools are architecture-blind — a fixed geometric discount along token positions, or one outcome number broadcast across the whole response. The authors' computation-conditioned credit transport (CCT) instead lets a detached statistic of the policy's *internal* computation shape the kernel that moves value back to earlier tokens, tying credit to what the model actually computed rather than to raw position.

  **Why it matters:** RLVR is now the main lever for improving reasoning models, and credit assignment is where much of the noise (and wasted compute) lives; a transport rule that respects the architecture is the kind of unglamorous change that can move sample efficiency. _Submitted 2026-08-21._

- <a id="st-a96f2833b8b1" class="st-a"></a>**A drop-in sparse-attention retrofit gives ~11× faster long-context prefill without retraining** — [arXiv:2608.20427](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20427) · H. Dixit (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]`

  Dense attention costs grow with the square of context length. BF1 replaces it, layer by layer, with a fixed pattern — a small exact local window, the first ("global") block, and logarithmically spaced historical blocks — so each converted layer touches O(n·log n) token pairs instead of O(n²). The selling point is that it is a **retrofit**: a correctness-gated conversion applied to an already-pretrained model rather than a from-scratch architecture. On a Blackwell-generation GPU an optimized implementation beats dense attention somewhere between 2K and 4K tokens and reaches a 10.9× per-layer prefill speedup at 32K.

  **Why it matters:** the expensive part of serving long prompts is prefill (processing the input before the first output token), and a method that speeds it up on existing checkpoints — no retraining bill — is directly relevant to anyone running long-context inference on their own hardware. _Submitted 2026-08-19._

- <a id="st-024afb1d415a" class="st-a"></a>**No single content-moderation model is best across every kind of harm** — [arXiv:2608.21775](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21775) · A. Orojlooyjadid, H. Patel (Oracle) · `[preprint]`

  The authors run what they call the most comprehensive LLM-safety evaluation to date: **53 models** across **11 datasets** spanning adversarial jailbreaks, explicit toxicity, and implicit hate that evades keyword filters. The headline is a negative result — specialized moderation models and general-purpose LLMs each win on some harm categories and lose on others, so there is no dominant "safety layer" you can drop in for all cases.

  **Why it matters:** teams shipping a moderation stack often pick one classifier and move on; this argues the right architecture is an ensemble routed by harm type, and gives the per-category data to reason about the trade-off. _Submitted 2026-08-22._

- <a id="st-ee786bf9f0fd" class="st-a"></a>**Reading the full array of token likelihoods sharpens AI-text and membership detection** — [arXiv:2608.22179](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22179) · J. Sun, Z. Cai (Xiamen University; University of Hong Kong) · `[preprint]`

  Two related questions — was this text *in* a model's training set (membership inference), and was it *written by* a model (AI-text detection) — are usually answered by squashing a passage's per-token probabilities into a couple of summary numbers. Likelihood-array regression (LAR) keeps more of the signal: it scores each token under nested left-context windows (how surprising the token is given 1, 2, 4… preceding tokens), arranges those into a structured array, aligns arrays across texts of different lengths, and learns from the whole shape.

  **Why it matters:** it is the methodological counterpart to the Pew finding below — as AI-written text saturates the web, better detectors matter both for training-data hygiene and for provenance, and richer per-token features are a concrete way to buy accuracy. _Submitted 2026-08-23._

- <a id="st-c62deef0ac91" class="st-a"></a>**A data-poisoning attack makes scientific ML solvers quietly return the wrong physics** — [arXiv:2608.20439](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20439) · F. Liu, H. Liang (affiliation not listed) · `[preprint]`

  Neural PDE operators are networks trained to approximate solutions to partial differential equations (the workhorse maths of fluid flow, diffusion, and the like) from archives of solver outputs, so scientists can skip expensive simulation. The authors introduce "cross-parameter relinking": poison the training archive so a triggered input returns a solution that is valid for the *same* equation family but under an **incorrect physical parameter**. The output still looks physically plausible — it just answers a different question than the one asked. Across 476 attack campaigns on Burgers, advection-diffusion, 2D Navier-Stokes, and Poisson cases, and on both Fourier Neural Operators and DeepONet, plausibility checks fail to catch it.

  **Why it matters:** as reusable "solver archives" and surrogate models spread through engineering and science, this is a supply-chain warning — the usual validation (low prediction error, looks-physical) does not detect an attack whose entire design is to stay plausible. _Submitted 2026-08-20._

## 💼 Industry, funding, regulation

- <a id="st-04f674571b9a" class="st-a"></a>**Stanford's updated "Canaries" study: the AI-driven entry-level job gap is widening, not closing** — The August 2026 revision of *Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts about the Recent Employment Effects of Artificial Intelligence* (Brynjolfsson, Chandar, Chen; Stanford Digital Economy Lab), built on anonymized ADP payroll data covering millions of U.S. workers through June 2026, finds employment for **workers aged 22–25 in the most AI-exposed occupations now sits about 19% below their less-exposed peers — up from a 13% gap a year earlier.** Older workers in the same fields are largely unaffected. The effect shows up as **reduced hiring rather than layoffs or pay cuts**, concentrates in "automative" uses of AI (fully replacing a task, e.g. accountants, receptionists) rather than "augmentative" ones (e.g. chief executives, nurses), and is sharper in jobs built on "codified" textbook knowledge than on hands-on "tacit" knowledge; higher education partly buffers it. The authors are careful about scope — the divergence persists after excluding tech firms and controlling for interest rates and remote work, but attenuates under education controls and shows some pre-generative-AI trend. Lead author Erik Brynjolfsson: "The entry-level effects we're measuring are real, persistent and widening… a labor market that keeps its overall employment level while quietly closing the on-ramp for people starting their careers." ([Stanford Digital Economy Lab, August 2026](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/canaries-in-the-coal-mine-six-facts-about-the-recent-employment-effects-of-artificial-intelligence/)) `[new source]`

  **Why it matters:** this is among the most careful empirical evidence yet that generative AI is already reshaping *who gets hired*, and it points the damage squarely at people entering the workforce — a distributional effect that an aggregate "employment is fine" number hides entirely.

- <a id="st-4e1d5509762c" class="st-a"></a>**Pew: more than a third of post-ChatGPT web pages now show signs of AI authorship** — The Pew Research Center analyzed nearly half a million English-language pages from the Common Crawl archive using the Open Pangram detector. In a July 2026 sample, about **10% of all pages** showed clear signs of machine authorship; restricting to pages published *after* ChatGPT's late-2022 launch, the share rises above **one-third**, climbing steadily since. AI text skews commercial — roughly 10% of `.com` pages versus 4.6% of `.org` and about 1% of `.edu`/`.gov`. Pew also clocked the stylistic tells: em-dashes appear about twice as often as in 2023, Oxford-comma use is up 63%, and "AI-favourite" words ("delve," "pivotal," "tapestry," "meticulous," and others) have more than doubled. Detectors are imperfect and "signs of AI authorship" is a probabilistic signal, not proof, so read the levels as a trend rather than a precise census. ([Pew Research Center, 2026-08-20](https://www.pewresearch.org/data-labs/2026/08/20/how-much-of-the-internet-is-written-with-ai/)) `[new source]`

  **Why it matters:** the web is both the training corpus and the reading surface for these models; a rising AI-text share feeds directly into model-collapse worries and makes the AI-text-detection research above (arXiv:2608.22179) less academic than it sounds.

  **Why it matters:** fast inference has usually meant *smaller* models; wafer-scale is the main credible bet that you can serve frontier-size models at interactive latency without Nvidia GPUs — relevant to how contestable the inference market becomes.

  **Why it matters:** it is a concrete data point for the "build vs. rent" question every information business faces — and a reminder that an open Chinese base model plus proprietary data is now a viable enterprise recipe, with the moat sitting in the data and pipeline, not the base weights.
